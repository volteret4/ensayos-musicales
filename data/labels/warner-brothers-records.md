**Financial Struggles (Mid-2000s)** : In mid-2005, Warner Brothers faced significant financial difficulties, including new management, cost-cutting measures, layoffs of over 1,000 employees and 30% of its artist roster in 2004, and a negative credit rating due to debt. The label's attempt to raise $750 million through a stock offering was met with a hesitant market, achieving only $16 per share instead of the targeted $24. **Label's Misjudgment of Linkin Park's Sound** : Despite Linkin Park's eventual success with over 100 million records sold, Warner Brothers Records was initially unsure how to market the band, with some, including the label president, reportedly disliking their sound and debating whether to release their debut album at all due to "screaming and rapping." They made various suggestions, such as cutting the rap, Mike Shinoda rapping like Fred Durst, bringing in a reggae vocalist, or adding gimmicks to Joe Hahn. **Major Label Adoption of "New Wave"** : Warner Brothers Records was one of the major labels that quickly adopted Seymour Stein's "New Wave" marketing term. This rapid embrace, alongside Polygram Records and other major labels by 1980, significantly contributed to establishing "New Wave" as a widely used, catch-all term for the diverse new music emerging in the post-punk era. **Prince's Contract (1977)** : Prince signed with Warner Brothers Records in 1977 at age 19, securing an unusual degree of creative control and explicitly being placed on the label's pop roster instead of R&B, a distinction that would define his career. **Artist-Friendly Reputation** : When R.E.M. chose to sign with Warner Brothers Records in 1988 for their major label debut, it was due to the label's reputation among mega-majors as an "artist's first label," respected for its handling of adventurous acts such as Prince and Paul Simon. This reputation also likely influenced The B-52s' earlier signing with Warner. **Minimal Promotion for B-52s (1986)** : After the B-52s declined to tour for their *Bouncing Off the Satellites* album in 1986, following Ricky Wilson's death, Warner Brothers provided minimal promotion for the record. This contributed to the album's poor chart performance, as it was off the charts in just 15 weeks.
