**Mellon Collie and the Infinite Sadness (1995)** : This album is considered a key piece for understanding 90s music in the United States. For its production, the band decided to replace their previous producer, Butch Vig, with two European producers, Alan Moulder and Flood (Mark Ellis), which was a significant risk and a major change for the album. The European producers revolutionized the recording process, making the band start from scratch due to fundamental differences in production techniques between Europe and the United States in the 90s. Mark Ellis advised the band to abandon their previous process, focusing instead on a more track-divided recording approach that prioritized well-recorded individual tracks. He also suggested dedicating a couple of hours daily to improvisation, creating new material without sheet music, which directly led to the creation of significant instrumental pieces like the album's title track. Alan Moulder, from day one, challenged Billy Corgan to define a guiding theme or narrative for the album, an idea that deeply resonated with Corgan but was complex given that most of the album was already composed. This pursuit of a conceptual thread led them to change some songs and discard over half of the existing material. The chosen theme for the album became "the sadness and personal frustration a person suffers throughout their life." The final guitar arrangements, especially the use of pedals and echo effects, aligned much more with 90s European rock production styles than with those prevalent in the US, a direction Billy Corgan greatly appreciated. **Mellon Collie and the Infinite Sadness (1995)** : Released in 1995, this double album was conceived as a concept record despite the mid-90s stigma against such ambitious projects, which were considered egotistical and "dumb" by the Alternative Nation. Billy Corgan insisted on creating what he called "The Wall for Generation X," referencing Pink Floyd's iconic album. With producers Alan Molder and Flood, the band created a thematically unified album (though without a linear narrative) exploring the cycle of life and death. Corgan intended it as a message and an exploration of his own experiences, advice, and observations for Generation X, specifically those between entering high school and graduating college. The CD version was divided into "Dawn to Dusk" and "Twilight to Starlight," while the vinyl offered more granular sections: "Dawn," "Tea Time," "Dusk," "Twilight," "Midnight," and "Starlight."
