## curiosities
**Avoidance of Political Humor** : Steve Martin consciously chose not to incorporate political humor into his routines. He believed that politics was "depressing" and saw no point in including it in his comedy. "He also took pride in the fact that he did not interject any political humor into his work at all. He said that he thought that politics was depressing and that there was no point in including it in his comedy." ← For the Record - The 70s > Ep. 41 - The Impact of The Troubadour on 1970s Popular Culture | https://seventies.libsyn.com/ep-41-the-impact-of-the-troubadour-on-1970s-popular-culture
**Banjo Playing** : Steve Martin is a "world-class banjo player" who purposefully brought his banjo on stage. His intention was to make the audience believe he was working hard at something, even though he felt his on-stage actions often seemed random. "He is an excellent. He's a world class banjo player. And he said the reason that he took his banjo up on stage, he said because it looks like I am doing things on stage that are not very difficult even though it's actually very hard." ← For the Record - The 70s > Ep. 41 - The Impact of The Troubadour on 1970s Popular Culture | https://seventies.libsyn.com/ep-41-the-impact-of-the-troubadour-on-1970s-popular-culture
**Comedy Style and "Showbiz Phony" Persona** : Richard Zoglin, in "Comedy at the Edge," described Martin's comedy as making fun of "the old showbiz" by transforming himself into a "showbiz phony" or an "annoying showbiz jerky guy." Martin decided that denying the audience the 1950s-style one-liner was key to modern comedy. He would intentionally create distractions, such as bashing the microphone or stumbling, which appeared self-deprecating but was actually making fun of showbiz. "Richard Zoglin in his book Comedy at the Edge wrote that Martin's brand of comedy was a take on how Lenny Bruce made fun of quote the old showbiz by turning himself into the showbiz phony." ← For the Record - The 70s > Ep. 41 - The Impact of The Troubadour on 1970s Popular Culture | https://seventies.libsyn.com/ep-41-the-impact-of-the-troubadour-on-1970s-popular-culture
**Early Writing Career** : Before achieving fame as a stand-up comedian, Steve Martin worked as a writer for The Smothers Brothers. He later joined the writing staff of "The Glenn Campbell Good Time Comedy Hour" from 1969 to 1972 but quit because he found the show's comedy "dumb." "Steve Martin had been working in comedy before he started opening at the Trubidor. He worked as a writer for two other comedians, the Smothers Brothers." ← For the Record - The 70s > Ep. 41 - The Impact of The Troubadour on 1970s Popular Culture | https://seventies.libsyn.com/ep-41-the-impact-of-the-troubadour-on-1970s-popular-culture
**Halloween Night Live Set at The Troubadour (1976)** : Steve Martin returned to The Troubadour on Halloween night, 1976, for a live set. His performance included characteristic self-deprecating humor and meta-commentary on professional show business, such as joking about waiting for drugs to take effect and the cost of entry. **Success as a Comedian** : Steve Martin achieved immense success, selling out arenas, selling millions of albums, and having thousands of fans repeat his catchphrases, akin to rock bands with their song lyrics. His brand of comedy was seen as a response to traditional showbiz. "Now, Steve Martin could sell out arenas. He could sell millions of albums. He had thousands of fans repeating his famous excuse me, catchphrase right along with him, just like rock bands had fans repeating the lyrics of their songs." ← For the Record - The 70s > Ep. 41 - The Impact of The Troubadour on 1970s Popular Culture | https://seventies.libsyn.com/ep-41-the-impact-of-the-troubadour-on-1970s-popular-culture
