## curiosities
**Coining the Term "Rock and Roll"** : Freed famously started referring to the music he played on his radio show as "Rock and Roll." This change in terminology occurred after a New York street musician, who claimed prior usage of the name "Moondog," threatened legal action. One theory suggests Freed derived the term from the 1951 recording "60 Minute Man" by Billy Ward and the Dominoes, which included the lyric "I rock them, roll them all night long," known as African American slang for sexual intercourse, a meaning Freed was likely aware of. "Fried also famously eventually started calling the music he played on his radio show, Rock and Roll." ← https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jD1dWxNLvfE ← alan-freed
**Early Career and Rendezvous Records Partnership (Late 1940s - 1951)** : Alan Freed, a radio announcer at WAKR in Akron, Ohio, developed a friendship with Leo Mintz, who operated Rendezvous Records, a prominent record store in Cleveland. Mintz observed that a significant number of white teenagers were purchasing R&B records sourced from the US South and suggested Freed feature these on his radio show. When Freed moved to Cleveland for a position at WJW, Mintz bought airtime on the station for Freed's show, which premiered on July 11, 1951, playing records supplied by Rendezvous. "Fried hosted, playing R&B records supplied by rendezvous records." ← https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jD1dWxNLvfE ← alan-freed
**The Moondog and Pioneering Rock Concerts** : Alan Freed achieved considerable fame, adopting the moniker "the Moondog" because his radio program aired after midnight. He subsequently began promoting live shows for the artists featured on these R&B records, naming these events "Moondog Balls." These concerts are historically recognized as the first-ever rock concerts. "And looking back, these were the first ever rock concerts." ← https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jD1dWxNLvfE ← alan-freed
**Forced Renaming and Coining "Rock and Roll"** : After losing a lawsuit to the original Moondog (Louis Hardin Jr.), Allan Freed was forced to stop using the "Moon Dog" moniker. Needing to revamp his image and find a new catchphrase, he decided to use "rock and roll," an African-American slang phrase for sex. This term was understood by the youth but not by parents, thus becoming the widely adopted descriptor for the new type of music emerging in the 1950s. "This was a problem because Fried had to revamp his image. He needed a new catchphrase, and that's when he decided to use an African-American slang phrase for sex." ← https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6SdPPsmpgJg ← allan-freed
**Pioneering Black R&B for White Audiences** : In the 1950s, Allan Freed was a disc jockey in Cleveland who played Black R&B records for white teenagers, a radical practice for the time. He referred to himself as "the Moon Dog" on air and hosted immensely successful "Moon Dog Balls" concerts. "Back in the 1950s, Alan Fried was a disc jockey and cleveland, playing black R&B records for white kids. Really radical thing to do at the time." ← https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6SdPPsmpgJg ← allan-freed
**Boston Concert Incident and Downfall** : During the 1958 Chuck Berry and Jerry Lee Lewis tour, Alan Fried's career began its precipitous decline at a Boston concert. After police forced the audience to sit down for dancing, Fried defiantly told the audience, "it looks like the Boston police don't want you to have a good time." This led to police falsely blaming the show for numerous crimes and contributed to Fried's eventual firing. **Career Downturn and Industry Changes** : Fried's career was already in decline as rock and roll evolved, shifting from predominantly black musicians to white artists and being increasingly dominated by major labels producing "pre-packaged teen idols." Fried lacked the connections with major labels and the understanding of this manufactured pop, unlike his rapport with R&B records from smaller labels like Chess. **Forced Resignation from Radio Station** : Following the Boston incident and existing difficulties working with him, Fried's radio station, now profitable, used a "morals clause" in his contract to fire him. He was permitted to frame it as a resignation, but it marked a significant loss of prominence. "Now that this had happened, they took the opportunity to use the Mubbles' clause in Fried's contract to fire him, although he was allowed to present it as a resignation instead of a firing." ← Episode 67： ＂Johnny B. **Payola Scandal Scapegoat** : Within a few years of losing his prominent radio job, Alan Fried became the industry's designated fall guy for the widespread practice of payola. His involvement included receiving writing credits for songs like "Mabeline" and "Sincerely" as a form of payment. "He would, within a couple of years, become the designated industry fall guy for the practice of Paola." ← Episode 67： ＂Johnny B. Goode＂, by Chuck Berry | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sHlA-qihuNw
**Post-Scandal Career and Death** : After the payola scandal broke, Fried lost his job at WABC by the end of 1959 and spent his remaining years moving between progressively smaller radio stations, never regaining his previous prominence.
