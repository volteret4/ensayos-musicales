# genre - Country Pop

## curiosities
**"Purity of Country Music" Debate** : The argument that country pop artists were "ruining the purity of country music" was a debate that had been ongoing for decades by the time "Urban Cowboy" was released, with roots in the 1960s when radio stations began actively courting pop music fans. **1970s AM Radio Airplay** : In the 1970s, country-pop combinations were highly likely to receive AM radio airplay, contributing to the crossover success of artists like Charlie Pride. While this approach pleased a broader audience, it often "pissed off the purists" of traditional country music. "That's a good example of where country music was headed in the 70s with that country, Paul, it's in this country pop combo that was likely to get a AM radio airplay and kind of pissed off the purists, but it also made Charlie Pride a star." ← For the Record - The 70s > Ep. 53 - More than Charley Pride - African Americans in 70s Country | https://www.ftr70.com/
**1970s Crossover Hits** : The 1970s were characterized by numerous country pop and country rock crossover hits, indicating that country music was already moving towards a broader appeal long before "Urban Cowboy" was released. "A decade littered with examples of country pop or country rock crossover hits." ← For the Record - The 70s > Ep. 28 - Soundtracks of the 70s | https://seventies.libsyn.com/028-soundtracks-of-the-70s-12221-5-45-pm
**1970s Blurring of Borders** : By the 1970s, the boundary between polished Nashville country records and straight-up pop music began to blur, exemplified by songs like Lynn Anderson's "Rose Garden" which didn't sound fully wedded to either format. Easy listening singer-songwriters like John Denver further muddied these waters by commanding both charts. "By the 70s, the border between polished Nashville country records and straight up pop was starting to blur." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Friends in Low Places, Part 1 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Disco Backlash Function** : In the era of the disco backlash, country pop served a function for radio programmers by providing palatable middle-of-the-road hits. Many of these singles, such as Kenny Rogers' "I Don't Need You" or Ronnie Milsap's "No Getting Over It," effectively functioned as straight-up adult contemporary pop. "Basically, country pop was riding away and serving a function for radio programmers. These were palatable middle of the road hits in the era of the disco backlash." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Friends in Low Places, Part 1 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Decline by 1983** : The country pop wave began to diminish around 1983, with smash duets like Eddie Rabbit and Crystal Gayle's "You and I" and Kenny Rogers and Dolly Parton's "Islands in the Stream" bringing the "Urban Cowboy" era to a close. By 1984, Top 40 radio had shifted to new wave, funkier, and more synthetic sounds, reducing the need for country stars. "But the country pop wave started to peter out by 1983." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Friends in Low Places, Part 1 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Chart Dominance in Contemporary Summer Music** : Country pop has emerged as a dominant force in contemporary summer music, exemplified by collaborations such as Post Malone and Morgan Wallen's "I Had Some Help." This song spent six weeks atop the Hot 100 and led the Songs of the Summer Chart, demonstrating the genre's strong cross-over appeal and chart success in the summer season.
