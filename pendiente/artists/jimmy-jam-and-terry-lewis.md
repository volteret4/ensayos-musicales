## songs
**Monkey (1988)** : Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, known for producing Janet Jackson's *Control* album, completely rearranged George Michael's "Monkey" for its single release. They "pumped up the intro and turned Monkey into a hot summer hit," which led to it topping the Hot 100. The original album version was described as a more ethereal skeletal pop-funk track. ## curiosities
**Origins in Minneapolis Funk (1970s)** : James Harris III (professionally known as Jimmy Jam) and Terry Lewis began their careers as a keyboardist and bassist, respectively, for a 70s Minneapolis funk band called Flight Time. This early experience in the Minneapolis music scene laid the groundwork for their future production and songwriting success. "James Harris III, known professionally as Jimmy Jam, and his partner, Terry Lewis, started as a keyboardist and bassist respectively for a 70s Minneapolis funk band called Flight Time." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > State of the World Edition | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Dismissal from The Time (1983)** : Prince fired Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis from The Time in 1983 after they missed a gig with the band. Prince considered their outside production work for another act, the SOS Band, to be disloyal. "Prince fired them in 1983 after they missed a gig with the band. **Breakthrough as Producers with SOS Band (1983)** : Following their dismissal from The Time, Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis achieved a breakthrough as producers with the SOS Band's song "Just Be Good to Me." This track became a sizable number two R&B hit in late summer 1983 and was the first example of their distinctive funk-driven electro-R&B style, establishing them as mid-80s R&B hitmakers. "Just be good to me was a number two R&B hit in the late summer of 1983 and a breakthrough for Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis as producers." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > State of the World Edition | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Mid-80s R&B Hitmakers** : After their breakthrough, Jam and Lewis adapted their distinctive funk-driven electro-R&B sound for a range of artists, becoming prominent mid-80s R&B hitmakers. Their production credits during this period included successful work for Cheryl Lynn, Alexander O'Neal, Thelma Houston, and Cherrelle (known for "I Didn't Mean to Turn You On"). **Creative Approach to Janet Jackson's Career Reboot** : Around 1985, Jam and Lewis expressed interest in working with Janet Jackson, believing her attitude was missing from her early records despite recognizing the quality of her voice. They aimed to involve her directly in the creative process to excite her about making music and being an artist, which proved critical to her career reboot with the "Control" album. "By involving her in the creative process, we thought we could get her excited about making a record and excited about being an artist." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > State of the World Edition | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**"Industrial Phase" Production (1989)** : By 1989, Jimmy and Terry Lewis entered what could be called their "industrial phase," characterized by an obsession with factory-like sounds of grinding gears and worrying motors. These unique sonic effects were incorporated into the production of Janet Jackson's "Rhythm Nation 1814" album, underlying its songs and adding richness and detail. "By 1989, Jimmy and Terry were entering what you might call their industrial phase, obsessed with the factory-like sounds of grinding gears and worrying motors." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > State of the World Edition | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Development of the "Wet Sound" Production** : Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis had been perfecting their "wet sound" production style for over half a decade, a keyboard-driven vibe that translates the feeling of pouring rain into a pop song. They scored multiple hits refining this sound, including "Tender Love" by The Force M.D.'s (1986), "Human" by The Human League (1986), and "Can You Stand The Rain" by New Edition (1988), with Janet Jackson's "Come Back to Me" (1990) representing its most polished version.
