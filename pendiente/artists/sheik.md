## songs
**Dance, Dance, Dance (Yowsah, Yowsah, Yowsah) (1977)** : Co-written by Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards, this complex R&B track combined jazzy chords with seemingly frivolous lyrics and a parenthetical subtitle, "Yowsah, Yowsah, Yowsah," a jazz phrase from the Roaring Twenties. Its infectious quality led to Sheik being signed by Atlantic Records in 1977 after initially being passed over, especially after a smaller label, Buddha Records, released an early pressing that created strong DJ and dance floor buzz. Reissued by Atlantic in summer 1977, it first topped Billboard's Dance Chart by December, then broke into the pop top 40, and reached number six on the Hot 100 in February 1978. "It was the infectious dance, dance, dance, Yausa, Yausa, Yausa that finally got Sheep signed to a label in 1977." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > These Are the Good Times, Part 1 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Everybody Dance (1977)** : Nile Rogers wrote "Everybody Dance" as a showcase for Sheik's instrumental prowess, featuring Bernard Edwards' unique bass part, Tony Thompson's relentless four-on-the-floor beat, and Nile's signature "chicken scratch" guitar. Luther Vandross arranged the vocals, which included himself, Diva Gray, Robin Clark (on background), and Norma Jean Wright (on lead). Despite seemingly simple, decadent lyrics, the song had intricate vocal arrangements and instrumental chops. Before being signed to a label, a lacquer test pressing generated immense buzz at New York's The Night Owl club. It was eventually released as a single, reaching number 38 on the pop chart by June 1978 and number one on the Disco Chart. **Good Times (1979)** : Sheik's most immortal hit, which topped the Hot 100 in August 1979. Nile Rogers conceived it as a variation on Kool & The Gang's "Hollywood Swinging." It features Rogers' distinctive guitar line and Bernard Edwards' iconic percolating walking bass line, which Edwards reportedly created on the spot. The lyrics, including "Happy Days are here again," were written by Rogers and Edwards to be wistful and ironic, reflecting the 1979 economic downturn by fusing 1920s nostalgia (inspired by Al Jolson) with 1970s disco, incorporating elements of George Clinton and Cole Porter, and featuring synchronized vocals from Alpha Anderson, Lucy Martin, and Fonsi Thornton. Its distinctive chiming bell sounds were noted for forecasting the sound of new wave dance music that would emerge in the subsequent decade, drawing comparisons to Donna Summer's pioneering "I Feel Love." **Le Freak (1978)** : The best-selling single in Atlantic Records history, this song originated from Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards being denied entry to the exclusive New York nightclub Studio 54. Initially conceived as "Awww, F*** Off! F*** Studio 54!", Edwards suggested changing the expletive to "freak," and Nile then proposed "freak out" in reference to a bad LSD trip. Sung by Alfa Anderson (who took over primary vocal duties after Norma Jean went solo) and Diva Gray, the "Frankophile duo" (Rogers and Edwards) created a track that was unusually wordy for Sheik, with verses that sounded like advertising copy and a chant-like chorus. Released in early fall 1978, it took only seven weeks to reach number one on the Hot 100, where it achieved a rare "hat trick" by returning to the top spot three times, accumulating six weeks at number one. Its chart run outperformed the combined weeks of Barbara Streisand & Neil Diamond's "You Don't Bring Me Flowers" and The Bee Gees' "Too Much Heaven." By the end of its run, it had sold six million copies in the US, making it unofficially the first "triple platinum" single. **Sophisticated Image and Future-Sounding Music** : In an era characterized by bell-bottom jeans and polyester leisurewear, Sheik cultivated a stylish, "natty," and elegant image. Nile Rogers, Bernard Edwards, and drummer Tony Thompson wore sharp suits, while their singers, led by Alfa Anderson, donned reinvented jazz-age outfits. **"Faceless Band" Concept** : Nile Rogers conceived of Sheik as a "faceless backup band professionally," drawing inspiration from two rock acts: Roxy Music's album covers (which featured glamorous models instead of band members like Brian Ferry, Amanda Lear, or Marilyn Cole) for sophistication, and KISS's full makeup for theatrical anonymity. Ironically, this concept became a hard lesson in "facelessness" when Rogers and Edwards were denied entry to Studio 54 because they weren't recognized. "Nile wanted to invert what KISS had done, writing in his memoir quote, what if we played the faceless backup band professionally?" ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > These Are the Good Times, Part 1 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Adaptation and Underestimation** : The enduring success of Sheik's sound and its extended "universe of hits" well into the 2010s is a testament to the band's remarkable adaptability and refutes the notion of disco's short-lived phenomenon. Their work, alongside that of Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards with other acts, also challenges the idea that rock killed disco, instead showing how disco elements were absorbed by rock, despite their underestimation by some.
