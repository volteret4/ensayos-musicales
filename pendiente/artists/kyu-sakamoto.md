# artist - Kyu Sakamoto

## songs
**Ui-Oh Muite Aruku (Sukiyaki) (1963)** : This enchanting Japanese pop song, originally titled "Ui-Oh Muite Aruku," became a global phenomenon after a British label executive heard it in Japan in 1962. Issued in the U.S. by Capitol Records under the nonsensical title "Sukiyaki" (a Japanese hotpot dish), it amazingly topped the Hot 100 for three weeks in June 1963, despite being sung entirely in Japanese. ## curiosities
**British Discovery and Mis-titling** : In 1962, the president of a British record label discovered Sakamoto's song "Ui-Oh Muite Aruku" in Japan and brought a 45 back to England. The label later issued an instrumental cover by Kenny Ball and his Jazzmen under the nonsensical title "Tsukiyaki" to make it more memorable for British listeners. "The story goes that the president of a British record label, while traveling on business through Japan in 1962, heard an enchanting pop song by a young Japanese singer named Kyu Sakamoto." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Singing Nuns and Green Tambourines Edition Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**American Radio DJ Catalyst** : In America, Kenny Ball's cover failed to become a hit, but a Washington radio DJ in 1963 played Sakamoto's original Japanese recording, leading to listeners "swooning for it" and Capitol Records acquiring the U.S. rights to release the original single under the "goofy title" "Tsukiyaki." "In America, Kenny Ball's cover was not a hit, but in 1963, a Washington radio DJ played the original Japanese recording and found his listeners swooning for it." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Singing Nuns and Green Tambourines Edition Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Accidental Cross-Cultural Pioneer** : Sakamoto became an "accidental cross-cultural pioneer" due to the improbable journey of "Sukiyaki" to the West, decades before the rise of J-Pop and K-Pop, with its melody proving shockingly enduring through various covers and interpolations.
