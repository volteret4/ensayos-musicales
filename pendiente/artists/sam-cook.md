## songs
**A Change Is Gonna Come (1964)** : Recorded in January 1964 and released on the B-side of the "Ain't That Good News" album, this song was monumentally different from anything Sam Cook had done before, being both more personal and political. Written by Cook himself in December 1963, inspired by his reflections on "Blowin' in the Wind" and the March on Washington, it vividly brought to mind a gospel melody but didn't come from any particular spiritual. Orchestrated by Renee Hall to sound like Nat King Cole classics, it was one of the first self-reflective civil rights songs and came early in the lineage of Black Pride and Black Power Anthems. Though only performed once live (on The Tonight Show with Johnny Carson), and not intended as a single, it posthumously became a modest pop hit (No. 31 Hot 100) and a Top 10 R&B hit three months after his death in March 1965, casting a long shadow over subsequent Black Popular Music. "Of a change is going to come, Peter Giralnik writes, quote, it was a song both more personal and more political, a song that vividly brought to mind, a gospel melody, but that didn't come from any spiritual number in particular." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Don’t Know Much About History, Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Ain't That Good News (1964)** : The title track of Cook's final studio album, this song is a down-home, southern-flavored R&B track based on a venerable 19th-century black spiritual. It was on the charts in February 1964, reaching number 13 on Billboard's Hot 100 on its way to an eventual number 11 peak, coinciding with Cassius Clay's victory over Sonny Liston. **Bring It On Home to Me (1962)** : A deeply felt soul ballad and slow dance classic, this song reached number two on the R&B charts and number thirteen on the Pop charts. Like "Chain Gang," it was a polished pop/R&B song with more profound depth than initially apparent. Cook re-contextualized a line from Odetta's "Oh Freedom" in this love song, illustrating his awareness of social justice movements and folk music a full year before Joan Baez sang "Oh Freedom" at the March on Washington. "As with Changer, two years earlier, Bring It On Home to Me was a polished pop slash R&B song that was more profound than it first appeared." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Don’t Know Much About History, Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Chain Gang (1960)** : Released three months after "Wonderful World," this single reached No. 2 on both the R&B and Pop charts. Cook wrote this "ingenious, deceptively deep pop song" after witnessing an actual chain gang, building its beat from the clank of shackles and prisoners' chants. It brought a social conscience to his music, addressing prison labor and mass incarceration of African Americans, well ahead of the civil rights movement's peak. ## curiosities
**Early Career Transition** : Sam Cook began his career as a revered gospel legend before successfully pivoting to secular music, a move that laid the groundwork for his extensive influence across soul, pop, and R&B. "I talked about how Soul Legend Sam Cook began his career as gospel legend Sam Cook. **One Night in Miami Factual Inaccuracies** : The 2020 film "One Night in Miami" reimagines aspects of Cook's career, compressing multiple years of accomplishments into early 1964. It inaccurately portrays Cook's music through 1964 as shallow and apolitical, ignoring deep, socially conscious hits like "Chain Gang" and "Bring It On Home to Me" which predate the film's timeline, and misrepresents the timing and inspiration behind "A Change Is Gonna Come." "I began a fact check of the film, which compresses multiple years' worth of his accomplishments into early 1964." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Don’t Know Much About History, Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Pioneering Entrepreneurship with SAR Records** : Even before leaving Keen Records, Sam Cook began establishing his own label, SAR Records, in 1959 with J.W. Alexander and Silas Roy Crane. Cook envisioned SAR as a haven for black performers, making former bandmates like The Soul Stirrers "new again" by writing and producing for them, and saw this work as a form of advocacy well before Motown had significant hits. "Cook envisioned the S.R. label as a haven for black performers." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Don’t Know Much About History, Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Artistic Versatility** : By 1962, Sam Cook was the second best-selling act on RCA Records after Elvis Presley, consistently flaunting his versatility. He could score with upbeat party records like "Twist in the Night Away" and "Having a Party," while simultaneously recording profoundly felt soul ballads such as "Bring It On Home to Me." "Cook was flaunting his versatility." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Don’t Know Much About History, Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Refusal of Segregated Shows** : Sam Cook actively lived out his principles by refusing to play separate shows for white and black audiences, even when touring in the Deep South, demonstrating his commitment to social justice beyond his music. "And he was living out his principles by refusing to play separate shows for white and black audiences, even in the deep south." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Don’t Know Much About History, Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Tragic Personal Losses** : Cook endured immense personal tragedies, including a 1958 car crash that killed his driver Ed Cunningham and hospitalized himself, Lou Rawls, and Cliff White. In 1959, his first wife Dolores was killed in another car crash (though divorced, Sam paid for her funeral). The greatest tragedy struck in June 1963 when his 18-month-old son Vincent drowned in the family swimming pool, leaving Cook inconsolable and incommunicative, with his sadness channeled into his music. "But 1963 brought the greatest tragedy of all. In June, at Sam's home in Los Angeles, his 18-month-old son Vincent wandered away from his mother and their housekeeper, fell into their swimming pool and drowned." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Don’t Know Much About History, Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Influence of Civil Rights and Folk Music** : Cook was deeply aware of social justice movements and folk music well before 1964, taking inspiration from figures like Odetta, the "Queen of American Folk Music." He incorporated lines from spirituals like Odetta's arrangement of "Oh Freedom" into his songs, demonstrating an early social conscience that fueled his later work, including "A Change Is Gonna Come." "This illusion offers ample evidence that Cook was aware of both social justice movements and the focusing well before 1964." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Don’t Know Much About History, Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Inspiration from "Blowin' in the Wind"** : Sam Cook was deeply moved and "carried away with the message" of Bob Dylan's "Blowin' in the Wind," to the point of being "almost ashamed not to have written something like that himself," particularly given it was written by a white artist. This profound impact spurred Cook to write his own social anthem, "A Change Is Gonna Come." "The fact is Sam Cook had been thinking he needed to write his own blowing in the wind as far back as the march on Washington." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Don’t Know Much About History, Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
**Posthumous Legacy** : Sam Cook's death on December 11, 1964, at the Hacienda Motel in Los Angeles, remains shrouded in mystery, with many questioning the official self-defense narrative. Like Tupac Shakur and Biggie Smalls, his passing cemented his legend, leading to hundreds of thousands of fans lining the streets for his funeral and the posthumous release of his masterpiece "A Change Is Gonna Come," which profoundly influenced subsequent Black Popular Music and became a lasting anthem. "In the popular imagination, Cook's death sits alongside the 1990s murders of Tupac Shakur and Biggie Smalls among the most disputed deaths of a black musical luminary." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Don’t Know Much About History, Part 2 | https://my.slate.com/podcasts/feeds/hit-parade/
