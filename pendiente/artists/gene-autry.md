Autry strategically sang about the military during the war to protect his masculine "singing cowboy" persona. ## curiosities
**Cowboy Movie Hero Archetype** : Gene Autry was mentioned as an example of the cowboy movie heroes from Larry Weiss's youth, who were singers rather than actual ranchers, influencing the concept behind "Rhinestone Cowboy." "He was writing about the cowboy movie heroes of his youth, the Jean Autry types, who were singers, not ranchers." ← For the Record - The 70s > Ep. 2 - Countryish Music of the 1970s (Encore) | https://www.ftr70.com/
**Singing Cowboy Film Star** : Gene Autry was a massive star during the 1930s and 40s, a period considered the heyday of singing cowboy films. His popularity contributed to the "Hollywoodization" of Western music, incorporating elements like crooning and orchestras. "But the 30s and 40s were the heyday of singing cowboy films with people like Gene Ortrie and Roy Rogers becoming massive, massive stars." ← Episode 3： ＂Ida Red＂ by Bob Wills and the Texas Playboys | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zu4E9Y1oJA0
**Western Music Star and Influence** : Gene Autry was the preeminent Western music star during the late 1930s and early 1940s, and his influence extended to a wide range of musicians, including figures like Les Paul and Bo Diddley. His rendition of "Blueberry Hill" potentially inspired Fats Domino. "Ortrý...was the biggest Western music star at the late 30s and early 40s and influenced everyone from Les Paul to Bowie Didley." ← Episode 45： ＂Blueberry Hill＂, by Fats Domino | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VqIwZGJHgBg
**Singing Cowboy Persona** : Gene Autry, the self-styled "singing cowboy," helped bring country and western to the masses. His masculine archetype persona, prominent on radio and screen, made him initially hesitant to record a song about an "underestimated magical caribou with an electric schnauz" like "Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer." "His singing cowboy persona was a masculine archetype, both on the radio and on the silver screen.
