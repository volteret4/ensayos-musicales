# concert - PBS Pledge Drive

## curiosities
**Origin and Purpose** : PBS pledge drives began in the early 1970s as a crucial fundraising mechanism for public television stations, which were prohibited from airing traditional commercials to generate revenue. These drives sought donations directly "from viewers like you" to support programming. "They started in the early 70s. **Evolution of Format** : Initially, PBS pledge drives were rudimentary, involving a static slide displayed after a program (like "Masterpiece Theater") with an announcer making a pitch. Over time, they evolved into the familiar format of periodically interrupting programming for direct appeals during a week or two, demonstrating the urgency and importance of viewer support. "In the old days, we'd just put a slide up after Masterpiece Theater and then sit there for five or ten minutes while the announcer would be making a pitch over the slide." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Decoder Ring - The New Age Hit Machine | https://shows.acast.com/hit-parade/episodes/695d4ee88e6dd12efb02c1d2
**Ideal Content for Fundraising** : Unlike many regular PBS shows with long story arcs that are difficult to interrupt (e.g., "Frontline" or "Nova"), successful "Pledge Shows" had shorter "little story arcs" that built for 15 or 20 minutes before a break. This structure made concert films particularly effective for pledge drives, as they could easily be segmented for fundraising appeals. "But Pledge Shows, successful Pledge Shows, have little story arcs so that they build for 15 or 20 minutes and then you go to a break." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Decoder Ring - The New Age Hit Machine | https://shows.acast.com/hit-parade/episodes/695d4ee88e6dd12efb02c1d2
**Success of Music Specials** : Music-focused pledge shows consistently proved to be highly effective fundraising tools, capable of generating significant viewer engagement and donations. These specials allowed stations to "come out on a high," with viewers often reacting with enthusiasm, such as getting up and dancing in their homes during "big band specials." "When you build a music pledge show, you come out on a high. **Replicable Model for Stardom** : The massive success of The Three Tenors and Yani demonstrated that PBS pledge drives could serve as a powerful "discovery channel" and "catapult" to launch artists' careers into the mainstream. This led to an "era of the unlikely blockbuster PBS Pledge special," as other artists and even major labels sought to replicate this model. **Major Label Involvement** : Following the success of independent artists like Yani and John Tesh, major record labels recognized the potential of PBS pledge drives and began investing money into producing specials. This led to the breakthrough of new artists such as Josh Groban and Charlotte Church, and even the creation of groups like Celtic Woman specifically for a PBS debut. **High Frequency of Airings** : PBS stations would air popular pledge specials not just once, but repeatedly, sometimes "six, seven, eight times a week" for periods of "a year and a half, two years." This constant repetition, driven by the need to generate significant funds, ensured maximum exposure for the artists featured. "PBS stations get the rights to air a special not just once, but in some cases six, seven, eight times a week." ← Hit Parade Music History and Music Trivia > Decoder Ring - The New Age Hit Machine | https://shows.acast.com/hit-parade/episodes/695d4ee88e6dd12efb02c1d2

Regular, heavy core viewers were often "very upset by this constant" repetition, while the specials succeeded in bringing in "a lot of new audiences" who appreciated the music, creating a "trade-off" for the stations.
