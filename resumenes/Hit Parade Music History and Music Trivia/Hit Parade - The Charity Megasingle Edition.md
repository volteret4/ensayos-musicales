## songs
**Do They Know It's Christmas? (1984)** : This charity single was conceived by Bob Geldof and Midge Ure in the fall of 1984, motivated by a BBC TV report on the Ethiopian famine. They decided to write an original Christmas song to avoid royalty payments for pre-existing carols. The song adapted an earlier Boomtown Rats piece by Geldof called "It's My World," with a Christmas-themed melody by Ure. Recorded rapidly in late November 1984 at Sarm West Studios in Notting Hill, West London, it featured a procession of leading British pop stars, with specific solo showcases for many. Its structure innovatively packed the first half with solos before a mass choir closing refrain. The single was issued in early December, barely a week after recording, along with a music video and a making-of video. It sold over three million copies in the UK, topping the charts for five weeks, and remains one of the two best-selling singles in UK chart history. In America, it sold 2.5 million copies by 1985 and peaked on the Hot 100 in mid-January 1985, though its airplay was seasonally limited. "The record came together rapidly, as Geldoff and Er prepared for a recording date in late November."

**Recording Session - Sarm West Studios** : The single "Do They Know It's Christmas?" was recorded on a chilly November day in West London at Sarm West Studios in Notting Hill. Cameras were present both outside and inside the studio, highlighting the importance of the visual medium for the project. "On that chilly November day in West London, when Geldolph's army of pop stars all arrived at the Sarm West studios in Notting Hill, they were greeted by cameras both outside the building and inside the studio itself."

## songs
**We Are the World (1985)** : This anthem was co-written by Michael Jackson and Lionel Richie, who were both at the peak of their careers, following the inspiration of Band-Aid's charity single. Recorded on January 28, 1985, at A&M Studios in Hollywood, hours after the American Music Awards, it gathered approximately 50 recording artists under the production of Quincy Jones, who famously instructed them to "check your egos at the door." The song structurally adapted the Band-Aid template but featured a traditional chorus, deployed early by Jackson and Diana Ross, which recurs as a gospel-style refrain. With a running time of seven minutes and five seconds, it nearly doubled the length of "Do They Know It's Christmas?" and featured 19 American soloists in the first three minutes, plus five bonus solos later. It was the first single in US history to be certified multi-platinum for sales of four million, with global sales estimated at over 20 million. The song dominated radio airplay, became a national event, and topped Billboard's Hot 100 for four weeks starting April 13, 1985, being both the top seller and most reported airplay record. It won Record of the Year and Song of the Year at the 1986 Grammys. "We Are the World wasn't just a best-selling single. It dominated the radio too."

# artist - George Harrison

## member of
The Beatles

## albums
**All Things Must Pass (1970)** : This triple LP set was released within the first year of The Beatles' split. It was a chart-topper in both the US and UK, demonstrating Harrison's immediate post-Beatles popularity. The album outsold solo debuts from his former bandmates, Paul McCartney's "McCartney" and John Lennon's "Plastic Ono Band." "Within the first year of the Fab Force split, his triple LP set All Things Must Pass was a chart-topper in both the US and UK, outselling his former bandmate Paul McCartney's solo debut McCartney, as well as John Lennon's plastic Ono band."
**The Concert for Bangladesh (1971) - George Harrison and Friends** : Released in late 1971 as a live triple album under the group name "George Harrison and Friends," this record captured an all-star concert organized by Harrison to benefit the starving people of the former East Pakistan. The album hit number two on the Billboard album chart and won Album of the Year at the 1973 Grammys, accepted by Ringo Starr. Its release proved challenging due to disputes among several record labels to clear performer rights. Despite its Grammy win, it was a modest seller for a 70s Grammy winner, spending nine months on the Billboard chart, the shortest of its peers, and lacking big radio hits. Even after a 2005 CD reissue, it sold only 163,000 copies in the SoundScan era and was never certified platinum. "Released in late 1971, the album captured an all-star concert which was conceived to benefit the starving people of the former East Pakistan and was mounted by a former beetle."

## songs
**My Sweet Lord (1971)** : George Harrison scored the first number one single by any former member of The Beatles with this song, reaching the top of the charts in America, England, and over a dozen countries worldwide. Its chart success at the start of 1971 arguably kicked off the biggest year of Harrison's career. The song was later identified as a "blatant ripoff" of an earlier Chiffons' hit. "Harrison scored the first number one single by any ex-beetle, reaching the top of the chart in America, England, and more than a dozen countries around the world, with the timeless and, as it turns out, blatant ripoff of an earlier chiffon's hit My Sweet Lord."
**Bangladesh (1971)** : Prior to the release of "The Concert for Bangladesh" album, George Harrison scored a minor hit single with this studio recording. Notably, this song was not taken from the live concert LP. "Before the album's release, Harrison did score a minor hit single called Bangladesh, but it was a studio recording not taken from the concert LP."

## curiosities
**Post-Beatles Popularity** : For a brief period immediately following The Beatles' breakup in the spring of 1970, George Harrison was momentarily the most popular former Beatle. This "imperial moment" allowed him to successfully launch ambitious projects like "The Concert for Bangladesh." "For a brief period just after the spring 1970 breakup of the Beatles, Harrison was momentarily the most popular former beetle."
**Concert for Bangladesh - Accounting Difficulties** : After organizing "The Concert for Bangladesh," Harrison experienced difficulties in ensuring that the project's proceeds reached its intended beneficiaries. "Harrison would later advise Band-Aid co-founders Bob Geldoff and Midge Err to find a good accountant after experiencing difficulty delivering the Bangladesh project's proceeds to its intended beneficiaries."

## songs
**Sun City (1985)** : This critically acclaimed multi-artist charity record was released towards the end of 1985, spearheaded by Little Steven. It served as a symbolic protest against South African ethnic separatism (apartheid) and specifically aimed to make it socially unacceptable for Western artists to perform in Sun City, a segregated resort in Baputaswana. The song featured an intriguing and diverse array of music stars, including rappers, punks, and jazz greats, along with artists who had previously participated in first-wave charity singles like Bruce Springsteen, Hall & Oates, Bob Dylan, and Bono. Despite only reaching number 38 on the Hot 100 and being largely ignored by pop radio programmers, it achieved strong MTV play and was hailed by critics, topping the 1985 Village Voice Pazz & Jop Poll. Its fundamental mission of making Sun City an unacceptable performance venue was considered a success. "Little Stevens' artists united against apartheid was not only a symbolic protest against South African ethnic separatism."

## curiosities
**Anti-Apartheid Mission** : The project "Artists United Against Apartheid" and its song "Sun City" were created as a direct protest against South African ethnic separatism. More specifically, it aimed to draw a clear line against Western artists performing in Sun City, which was a segregated resort located in Baputaswana. "On a narrower scale, it drew a line in the sand against Western artists performing in Sun City, a segregated resort in Baputaswana."
**Bono's Politically Pointed Solo** : In "Sun City," Bono delivered a "politically pointed, rageful solo showcase." This performance was seen as an attempt to "neutralize his infamous thank god it's them star-turn" from the earlier Band-Aid single, showcasing a different artistic and political stance. "Looking to neutralize his infamous thank god it's them star-turn, Bono offered a more politically pointed, rageful solo showcase."
**Critical Acclaim and Mission Success** : "Sun City" was widely hailed by critics and tastemakers, topping the 1985 Village Voice Pazz & Jop Poll and having its accompanying album place in the poll's top five. Little Steven later pointed out that the song succeeded in its fundamental mission of making it socially unacceptable for artists to perform in Sun City, despite its modest commercial chart performance. "As little Stephen would point out years later, the song succeeded in its fundamental mission, making it socially unacceptable for artists to perform in Sun City."

**Historical Event (1985)** : Live Aid was the largest concert event in history, held on July 13, 1985, simultaneously in two cities: Wembley Stadium in London and JFK Stadium in Philadelphia. Billed as a "global jukebox," it featured some 70 music superstars and was watched by billions around the world, serving as a benefit for Ethiopian famine relief. "a plethora of music stars put on the largest concert event in history, in two cities on a single day, July 13th, 1985."
**Ending Sing-alongs** : Both Live Aid concerts in London and Philadelphia ended not with galvanizing rock bands, but with "shambling sing-alongs." The London concert closed with a Christmas song in July, while the Philadelphia show also concluded with a similar group performance. These songs, "Do They Know It's Christmas?" and "We Are the World," were unusual because they were conceived from the start for big group sing-alongs and had been enormous hits just months prior. "each of the two Live Aid concerts ended not with a galvanizing rock band, but with a shambling sing-along."
**Immortal Performances** : A few of the performances at Live Aid are now considered "immortal." These include sets by Queen, U2, Mick Jagger, and Tina Turner, cementing their place in music history. "A few of the day's performances are now considered immortal, such as those by Queen, U2, Mick Jagger, and Tina Turner."

# concert - The Concert for Bangladesh

## curiosities
**Groundbreaking Benefit Event (1971)** : Organized by George Harrison at Madison Square Garden in New York in August 1971, "The Concert for Bangladesh" was the first large-scale benefit concert of its kind, essentially "inventing the form nearly a decade and a half before livin." It brought together a half dozen superstars and generated funds for Bangladeshi refugees, ultimately raising some $12 million through the concert, a documentary film, and a triple album. "It was the first large-scale benefit concert of its kind, essentially inventing the form nearly a decade and a half before livin."
**Fundraising Impact (1971-1973)** : Over a year and a half after the concert, its offshoot projects, including a documentary film (a box office hit in 1972) and a triple album (Billboard number two, 1973 Grammy Album of the Year), continued to generate funds for Bangladeshi refugees. It raised a quarter million dollars in the first year and ultimately accumulated some $12 million. "For a year and a half after the concert itself, its offshoot projects would continue generating funds for the cause of Bangladeshy refugees, raising a quarter million dollars in the first year and ultimately some 12 million dollars."
**Grammy Outlier (1973)** : Winning Album of the Year at the 1973 Grammys, "The Concert for Bangladesh" was considered an odd choice, especially for the 1970s, which favored "consensus monocultural Grammy wins" for pop blockbusters. It was a modest seller, lacked big radio hits, and had already been off the charts for months by Grammy Night, contrasting sharply with other million-selling, chart-topping winners of the decade. The Recording Academy was seen as rewarding "Harrison's good intentions and his cavalcade of stars." "the concert for Bangladesh was an odd album to win the top Grammy, especially in the 1970s, the premier decade for consensus monocultural Grammy wins."

# curiosity

**Robert Evans - "Get High on Yourself" TV Special (1981)** : Legendary Hollywood movie producer Robert Evans was ordered by a court to produce a public service TV special as community service in 1981, after being busted for purchasing $19,000 of cocaine. He gathered over 50 famous friends (including Bob Hope, Andy Gibb, Henry Winkler, Scott Baio) to record an anti-drug song, a "cloying, rancid, and oddly infectious diddy" written expressly for TV. The project, which one blog aptly called an "anti-drug celebrity clusterfuck," was a series of video bumpers run on ABC for a week. Few participants were actual singers, most chosen for their celebrity profile. This "ghastly spectacle" prefigured later charity singles in its visual spectacle of famous people harmonizing for cameras, introducing elements of social justice veneer, camp, and rewatchability. "Very few of the participants were singers, most were chosen for their celebrity profile and their ability to look whin some, bopping along next to a children's chorus."
**Imperial Period Concept** : This concept describes a peak career moment for an artist when they possess such influence and public embrace that they "can pull off virtually anything and see it embraced by the public." George Harrison, Michael Jackson, and Lionel Richie are examples of artists in their imperial periods who leveraged this power to create major charity works. "that peak career moment when he can pull off virtually anything and see it embraced by the public."
**1970s Grammy Album of the Year Winners' Profile** : From 1970 to 1979, the National Academy of Recording Arts and Sciences typically selected "undeniable pop blockbusters" for Album of the Year. These winning albums, such as those by Stevie Wonder, Paul Simon, Carole King, Fleetwood Mac, and Billy Joel, were almost always million or multimillion sellers, with eight of nine topping the Billboard album chart and all spawning enormous hit singles. "The Concert for Bangladesh" was a notable outlier to this trend. "From 1970 to 1979, the National Academy of Recording Arts and Sciences picks for album of the year were almost entirely undeniable pop blockbusters."
**Evolution of the Charity Mega-Single (1971-1984)** : The form of the charity mega-event evolved from initial large-scale benefit concerts like "The Concert for Bangladesh" (1971) and ensemble performances such as The Band's "The Last Waltz" (1976), through early television specials like "Get High on Yourself" (1981). It fully crystallized in 1984 with the advent of the music video era. The final form incorporated a mass display of pop stars, a round-robin sing-along, humanitarianism, and a readily available visual medium that packaged the recording as a rewatchable event. "All the elements were in place, not just the round robin sing along, not just the humanitarianism, but a commonly available visual medium that packaged a recording as a rewatchable event."
**Music Video Era's Influence (1984)** : The music video era, described as being in "full flower" in 1984, was a critical factor in the final development of the charity mega-single format. It provided a widely accessible visual medium that allowed recordings like "Do They Know It's Christmas?" to be packaged as rewatchable events, showcasing celebrity participation and humanitarian causes simultaneously. "What makes 1984 the moment when the charity mega-single achieves its final form was the fact that the music video era was in full flower."
**Billboard Hot 100 vs. British Charts - Airplay Factor** : A significant distinction between the Billboard Hot 100 in the United States and the British charts is that airplay is a direct factor in determining chart positions on the Hot 100. This meant that event records like charity singles, despite being "ungainly on the airwaves" due to their length, could become "power-rotated radio smash[es]" in the US if they garnered sufficient airplay, unlike the British charts where sales were more dominant. "One major difference between the Billboard Hot 100 and the British charts is that Airplay is a factor on our hit parade."
**Shift in Charity Single Causes (Mid-1980s)** : After the initial wave of charity singles focused primarily on North African famine relief, the mid-1980s saw a diversification of the causes addressed. As the Ethiopian famine entered a more complex phase requiring political and military intervention, subsequent mega-singles broadened their scope to include issues such as apartheid, national holidays, hunger, homelessness, and anti-violence movements, often with a goal of simple awareness rather than direct aid. "The three-year famine in Ethiopia had by then entered a more complex, less headline-friendly period, demanding political and military intervention more than direct aid."
**Critics' Divide: "Christmas vs. World Wars"** : For more than three decades, the charity singles "Do They Know It's Christmas?" and "We Are the World" have sparked ongoing debate among critics and the public about which is the better or worse cultural artifact. Critics like Robert Christgau and Tom Ewing took opposing sides, with Christgau praising "We Are the World" as "good, maybe great" and dismissing Band-Aid's as "bad or terrible," while Ewing saw Band-Aid's as more enduring as a Christmas record and "We Are the World" as "long and cumbersome." "Many of my fellow music critics have taken sides in the Christmas vs. World Wars."
