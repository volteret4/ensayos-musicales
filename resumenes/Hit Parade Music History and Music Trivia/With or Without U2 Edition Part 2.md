## albums
**The Joshua Tree (1987)** : This album topped the charts and solidified U2's reputation as "rock's conscience" by 1987. It represented the evolution of the band's punk ideals into anthemic pop, which successfully galvanized fans and filled arenas, even as lead singer Bono's stage antics began to draw criticism from pundits. "By the time of 1987's The Joshua Tree topped the charts."

**Rattle and Hum (Late 1980s)** : Released in the late 1980s, this album was deemed "middling" and, alongside a flop film, contributed to U2's perception that they needed to "reboot the band" to successfully transition into the 1990s. The mixed reception prompted a hiatus and a search for a new sound. "after a flop film and middling album called Rattle and Hum."

**Achtung Baby (1991)** : U2's seventh studio album, given a "deliberately cheeky title" borrowed from the 1983 Mel Brooks comedy film "To Be or Not to Be." Recorded with producers Brian Eno and Daniel Lanois, beginning in Berlin in the fall of 1990 and concluding in Dublin in early 1991, this album debuted at number one on the Billboard 200, marking U2's first album to launch atop the chart. It proved to be U2's deepest album in terms of charting singles and effectively reset their image for the entire decade of the 90s. "Bono decided that Achtung Baby would both allude to the album's German origins and convey its sacred cow slaughtering spirit."

**Zooropa (1993)** : This album served as a follow-up to "Achtung Baby," comprising material left over from those sessions and named after the European leg of the "zoo TV tour." It pushed U2's sound further, affirming that the band was no longer confined to a single identity, with more pronounced electronic textures and songs that diverged from traditional verse-chorus structures. Despite only one major Top 40 single, critics praised its experimental spirit, with some rating it even higher than its predecessor, and it topped the Billboard 200 for two weeks, showing loyal fans embraced this new, glossier direction. "Zooropa was named after the European leg of the zoo TV tour, the made-up word rhymed with Europa."

**Original Soundtracks 1 (1995) - by Passengers** : Recorded in 1995 by U2 in collaboration with Brian Eno under the pseudonym Passengers, this album represented U2's "Hollywood" phase. Its lead single, "Miss Sarajevo," featured guest vocals from opera star Luciano Pavarotti, and while it didn't chart in America, it became a Top 10 hit in over a dozen other countries. "At one point in 1995, they even recorded an album with Brian Eno under a pseudonym, Passengers."

**Pop (1997)** : U2's final album of the 1990s, ironically titled, delved deeper into sampling, looping, and synthesizers than "Achtung Baby" or "Zooropa." Its recording sessions were "labored and overwrought," stretching for nearly two years, forcing manager Paul McGinnis to book the PopMart tour prematurely to compel the band to finish. Upon its March 1997 release, it debuted at number one on the Billboard 200 and quickly went platinum, but sales were heavily "front loaded to die hard fans," causing it to fall out of the Top 10 in just three weeks and sell only single platinum, unlike U2's previous five multi-platinum studio albums. "But the pop album wound up labored and overwrought, with recording sessions stretching over nearly two years."

**The Best of 1980–1990 (1998)** : Released as the 90s ended, this was U2's first greatest hits album, a move typically associated with bands regrouping after a career downturn. Its release suggested U2 might be transitioning from their "MTV years to their VH1 years," as members approached 40 and pop charts were dominated by hip hop and teen pop. "As the 90s drew to a close, U2 released their first greatest hits album, a move typical of a band regrouping after a career down turn."

**All That You Can't Leave Behind (2000)** : This album marked U2's second successful career reboot, signaling a return to "earnestness and classic U2" at the turn of the century. Its title acknowledged this "back-to-basics approach," and it became one of U2's longest-charting albums, surpassing the combined chart runs of "Zooropa" and "Pop," attributed to its collection of "very ingratiating songs." "U2 pointedly titled their 2000 album All That You Can't Leave Behind, an acknowledgement of their back-to-basics approach."

**How to Dismantle an Atomic Bomb (2004)** : Following two successful albums that rebuilt U2's stadium-rocking brand, this album's lead single, "Vertigo," was arguably their most punk-rock hit, blending 80s earnestness with 90s irony. Introduced through a shrewd promotional partnership with Apple Computer, including a U2-branded iPod, the album became U2's biggest opening ever, debuting at number one on the Billboard 200 with a staggering 840,000 first-week sales, nearly double their previous SoundScan record. "How to Dismantle Anatomic Bomb became U2's biggest opening album ever."

**No Line on the Horizon (2009)** : Released five years after "How to Dismantle an Atomic Bomb," this album reunited U2 with classic-era producers Steve Lillywhite, Brian Eno, and Daniel Lanois, in an attempt to recreate their "old atmospheric magic." While it debuted at number one in March 2009, fueled by loyal fans, its first single, "Get On Your Boots," was widely considered a "dud" that killed the album's momentum. The album ultimately settled for platinum sales, a performance roughly matching "Pop" from a decade earlier. "No Line on the Horizon settled for platinum sales, roughly matching the performance of Pop a decade earlier."

**Songs of Innocence (2014)** : The first in a thematically connected trilogy, released in the fall of 2014. This album caused significant controversy not for its content, but for its release method: a deal with Apple to automatically give it away to all iTunes and iPhone users' music libraries. This "APR Disaster" was criticized as an "unasked for memory hog" that was initially "impossible to delete," leading Apple to release a special app for its removal. It debuted at a "lowly number nine" when physical copies finally sold, and was off the Billboard 200 in eight weeks. "Songs of Innocence released 10 years ago in the fall of 2014, caused some controversy, not for its content, but for the way U2 released it out into the world."

**Songs of Experience (Undated)** : The second album in U2's thematically connected trilogy, which provides meditations on the band's own history. No specific release date or chart information is provided in the transcript. "a thematically connected trilogy of meditations on the band's own history, titled Songs of Innocence, Songs of Experience, and Songs of Surrender."

**Songs of Surrender (2023)** : The final album in U2's "Songs Trilogy," released in 2023. This album is a series of "stripped-down covers of their own back catalog," primarily performed by Bono and The Edge, reflecting the band's age and accumulated wisdom. "The last album in their songs Trilogy, 2023's Songs of Surrender, is a series of stripped-down covers of their own back catalog, mostly performed by Bono and the Edge, informed by age and wisdom."

## songs
**Night and Day (1990)** : U2's cover of the Cole Porter standard, contributed to the 1990 AIDS research charity album "Red Hot and Blue." For this track, U2 fused their "brooding late 80s sound with drum machines and industrial style synthesizers." It served as a stopgap single during a prolonged break between albums and reached number two on Billboard's Modern Rock Chart. "On their cover of the Porter Standard Night and Day, U2 fused their brooding late 80s sound with drum machines and industrial style synthesizers."

**The Fly (1991)** : Released a couple of months before the "Achtung Baby" album in early fall 1991, this song announced U2's new direction. It originated from Bono's "fly character" (a leather-clad egomaniac rock star) inspired by oversized sunglasses. The track featured Adam Clayton and Larry Mullin Jr. playing with heavy syncopation, emulating Madchester and industrial music, while The Edge applied heavy distortion, departing from his characteristic 80s chime. Bono's lyrics were "cryptic aphorisms" delivered in voices ranging from an "echoey whisper to a loungy falsetto." Bono famously described it as "the sound of four men chopping down the Joshua Tree." It peaked at number 61 on the Hot 100 but topped the Modern Rock Chart in three weeks. "Released a couple of months ahead of the album in the early fall of 91, The Fly served as notice of U2's new direction."

**Mysterious Ways (1991)** : The second single from "Achtung Baby," released in November 1991, on which U2 "found the funk." It was built around a "groovy Adam Clayton bass line" and a "novel guitar effect by the edge that produced a squelching disco-style riff." Larry Mullin Jr. performed like a "human drum machine," and Bono's lyrics blended the sacred and profane, idealizing the female form and equating sex with the divine. It topped the Modern Rock Chart in November 1991, replacing a Nirvana song at number one, and also reached the top 10 on the Hot 100. "When mysterious ways topped the Modern Rock Chart in November 1991, it fit right in alongside the other danceable rock on alternative radio."

**Until the End of the World (1991)** : A syncopated rocker from "Achtung Baby" that charted on both the Modern and Album Rock charts. U2 specifically wrote this song for Wim Wenders' film of the same name. "Songs that wound up charting on the Modern and Album Rock charts included until the end of the world, a syncopated rocker U2 wrote for the Vim Vendor's film of the same name."

**Even Better Than the Real Thing (1992)** : This song from "Achtung Baby" was initially titled "The Real Thing" until Bono and producer Brian Eno changed it to its final, "more sardonic and tongue in chic" form. It topped the Album Rock Chart and also broke into the Top 40 on the Hot 100. "And even better than the real thing, a song originally titled simply The Real Thing until Bono and producer Brian Eno changed it to make it more sardonic and tongue in chic."

**One (1992)** : The quietest and most enduring song from "Achtung Baby," demoed early in the project and providing the band hope that they didn't need to break up. It represented "90s U2" with "layers of overdubs and a shimmer that reflected their new production style." Released as the album's third single, it topped both the Modern and Album Rock charts and reached the pop Top 10. "One" is routinely cited in surveys as one of the greatest singles of the rock era, often as U2's highest-ranked song, and has been covered dozens of times. "The one U2 had demoed back at the beginning of the project that gave them hope the band didn't have to break up. Appropriately enough it was titled One."

**Numb (1993)** : The first single from "Zooropa," a "droning" track that notably featured lead vocals performed by The Edge rather than Bono. This decision highlighted the album's experimental nature and its departure from traditional song structures and band roles. "Its first single, The Droning Numb, even featured lead vocals not by Bono but by The Edge."

**Lemon (1993)** : The follow-up single from "Zooropa," on which Bono sang entirely in falsetto. A critic described the backing track as "Space Age German Disco," underscoring the album's heightened electronic textures and experimental sound, moving further away from U2's traditional rock identity. "And on the follow-up single, Lemon, Bono sang entirely in falsetto and the backing track was described by one critic as Space Age German Disco."

**Hold Me, Thrill Me, Kiss Me, Kill Me (1995)** : Recorded for the soundtrack to Warner Bros.' 1995 film "Batman Forever," the third installment in the Batman series. This song topped both Billboard Rock charts and reached number 16 on the Hot 100, and with its "orchestral overtones, dramatic swoops and jokily violent title," it evoked the style of a James Bond theme. "Hold Me, Thrill Me, Kiss Me, Kill Me was recorded for the soundtrack to the third film in Warner Bros. Batman series, 1995's Batman Forever."

**GoldenEye (1995)** : The title song for the 1995 James Bond film of the same name. This track was written by Bono and The Edge, specifically for rock legend Tina Turner to perform. "For 1995's Bond film Golden Eye, Bono and the Edge wrote the title song for Rock Legend Tina Turner to perform."

**Mission: Impossible Theme (1996)** : Adam Clayton and Larry Mullin Jr. performed a cover of the 1960s TV series theme, updated for the first movie in the new Tom Cruise film franchise in 1996. This marked their debut as frontline artists, and their instrumental cover impressively made the Top 10 on the Hot 100, peaking at number 7 in June 1996, proving U2's rhythm section was "groovier than a drum machine" five years after "Achtung Baby." "Adam Clayton and Larry Mullin performing as frontline artists for the first time did a cover of the Mission Impossible theme from the 60s TV series, updated for the 90s as the theme to the first movie in the new Tom Cruise film franchise."

**Miss Sarajevo (1995) - by Passengers** : The lead single from the "Original Soundtracks 1" album, recorded by U2 and Brian Eno under the pseudonym Passengers. This song famously featured guest vocals by opera star Luciano Pavarotti. While it did not chart in America, it achieved Top 10 status in over a dozen countries. "its lead single, Miss Sarajevo, featured guest vocals by opera star Luciano Pavarotti."

**Discothèque (1997)** : The lead-off single from the "Pop" album, characterized as U2's "kitchiest single ever" and a "Dance Rock Track." Its lyrics, as Bono sang, addressed the "flimsiness of pop music itself," using the analogy of "chewing bubblegum." The accompanying video featured U2 inside a giant mirror ball, performing disco moves and dressing like the Village People. It debuted at number one in the UK, number three on the US Modern Rock Chart (instantly hitting number one), and number ten on the Hot 100 (U2's only single to debut that high), but "then it fell quickly." "When pop finally arrived in March of 97, it led off with the band's kitchiest single ever, The Dance Rock Track Disco Tec."

**Staring at the Sun (1997)** : A "melancholy Britpop-esque anthem" released as a single in an attempt to revive the "Pop" album. Despite being "better reviewed" than "Discothèque," it only peaked at number 26 on the Hot 100 and failed to return the album to the top ten or help it sell beyond single platinum. "an attempt to revive the album with a better reviewed single, the melancholy Britpop-esque anthem staring at the sun."

**Sweetest Thing (1998)** : An 80s B-side from "The Joshua Tree" era, re-recorded by U2 for their first greatest hits album. This "frothy Bono-Pen love song" became U2's first single to reach the top 20 on Billboard's Adult Pop Airplay Chart, although it "missed the top 40 on the Hot 100." "For the compilation, U2 re-recorded an old 80s B-side from their Joshua Tree days, the frothy Bono-Pen love song Sweetest Tha."

**Beautiful Day (2000)** : The lead single from "All That You Can't Leave Behind," which quickly joined U2's catalog of anthems but distinguished itself from earlier tracks like "Sunday, Bloody Sunday," "Pride," or "Where the Streets Have No Name." Although a rock song, it "shimmered with a digital sheen and soared like a pop song from the Max Martin era." The song reached number 21 on the Hot 100 and notably won the Record of the Year prize at the 2001 Grammy Awards. "The lead single from the set Beautiful Day took its place among the band's catalog of anthems."

**Stuck in a Moment You Can't Get Out Of (2001)** : A song from "All That You Can't Leave Behind," which featured a gospel piano melody played by The Edge and heartfelt lyrics by Bono, written as an attempt to dissuade a friend from suicidal thoughts. It became a Top 30 pop airplay hit. "These included stuck in a moment you can't get out of, which combined a gospel piano melody played by the edge with heartfelt lyrics by Bono, who was trying to convince a friend not to succumb to suicidal thoughts."

**Elevation (2001)** : A rockier track from "All That You Can't Leave Behind," built on a distorted guitar effect by The Edge and a "howling Bono vocal," which illustrated the evolution of U2's rock sound since albums like "Boy" and "War." Six months after the album's release, U2 remixed "Elevation" for the soundtrack to Angelina Jolie's first "Lara Croft Tomb Raider" film in 2001, transforming it into a top 10 alternative rock hit that summer. "Elevation, which built off a distorted guitar effect by the edge and a howling Bono vocal that showed just how far their rock sound had traveled since the era of boy and war."

**Walk On (2002)** : A song from "All That You Can't Leave Behind," written by Bono for a Burmese Freedom Fighter, which gained "added resonance in America after the September 11th attacks." It won the 2002 Grammy for Record of the Year, making "All That You Can't Leave Behind" the only album in history to produce two songs that won the flagship Grammy prize in consecutive years. "walk on, a song Bono wrote for a Burmese Freedom Fighter that took on added resonance in America after the September 11th attacks, won the 2002 Grammy for Record of the Year."

**Vertigo (2004)** : The lead single from U2's 2004 album "How to Dismantle an Atomic Bomb," described as "arguably their most punk rock hit" for its blend of 80s earnestness and 90s irony. The Edge constructed the song around a "hard Fender Telecaster riff," while Bono experimented with lyrics, even writing an entire Spanish version, with elements like the "Ola" and "Donde Estar" backing vocals and the famous "un, dos, tres, catorce" count-in surviving into the final track. It reached number 1 on alternative songs and cracked the top 40 on the Hot 100. "Vertigo, the lead single of U2's 2004 album How to Dismantle Anatomic Bomb, was arguably their most punk rock hit."

**Get On Your Boots (2009)** : The first single from "No Line on the Horizon," released in 2009. This track was widely perceived as a "half-assed rewrite of Vertigo" and ultimately a "dud," which significantly hampered the album's commercial momentum. "the first single, a half-assed rewrite of Vertigo called Get On Your Boots, was widely agreed to be a dud killing the album's momentum."

**XXX. (2017)** : A single by rapper Kendrick Lamar from his Pulitzer Prize-winning album, "Damn," on which U2 were featured performers. This track peaked at number 33 on the Hot 100 in 2017, marking U2's last appearance in the chart's Top 40 to date. U2 and Kendrick Lamar performed the song together at the 2018 Grammy Awards. "To date, their last appearance in the Hot 100's Top 40 came in 2017, as featured performers on rapper Kendrick Lamar's single XXX, aka X-rated, from Lamar's Pulitzer Prize-winning album, Damn."

## curiosities
**Punk Ideals to Anthemic Pop (1980s)** : U2's early career saw a transformation where they converted "punk ideals into anathemic pop" that successfully galvanized fans and consistently filled arenas, a journey culminating in the chart-topping success of "The Joshua Tree." "Irish rockers U2, who turned punk ideals into anathemic pop that galvanized fans, filled arenas."

**Band Reboot (Late 1980s-Early 1990s)** : Following a commercially "middling album" (Rattle and Hum) and a flop film in the late 1980s, U2 recognized the imperative to "reboot the band" to ensure their survival and relevance in the transition into the 1990s, leading to a period of hiatus and musical exploration. "U2 perceived that the only way they would survive the transition into the 90s was to reboot the band."

**Madchester Influence (Late 1980s-Early 1990s)** : During their hiatus, The Edge closely observed British rock, particularly the "Baggy" or "Madchester" scene from Manchester, which integrated dance beats, acid house, and rave psychedelia. Bands like The Stone Roses and Happy Mondays demonstrated how "rock bands and drum machines could coexist," a sound that keenly interested The Edge and Bono as a means to "freshen up U2." "The Edge was keeping tabs on what was happening in British rock...adopting dance beats, acid house and rave psychedelia, into a style variously tagged as Baggy or Madchester."

**Larry Mullin Jr.'s Concern with Drum Machines (Early 1990s)** : U2 drummer Larry Mullin Jr. expressed apprehension regarding the band's embrace of drum machines, fearing that "drum machines were designed to replace him" and thus threatening his role within the band. "Mullin fredded that drum machines were designed to replace him."

**Bleak Berlin Sessions (Fall 1990)** : In the fall of 1990, U2 relocated to a studio in Berlin, Germany, one year after the fall of the Berlin Wall, with producers Brian Eno and Daniel Lanois, hoping to emulate David Bowie's success there. However, the sessions were "as bleak as the rebuilding city, and often frustrating," marked by bickering and uncertainty about their new 90s sound. "Then in the fall of 1990, seeking inspiration, U2 decamped to a studio in Berlin, Germany."

**Bono's "Fly" Character (Early 1991)** : During the Dublin recording sessions for "Achtung Baby," Bono began wearing oversized wrap-around sunglasses he had "pinched from the band's wardrobe department," joking that they made him look like a house fly. This led him to invent an "entire fly character," a "leather-clad egomaniac rock star," which directly inspired the song "The Fly." "Bono made his bandmates laugh by wearing a pair of oversized wrap around sunglasses that he'd pinched from the band's wardrobe department."

**"Chopping Down The Joshua Tree" (1991)** : Bono famously articulated U2's dramatic shift in sound and image by describing the song "The Fly" as "the sound of four men chopping down the Joshua Tree." This metaphor underscored their deliberate and even aggressive departure from their signature 1980s sound and the earnest persona established by that iconic album. "Bono would later describe the fly as, quote, the sound of four men chopping down the Joshua Tree."

**"Human Drum Machine" (1991)** : In the song "Mysterious Ways," drummer Larry Mullin Jr. demonstrated his exceptional rhythmic adaptability, performing with such precision and groove that he was lauded as being his "own human drum machine." This showcased his ability to integrate seamlessly into the band's new funk and dance-oriented sound without being supplanted by technology. "Mullin was his own human drum machine."

**Zoo TV Tour (1990s)** : The "zoo TV tour" was an "elaborately staged multimedia spectacle" that U2 conceived as a satirical commentary on television and an attempt to provide audiences with "sensory overload." This tour, which saw Bono performing as both "the fly" and a devil character named "MacFisto," operated within the conceptual shadow of the "Achtung Baby" album. "That included their zoo TV tour, an elaborately staged multimedia spectacle that satirized television and sought to give the audience sensory overload."

**Hollywood Phase (Mid-1990s)** : In the mid-1990s, U2 embraced a "going Hollywood" phase, undertaking soundtrack projects and collaborations that would have been "unthinkable during their earnest 80s phase." This period included contributions to films like "Batman Forever" and writing a James Bond theme. "They spent the middle of the 90s going Hollywood, taking on projects that would have been unthinkable during their earnest 80s phase."

**Rhythm Section Frontline Debut (1996)** : In 1996, Adam Clayton and Larry Mullin Jr., U2's rhythm section, made their debut as "frontline artists" by recording a cover of the "Mission Impossible theme." Their instrumental track was updated for the 90s Tom Cruise film franchise and achieved significant commercial success, peaking at number 7 on the Hot 100. "Adam Clayton and Larry Mullin performing as frontline artists for the first time did a cover of the Mission Impossible theme from the 60s TV series."

**Marketplace Reward for Insuciance (1990s)** : Having been praised for their conscience in the 1980s, U2 in the 1990s found commercial success by adopting a more "insuciant" and ironic approach. This willingness to experiment with image and sound, including Hollywood projects, was rewarded in the marketplace. "After being hailed in the 80s for their conscience, U2 in the 90s were being rewarded in the marketplace for their insuciance."

**Manager Books Tour to Force Album Completion (1997)** : U2's manager, Paul McGinnis, took the drastic step of booking the "PopMart" tour to begin in early 1997, specifically to "finally compelled the band to finish" their "Pop" album, whose recording sessions had become "labored and overwrought" over nearly two years. The band later "came to regret agreeing to tour when the sessions to complete the CD in time were rushed." "U2 manager Paul McGinnis finally compelled the band to finish the album by booking a tour called Pop Mart, starting in early 1997."

**"Chewing Bubblegum" Lyrics (1997)** : In the song "Discothèque," Bono's lyrics directly commented on the "flimsiness of pop music itself," singing, "you know your chewing bubblegum... You know what that is, but you still want some." This self-aware commentary underscored the ironic and deconstructive nature of the "Pop" album. "This lyrics were about the flimsiness of pop music itself, quote, you know your chewing bubblegum, Bono Sang. You know what that is, but you still want some."

**"Easy Record to Admire, Hard to Love" (1997)** : All Music critic Stephen Thomas Urlewine's retrospective assessment of the "Pop" album succinctly stated that it "never seems joyous" and was "an easy record to admire, but a hard one to love." This critique captured the album's deconstructed ideas and ironic distance, rather than its capacity for inspiration or affection. "all music's Stephen Thomas Urlewine put it best when he wrote that pop quote, never seems joyous. It's an easy record to admire, but a hard one to love."

**Re-recording Old B-side for Greatest Hits (1998)** : For their first greatest hits album, U2 opted to re-record "Sweetest Thing," an old 80s B-side originally from "The Joshua Tree" era. This action was indicative of a band regrouping and re-evaluating their catalog after a period of career uncertainty. "For the compilation, U2 re-recorded an old 80s B-side from their Joshua Tree days, the frothy Bono-Pen love song Sweetest Tha."

**Transition to Legacy Act (Late 1990s-Early 2000s)** : As the 1990s drew to a close, U2 appeared to be transitioning into a "legacy act," moving from their "MTV years to their VH1 years." With all band members nearing 40 and pop charts dominated by hip hop and teen pop, they nevertheless demonstrated that they "weren't quite ready to become a legacy act." "It appeared U2 were transitioning from their MTV years to their VH1 years."

**Second Career Reboot (2000)** : In a remarkable move, U2 successfully rebooted their image for a second time starting in 2000, shifting back towards "earnestness and classic U2" with the album "All That You Can't Leave Behind." This allowed them to remain "frontline hitmakers" into middle age, achieving a rare feat of sustained chart success. "starting in 2000, U2 rebooted their image again, back toward earnestness and classic U2. And it worked."

**Grammy Record of the Year Back-to-Back Wins (2001-2002)** : U2 achieved a historic first with their album "All That You Can't Leave Behind," as two separate songs from it, "Beautiful Day" (2001) and "Walk On" (2002), won the Grammy for Record of the Year in consecutive years. This marked the "only time in history" an album produced two songs that earned this flagship Grammy prize in successive years. "It was the only time in history one album had generated two songs that took that flagship Grammy prize in consecutive years."

**Super Bowl Halftime Show Dedication (February 2002)** : In February 2002, U2 performed at the Super Bowl halftime show, dedicating their performance to the victims of the 9/11 attacks, with their names scrolling on a projected screen behind them. This poignant moment, with an "Irish band...comforting America" at the first Super Bowl after the national tragedy, elevated U2 beyond hitmakers to a "public resource or national treasure." "the band also played the Super Bowl halftime show, dedicating their performance to the 9-11 Fallen, whose names scrolled behind them on a projected screen."

**Apple iPod Partnership (2004)** : U2 engaged in a "saviast move" by partnering with Apple Computer in 2004 to introduce their "How to Dismantle An Atomic Bomb" album and the single "Vertigo." This included Apple's first ad featuring an established band's music and members (as silhouettes), and Bono and The Edge joining Apple CEO Steve Jobs on stage to unveil a U2-branded iPod, described as "gorgeous black with this beautiful red click wheel" and laser-engraved band signatures. This shrewd "Apple Gambit" made the album U2's biggest opening ever. "U2's Saviast move to introduce the song and the album was teaming with Apple Computer, which was then selling millions of its iPod music players."

**"Un, dos, tres, catorce" Count-in (2004)** : In the song "Vertigo," Bono included a count-in that famously declares "un, dos, tres, catorce," (which any rudimentary Spanish speaker knows means "one, two, three, fourteen"), a playful nod to his experimentation with Spanish lyrics for the track. "Which as any rudimentary Spanish speaker knows means 1, 2, 3, 4, 4."

**U2 360 Degrees Tour Success (2009)** : The album "No Line on the Horizon" spawned U2's "most successful tour," the U2 360 degrees tour, an "all-stadium series" featuring an "innovative in-the-round stage structure" that increased venue capacity by up to 25%. This led to attendance records at most stadiums and, for about a decade, it ranked as the "highest grossing and most attended tour of all time," only recently surpassed by Elton John, Coldplay, and Taylor Swift. "For about a decade, U2 360 degrees ranked as the highest grossing and most attended tour of all time."

**Songs of Innocence Apple Giveaway Controversy (2014)** : U2's 2014 deal with Apple to distribute "Songs of Innocence" for free directly to all iTunes and iPhone users was branded an "APR Disaster." Users widely criticized the album as an "unasked for memory hog" that was initially "impossible to delete," leading to Apple releasing a "bespoke application" for its removal. This move, which made the band appear "thirsty, sweaty, a little desperate, and as the kids say, totally cringe," was also a poor chart strategy, as the free digital giveaway rendered it ineligible to chart until physical copies were sold. "This move has been called APR Disaster for both Apple and U2."

**Long-standing Interest in Technology (Career-spanning)** : U2, particularly The Edge, has demonstrated a persistent interest in technology throughout their career, with The Edge "geeking out over new gadgets like effects pedals as far back as U2's 1980 debut album." This continuous embrace of technological innovation, seen from early gear to later partnerships with Apple, has been a key factor in their ability to adapt and prolong their career as a chart-topping band. "Heck, the edge was geeking out over new gadgets like effects pedals as far back as U2's 1980 debut album, Technology Made YouTube."

**Cultural Relevance Through Collaboration (2017)** : U2's most recent appearance in the Hot 100's Top 40 occurred in 2017 as featured performers on rapper Kendrick Lamar's single "XXX." This collaboration, which included a joint performance at the 2018 Grammy Awards, demonstrated U2's continued ability to seek "new ways to stay on the radar of people decades younger than them" through strategic partnerships. "U2 will probably never stop seeking new ways to stay on the radar of people decades younger than them."

**Acceptance of Legacy Status (2023)** : With their 2023 album "Songs of Surrender," a collection of stripped-down covers from their own back catalog, U2 appeared to have "finally accepted, you might say, surrendered to, their status as a legacy act."
