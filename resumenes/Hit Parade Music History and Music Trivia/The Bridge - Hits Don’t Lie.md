His enduring success is exemplified by Luis Fonsi reaching out to him for the song "Despacito" in 2016-2017, highlighting his continuous influence and status as a top artist. ## songs
**Despacito (2017)** : Luis Fonsi collaborated with Daddy Yankee on "Despacito," a song that proved to be a massive international hit. Fonsi himself has described the song as "pop music with a light, demo rhythm behind it and not necessarily a raggedón," demonstrating self-awareness about its genre classification. "Luis Fonsey, I've spoken to him once and he completely accepts that he's not a Raggedón or that he was fortunate to land in this world and be allowed in it for one song, but he recognizes that this was pop music with a light, demo rhythm behind it and not necessarily a raggedón."

## curiosities
**Self-Awareness Post-Success** : During the immense chart rampage of "Despacito" in 2017, Luis Fonsi was noted for his "wonderfully self-deprecating and self-aware" demeanor in interviews, understanding the unique moment he was experiencing without letting it get ahead of him. He has also expressed no intention of trying to replicate the success of "Despacito," preferring to stick to writing pop ballads on guitars, which he identifies as his core strength. "Every time I saw interviews with Fonsey, he was wonderfully self-deprecating and self-aware and realized he had a tiger by the tail and he knew it was a moment and he was not letting it get ahead of him."

# genre - Latin Pop

## curiosities
**20th Century Crossover Hits** : In the 20th century, songs like "Juan Thadamera" and "Oye Comová" achieved standard status and resonated with Anglo audiences, partly because they were popular, catchy, and frequently featured in commercials or movies, becoming part of pop culture. They were also easily accessible to non-Latino audiences, which paved the way for later crossover efforts where songwriters integrated elements appealing to Anglo tastes. "In the 20th century, songs like Juan Thadamera and Oya Comová became standards. And they resonated with Anglo audiences. Why do you think that was?"
**"Version 1.0 Latin Boom" (1999-2001)** : This period saw artists like Ricky Martin, Jennifer Lopez, and Enrique Iglesias achieve significant chart success, though their music often didn't align with what many Latino communities were listening to at the time. This phase was characterized by a "tokenized movement" where artists often adapted their sound to appeal to Anglo audiences, although it retrospectively opened doors for future Latin artists to maintain their authenticity. "what I'm calling the version 1.0 Latin boom of 99 to 2001, were you excited, dismayed, confused by what Ricky Martin and JLo and Enrique all did during that period?"
**Post-Despacito Era** : The period following "Despacito" in 2017 has led to a less compromised form of Latin music crossover, with artists like Bad Bunny, Cardi B, and J Balvin charting successfully. This shift suggests that Latin music is now so ingrained that it's considered "part of our fabric" rather than merely a crossover phenomenon, reflecting the changing demographics and listening habits of the country. "we're living in a kind of post-Despacito world in terms of Latin crossover now and it blows my mind, as I said in the podcast, how much less compromised crossover Latin music is now at the turn of the 20s that it was at the turn of the millennium."

# genre - Reggaeton

## curiosities
**Emergence and Influence** : Reggaeton became a significant musical force in the early 2000s, building upon its late 1990s existence. Its global translation and the irresistible, adaptable Dem Bow rhythm can be attributed to the rhythm's Afro-diasporic origins, found in Afrobeats, Soca, and Dancehall. "reggaeton, which becomes a force in the early 2000s. It exists in the late 90s, but it really kind of takes over in the Auts."
**Origin of Dem Bow Rhythm** : The Dem Bow rhythm, originally from Dancehall, has roots in Africa and is heard across various cultures, including Afrobeats and Soca. While "Dem Bow" is now a catch-all term for the driving beat in reggaeton, old-school producers note it refers to a very specific rhythm. "First of all, if you look at where the demo rhythm came from, and and demo is a very, you know, if you talk to one of some of the old school reggaeton producers, they'll tell you like, demo is one very specific rhythm that was used, and now it's kind of used as a catch-all term for what the reggaeton driving beat is, but it's not technically demo."
**Evolution from Underground** : The genre originated in Panama when Jamaican artists sent records to cousins who began rapping in Spanish over them. This evolved into what was called "Underground" in Puerto Rico, where elements like "malise" and Bachata guitars were added, solidifying its Latino identity around the mid-1990s and transforming it into what is now known as reggaeton. "it came from Panama. A lot of Jamaican artists were sending records to their cousins in Panama, and they started wrapping in Spanish over it, and then eventually it became what was called Underground in Puerto Rico."
**Misconception of Decline** : There was a widespread misconception around 2009-2010 that reggaeton had died. However, this was inaccurate, as sales for the genre remained strong; it was primarily media outlets that ceased paying attention. "There was this misconception that Raggedón had died in like 2009 and 2010. It was a big misconception because the sales were still there, just the media outlets stopped paying attention after a while, right?"
**Post-Despacito Resurgence** : The success of "Despacito" in 2017 played a crucial role in bringing the spotlight back to the reggaeton genre, clarifying that it had never truly died and re-engaging public and media attention. "kind of what Despacito did was bring back the spotlight to the genre and then, for example, we had a column at Ramessa before I actually worked there that was the history of Raggedón, Coptu, Pum Pum, Pum."
