## albums
**Credence Clearwater Revival (1968) - Self-Titled Debut** : The band's self-titled debut album was issued in the summer of 1968. Notably, the only songs on the album not entirely written by John Fogarty were covers, indicating an early reliance on outside material. "That same summer, the renamed Credence Clearwater Revival, issued their self-titled debut album, and the only songs not written entirely by John Fogarty were covers."
**Bayou Country (1969) - Early 1969 Release** : This album was released in early 1969 and spawned the hit single "Proud Mary." Its title reflected the "Bayou Country" sound that the California band was becoming known for, despite not being from the South. "Indeed, the album that spawned this song, Proud Mary, was even called Bayou Country."
**Green River (1969) - Third Album and Second of 1969** : Released in the summer of 1969, this was CCR's third album overall and their second of that year. Its title track, "Green River," became the lead-off single. "Bad Moon Rising was the lead-off single to Credence's third album and second of 1969, called Green River."
**Willy and the Poor Boys (1969) - Previewed by Double A-Side** : This album was previewed by the late 1969 double A-side single "Fortunate Son" coupled with "Down on the Corner." The LP was only two months old when the band issued a new single, "Travelin' Band," not from this album. "Both tracks previewed the band's forthcoming album, Willy and the Poor Boys."
**Cosmo's Factory (1970) - Chart-Topping Release** : This chart-topping studio album collected several of Credence's hits from early 1970, including "Travelin' Band" and "Who'll Stop the Rain." A significant portion of its tracks, half, wound up as singles, most of which were Top 10 hits. "Both of Credence's latest tracks would finally be collected on a CCR studio album, the chart-topping Cosmos Factory."

## songs
**Susie Q (1968)** : This song was CCR's breakthrough hit in 1968, an old-school rock-a-billy track disguised as a sprawling jam, which was so long it had to be spread across both sides of a 45 RPM single. The original had been a Top 30 hit for rock-a-billy singer-guitarist Dale Hawkins in 1957. Credence's version slowed the song down to a bluesier tempo and more than doubled its length to eight and a half minutes, an attempt by John Fogarty to adapt to the new FM dial sound. "Divided in half for release as a single, the four-minute A-side of Susie Cue got Credence on the national airwaves for the first time in the summer of 1968."
**I Put a Spell on You (1968)** : This was CCR's second single, a cover of the Screamin' Jay Hawkins classic, which followed "Susie Q." It reached number 58 in the final weeks of 1968, showing solid support for the band but indicating that relying on covers might not make them permanent hitmakers. "This second single reached number 58 in the final weeks of the year, indicating that Credence had a solid base of support, but that issuing covers was not going to make them permanent hitmakers."
**Proud Mary (1969)** : Released as a single in the first two weeks of 1969, this Roots Rock song became the band's first Top 10 hit, peaking at No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100 by March 1969. John Fogarty, who had never been to the American South, wrote the song with influences from Mark Twain and Stephen Foster, and the line "Rollin' on the River" was inspired by a movie about riverboat racing. "By March of 69, Proud Mary would reach its Billboard Hot 100 Peak of No. 2."
**Born on the Bayou (1969)** : This swamp rock homage to the Mississippi Delta was the flip side of the "Proud Mary" single and led off the "Bayou Country" album. Despite Fogarty never having visited the Mississippi Delta, the song helped establish Credence, native Californians, as the foremost swamp rockers of their day. "For example, the flip side of the Proud Mary single was born on the Bayou, a swamp rock homage to the Mississippi Delta."
**Bad Moon Rising (1969)** : CCR's second straight Top 10 hit, this song reached number two in June 1969, matching the peak of "Proud Mary." John Fogarty wrote it as a gentle satire of the hippie generation's fascination with horoscopes and astrology, packing three verses, choruses, and a guitar solo into less than two and a half minutes. "Credence's second straight top 10 hit was Bad Moon Rising, the song about a bad moon on the rise, and not for all humongering fans out there directions to a bathroom on the right."
**Green River (1969)** : Released as a single in July 1969, this "fast-breaking Hot 100 Smash" reached number two by late September. Driven by a blistering swamp rock riff, it was one of John Fogarty's most personal songs, a memoir about a childhood vacation spot near Puta Creek in Winters, California, unlike his more imaginary Southern-themed songs. "Green River was another fast-breaking Hot 100 Smash, released in July 1969."
**Commotion (1969)** : This pounding rocker was the B-side of the "Green River" single. By late summer, it had garnered enough airplay on its own to reach the Top 40, peaking at number 30 in mid-September, highlighting John Fogarty's policy of placing quality songs on flip sides. "By late summer, the B-side of Green River, a pounding rocker called Comotion, had garnered enough airplay on its own to reach the top 40, peaking at number 30 in mid-September."
**Fortunate Son (1969)** : Issued in late September 1969 on a 45 paired with "Down on the Corner," this song is ranked among the greatest in rock history. It was a lament on behalf of average young Americans who couldn't avoid the Vietnam draft, speaking "more to the unfairness of class than war itself" according to John Fogarty. "Ranked among the greatest songs in rock history, Fortune at Sun was a lament on behalf of the average young American who could not pull strings of privilege to avoid the draft for Vietnam."
**Down on the Corner (1969)** : This fun-loving, danceable Roots Rock track was the flip side of the "Fortunate Son" single, previewing the "Willy and the Poor Boys" album. After a Billboard chart policy change in November 1969, which required flip sides to share a single Hot 100 position, "Down on the Corner" and "Fortunate Son" combined forces to shoot into the Top 10, peaking at number three just before Christmas. "A fun-loving, danceable Roots Rock track down on the corner could not have been much different from its brother single."
**Travelin' Band (1970)** : Released in January 1970, this song was deliberately written and recorded to resemble a classic rock and roll rave-up from the 1950s, with John Fogarty's shouting vocal reminiscent of Little Richard. Its similarity to Little Richard's "Good Golly Miss Molly" led to a plagiarism lawsuit in 1972, which was settled out of court. "Released in January 1970, Travel in Band was both an old thing and a new thing for Credence Clearwater Revival."
**Who'll Stop the Rain (1970)** : The flip side of "Travelin' Band," this longer, statelier, and more melancholy song became one of Fogarty's most cherished anthems, written after CCR's performance at Woodstock. It was an elegy for his generation, an indirect protest song with resigned and somber lyrics that Fogarty intended to be "symbolic, not political." "Cool Stop the Rain was John Fogarty's elegy for his generation."
**Up Around the Bend (1970)** : This uptempo John Fogarty cut was released on a 45 in the spring of 1970, backed with "Run Through the Jungle." This two-sided hit peaked at number 4 in May. "Another uptempo Fogarty cut up around the bend."
**Run Through the Jungle (1970)** : Backed with "Up Around the Bend" on a spring 1970 45, this song was described as perhaps CCR's "swampiest swamp rock track ever." The combined single peaked at number 4 in May. "Backed with perhaps their swampiest swamp rock track ever, run through the jungle."
**Looking Out My Back Door (1970)** : Tapped as CCR's summer jam, this was described as Fogarty's "daydreamiest song ever," a country line dance crossed with a children's ditty. Despite listener suspicion that it was a clandestine drug anthem, John Fogarty insisted he wrote it for his three-year-old son. "A country line dance crossed with a children's ditty called, Looking Out My Back Door."
**Long As I Can See the Light (1970)** : This New Orleans-flavored soul-ballad was the B-side to "Looking Out My Back Door," offering a strong contrast. The potent two-sided hit quickly scaled the charts, reaching the Top 10 in just a month, then the Top 5 and Top 3 by October 1970. "A New Orleans flavored soul-ballad called, Long as I can see the light."
**Have You Ever Seen the Rain (1971)** : This classic single, released in 1971, was a number eight hit for CCR. In hindsight, it "reads in hindsight as a Requiem for the Band itself," reflecting the internal tensions and burnout leading to their 1972 breakup. "Their classic 1971 single, the number eight hit, have you ever seen the rain, reads in hindsight as a Requiem for the Band itself?"

## curiosities
**Chart Curse of No. 2 Hits** : From 1969 through 1971, CCR generated an amazing streak of classic pop hits, none of which topped the Hot 100. Five of these hits between March 1969 and October 1970 reached the runner-up position on the chart and no further, a unique Billboard chart feat as the most No. 2 hits by an act never to reach No. 1. "But that chart position, No. 2, would prove to be a familiar one for Credence and a bit of a curse."
**Relentless Creativity and "Overnight" Success** : At their peak in 1969 and 1970, CCR's success seemed relentless, almost an overnight phenomenon. This was fueled by John Fogarty's furiously creative songwriting period, where the band released three new albums in 1969 alone, with all singles written by Fogarty. "Their success at their peak in 1969 and 1970 seemed relentless, almost an overnight success."
**Biggest Band of 1969 (Except The Beatles)** : John Fogarty claimed that in 1969, Credence was "the biggest band in the world, except for The Beatles." Several sources even suggest that CCR outsold The Beatles in that year, highlighting their massive, albeit No. 1-less, commercial success. "John Fogarty, with later point out that in 1969, Credence were, quote, the biggest band in the world, except for The Beatles, unquote."
**Burnout and Breakup** : The relentless activity and internal tension stemming from John Fogarty's command of the band's fortunes led to burnout. CCR lasted only two more years after their major hit-making period, generating only a couple more hits before their breakup in 1972. "Burnt out from the relentless activity and the tension within the band arising from John Fogarty's command of their fortunes, CCR lasted only two more years, generating only a couple more hits before their 1972 breakup."
**Post-Breakup Legacy and Covers** : Even before the group broke up, within months of their classic singles run, covers of Credence's hits began appearing across genres, including R&B versions of "Bad Moon Rising," rock and country versions of "Who'll Stop the Rain," and a live Elvis Presley version of "Proud Mary." This demonstrated their immediate and lasting musical impact. "Within months of their run of classic singles, covers of Credence's hits began appearing across genres, R&B versions of Bad Moon Rising, rock and country versions of Hool Stop the Rain, even a live Elvis Presley version of Proud Marif."

## curiosities
**Early Musical Influences** : Growing up in El Serito, John Fogarty absorbed a diverse mix of musical influences in the 1950s, listening to 45 RPM singles and radio hits across genres from Bill Haley to Hank Williams, Ray Charles to The Kingston Trio, Buddy Holly to Little Richard, Johnny Cash to Ricky Nelson, and various Bluesmen. He claimed he picked up his "odd phrasings" like "Toinen and Boinen" on "Proud Mary" from the vocals of Howlin' Wolf. "In his autobiography, Fortune It's Son, Fogarty speaks rapturously about the music he digested in the 50s, the 45 RPM singles he bought, and the hits he heard across the radio dial, from Bill Haley to Hank Williams, Ray Charles to the Kingston trio, Buddy Holly to Little Richard, Johnny Cash to Ricky Nelson, as well as a host of Bluesman,"
**Bay Area Musician Disdaining Hippie Rock** : Despite being a Bay Area musician reaching his prime in the late 1960s, John Fogarty largely disdained hippie rock. He self-identified as "a square," found The Grateful Dead "overlong, and boring," and maintained a strong preference for the two to three-minute pop single. "This was the great irony of John Fogarty, a Bay area musician who reached his musical prime in the late 60s and mostly disdained hippie rock."
**Imagined American South in Songwriting** : John Fogarty frequently depicted an imagined, pastoral American South in his writing, such as in "Proud Mary" and "Born on the Bayou." He wrote these songs about places and experiences he had never personally encountered, like the American South or the Mississippi Delta. "This imagined pastoral American South was a recurring theme in Fogarty's writing."
**Songwriting Policy on B-sides** : John Fogarty had a policy of placing quality songs, such as "Born on the Bayou" and "Lodi," on the flip sides of CCR's 45 RPM singles, rather than using them for throwaway tracks. This strategy began to pay off as B-sides garnered their own airplay and chart positions. "John Fogridi's policy of placing quality songs like Born on the Buy You and Lodi on the flips of their 45s, not throwaway tracks, was starting to pay off."
**Most Versatile American Rock Songwriter** : John Fogarty was recognized as the most versatile American rock songwriter of his generation, a testament to his prolific and genre-spanning output during CCR's active years. This was evident even before the band's breakup. "And John Fogarty as the most versatile American rock songwriter of his generation."

## curiosities
**Formation and Early Work** : The band started as The Blue Velvets in El Serito, California, with John Fogarty, Doug Clifford, and Stu Cook meeting in junior high school and jamming by high school. By 1959, they were tight enough to be hired by singer James Powell to back him on his Do-Wop single "Beverly Angel," which was a minor regional hit and the first record appearance for Fogarty and his bandmates. "The band that started as the Blue Velvets in El Serito, California had actually been knocking around for almost a decade."
**Diverse Early Influences** : The early single "Beverly Angel" reflected the mix of influences John Fogarty and his bandmates had absorbed growing up in El Serito, synthesizing an array of genres. The track itself was a pure R&B record sung by a black vocalist, and John Fogarty played a stand-up bass borrowed from a local country musician. "What is prescient about this early single, even though the band is buried in the background, is how it reflected the mix of influences John Fogarty and his mates had absorbed growing up in El Serito."
