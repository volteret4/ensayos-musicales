## albums
**Of the Heart, Of the Soul and of the Cross (1991) - Debut Album** : PM Dawn's debut album was released just weeks after Billboard began converting its flagship charts to the new SoundScan system in May 1991. It achieved significant critical acclaim, ranking fifth in the Village Voice's annual Pazz & Jop poll of hundreds of music critics nationwide in the winter of 1992, a remarkable achievement for a new rap act.
"PM Dawn's debut album of The Heart of the Soul and of the Cross would rank fifth in the poll, a stunning result for a brand new rap act."

## songs
**Ode to a Forgetful Mind (late 1980s)** : This first single by PM Dawn, then Atrell and Jarrett Cordes, was initially rejected by executives at Tommy Boy Records for sounding too similar to De La Soul, the label's flagship act. It was eventually issued by the British label G Street.
"PM Dawn's first single owed to a forgetful mind bore more than a passing resemblance to such Dayla tracks as plug-tune."
**Set Adrift on Memory Bliss (1991)** : This song reached number one on Billboard's Hot 100 on November 30, 1991, during the first week the chart was rebooted with fully computerized SoundScan data, making it PM Dawn's first and only chart-topper. It prominently samples the British New Romantic hit "True" by Spandau Ballet, a sample that was credited, authorized, and even received a de facto seal of approval from Spandau Ballet lead singer Tony Hadley, who made a cameo in its video. It marked a significant chart milestone as the first number one rap hit by an African American act.
"Famously, PM Dawn's first and only chart-topper is built on top of a prominent sample of a hit from the early 80s."
**Reality Used to Be a Friend of Mine (1991)** : This track showcased PM Dawn's evolved ethereal sound, described as "De La Soul on happy pills," indicating a lighter, more melodic approach that distinguished them from their earlier influences.
"On tracks like Reality Used to be a friend of mine PM Dawn were Dayla Soul on happy pills, and their timing couldn't have been better."
**Paper Doll (early 1992)** : This song was a follow-up hit to "Set Adrift on Memory Bliss," rising toward the top 40 on the Hot 100 in early 1992. Despite early promise, it peaked below the top 20.
"Their follow-up hit, Paper Doll, was already on the Hot 100, rising toward the top 40."
**I'd Die Without You (1992)** : A gentle ballad featured on the soundtrack to the Eddie Murphy romantic comedy *Boomerang*, this song contained no rap content and became a number three hit in the fall of 1992, indicating PM Dawn's versatility beyond rap.
"I'd die without you, which contained no rap content at all was a number three hit in the fall of 92."
**Looking Through Patient Eyes (1993)** : This track marked PM Dawn's last big hit, reaching number six on the charts approximately six months after "I'd Die Without You." It featured a clever rap interpolation of George Michael's 1980s torch ballad "Father Figure."
"They turned George Michael's torch ballad father figure into the number six hit looking through patient eyes."

## curiosities
**Early Sound Comparison to De La Soul** : Before signing with a label, PM Dawn (then Atrell and Jarrett Cordes) were told by executives at Tommy Boy Records that they sounded too much like the label's flagship act, De La Soul. This initial assessment led to them being passed over by the label.
"they were told by executives at Tommy Boy Records that they sounded too much like the Label's flagship act Dayla Soul."
**Name Changes** : Atrell Cordes adopted the stage name Prince B, and Jarrett Cordes became DJ Minutemix. These new monikers were adopted after their first single was issued by a British label.
"the brothers had taken on new names. Atrell became Prince B, and his brother Jarrett DJ Minutemix."
**First Black Rap Act to Top Hot 100** : On November 30, 1991, PM Dawn achieved a significant chart milestone by becoming the first black rap act to hit number one on Billboard's Hot 100 with "Set Adrift on Memory Bliss" following the chart's conversion to SoundScan data.
"PM Dawn's real chart milestone was becoming the first black rap act to hit number one on the Hot 100."
**Prince B's Criticism of Conscious Rappers** : In a late 1991 interview with Details magazine, Prince B expressed his underwhelming view of the "glowering approach" of conscious rappers like Public Enemy's Chuck D, and provocatively questioned KRS-One's role as a teacher of hip hop culture, asking "KRS1 wants to be a teacher, but a teacher of what?"
"Prince B, aka a Trel Cortes, told the magazine he was underwhelmed by the glowering approach of conscious rappers like public enemies Chuck D."
**Sound Factory Confrontation** : In January 1992, during PM Dawn's set at a multi-act concert at New York's Sound Factory, KRS-One and Boogie Down Productions interrupted their performance, hijacked the turntables, and physically pushed Prince B and DJ Minutemix off the stage in an incident dubbed "the shove felt round the world" by the media. KRS-One then performed his track "I'm Still Number One."
"KRS 1 and the rest of Boogie Down Productions marched on stage during PM Dawn's set, hijacked the turntables, and literally pushed Prince B and DJ Minutemix off the stage."
**Prince B's Passing** : Atrell "Prince B" Cordes passed away in 2016 at the age of 46 due to complications from diabetes. His obituary in The New York Times acknowledged PM Dawn as early progenitors of hip hop and R&B blending, and Prince B as "underappreciated and quietly influential."
"A trail, aka Prince B, would pass away in 2016 at just 46 years of age from complications of diabetes."

# artist - Vanilla Ice

## members

## genres
Rap

## albums
**To the Extreme (1990)** : This album shot to the top of the album chart the week after "Ice Ice Baby" hit number one, ejecting MC Hammer's album from the top spot. It remained at number one for 16 weeks and had sold 7 million copies by the end of 1990, reinforcing the perception that white rappers faced lower crossover hurdles than black rappers.
"The very next week, Ice's album To The Extreme, shot to the top of the album chart, actually ejecting Hammer's album from the number one spot and staying there for 16 weeks."

## songs
**Ice Ice Baby (1990)** : This blockbuster hit by Robert Van Winkle, known as Vanilla Ice, became rap's first number one song on Billboard's Hot 100 during the week ending November 3, 1990. Vanilla Ice drew immediate criticism from rap purists for inventing a debunked street hustler backstory and for controversially sampling the bassline of Queen and David Bowie's 1981 classic "Under Pressure," which he attempted to pass off as an original creation. Despite or because of the controversy, it sold exceptionally well.
"Not only did Ice Ice Baby during the week ending November 3rd, 1990, become rap's first number one on Billboard's Hot 100."

## curiosities
**Controversial Backstory and Sampling** : Vanilla Ice, originally a white South Dallas motocross racer, generated immediate offense among rap purists following his pop breakthrough. He fabricated a street hustler backstory that was quickly exposed by the Dallas press, and controversially "nicked" the baseline from Queen and David Bowie's "Under Pressure," attempting to claim it as an original creation.
"Young Robbie offended rap purists almost immediately after his pop breakthrough, inventing a street hustler backstory that was soon debunked by the Dallas press, and pinning his tracks to huge unmistakable pop samples."
**Lower Crossover Hurdles for White Rappers** : Vanilla Ice's significant chart and sales success with "Ice Ice Baby" and "To the Extreme" reinforced the idea that white rappers faced fewer obstacles to mainstream crossover success compared to their black counterparts.
"In essence, reinforcing that the hurdles for crossover for white rappers were simply lower than they were for black rappers."

# artist - Public Enemy

## members
Chuck D

## genres

## albums
**It Takes a Nation of Millions to Hold Us Back (1988)** : This album received critical acclaim but peaked at a modest number 42 on the Billboard chart in 1988. Despite its relatively low chart position, it achieved gold and eventually platinum certification, indicating significant street sales not fully captured by the old chart methodology.
"The album peaked in 1988 at number 42, although it did go gold and eventually platinum."
**Fear of a Black Planet (1990)** : Public Enemy's third album was a massive success "out of the gate" in the spring of 1990, reportedly selling 1 million copies in its first three weeks—a staggering figure for a rap album at the time, according to PE's record label. However, on the Billboard album chart, it debuted at a modest number 40 and took over a month to scrape into the top 10, highlighting the inadequacies and bias of Billboard's pre-SoundScan manual tabulation system. Had computerized charts been in place, it would likely have been a number one album.
"According to a press release from P.E.'s own record label, in its first three weeks, Black Planet sold 1 million copies, a staggering number for a rap album at the time."

## songs
**Fight the Power (1989)** : This theme song was recorded for Spike Lee's seminal movie *Do the Right Thing* in 1989. It is still played at protest rallies and is routinely cited among the greatest rap tracks of all time, demonstrating its enduring cultural and political significance. Despite its acclaim, it did not make the Hot 100 and only reached number 20 on Billboard's R&B chart, even struggling for drive-time play on Black Radio due to its fiery nature.
"That single and theme song, Fight the Power, is still played at protest rallies and routinely cited among the greatest rap tracks of all time."

## curiosities
**Launch of Conscious Era** : Since 1987, Public Enemy, originating from Long Island, New York, were instrumental in launching rap's conscious era, emphasizing political and social themes in their music.
"Since 1987, Long Island New York rappers public enemy had helped launch rap's conscious era."
**"Rap is Black America's CNN"** : PE frontman Chuck D coined this now-oft-quoted phrase, encapsulating the band's view of rap as a vital communication channel for Black America, amping up the political fury possible on a rap record beyond earlier social critiques.
"It was PE frontman Chuck D who coined the now oft quoted phrase, rap is black America's CNN."
**Collaboration with Spike Lee** : By 1989, Public Enemy's respect was such that provocative political filmmaker Spike Lee chose them to record the theme song for his movie *Do the Right Thing*, resulting in the influential single "Fight the Power."
"So respected were public enemy by 1989 that Spike Lee, the provocative, political filmmaker, tapped PE to record the theme song to his seminal movie Do the Right Thing."
**Chart Underperformance vs. Street Success** : Despite being "huge on the streets" and achieving gold and platinum sales for albums like "Nation of Millions" and "Fear of a Black Planet," Public Enemy's tracks consistently underperformed on Billboard's charts, reflecting the systemic biases and inadequacies of the pre-SoundScan chart methodology against potent and political rap acts.
"Millicent or gangster acts like P.E. and N.W.A. were rap's other model, huge on the streets, nowhere on the charts."

## albums
**3 Feet High and Rising (1989)** : This celebrated debut album was widely embraced by white listeners, acting as a "rap Trojan horse" that infiltrated pop, rock, and alternative playlists. Alt Rock stations played early De La Soul alongside bands like The B-52's and The Cure, with their samples ranging from rock-friendly artists such as Steely Dan to Hall & Oates.
"Dayla Soul's celebrated album, Three Feet High and Rising, was widely embraced by white listeners."
**De La Soul Is Dead (1991) - Second Album** : On their second album, De La Soul actively tried to "kill the Daisy Age theme," indicated by the album cover featuring an overturned flower pot. The album also playfully engaged in lyrical "fights" with gangster rappers, signaling their move away from the "hip-hop hippies" image and the pop-rock crossover equation.
"Dayla even tried to kill the Daisy Age theme on their second album, Dayla Soul is dead."

## songs
**Me, Myself and I (1989)** : This single was De La Soul's early 1989 breakthrough, topping Billboard's R&B chart (only the second rap song to do so after LL Cool J's "I Need Love") and reaching number 34 on the Hot 100, which was an impressive crossover for a "left field rap hit." It was built off a sample of Funkadelic's 1979 single "Not Just Knee Deep" and explicitly rejected the "hip-hop hippies" label.
"Me, Myself and I was commercially potent for a rap single in 1989."

## curiosities
**Origin and Cerebral Style** : Originally from Long Island, New York, De La Soul, like Public Enemy, preferred a cerebral form of hip-hop, but they carved a distinct path with a quirkier style, blending conscious rap with more melodic samples, loopier lyrics, and madcap humor.
"This trio of rappers called Postenus, Trugoy and Mace, and backed by DJ Prince Paul were at the forefront of a new quirkier style, conscious rap but with more melodic samples, loopier lyrics and madcap humor."
**The Daisy Age** : De La Soul coined the term "the Daisy Age" for their new style, with Daisy being an acronym for "Da Inner Sound Y'all."
"They called it the Daisy Age. Daisy was an acronym for Da inner Sound Yall."
**Rejection of "Hip-Hop Hippies" Label** : When their first singles were released on the independent Tommy Boy label in 1988, the press tagged them as "hip-hop hippies" due to their "flower child ethos." The trio immediately rejected this term, openly dissing it on their 1989 breakthrough single "Me, Myself and I."
"the press latched onto Dayla's flower child ethos, tagging the crew as hip-hop hippies, a term the trio rejected almost immediately."

# label - Billboard

## curiosities
**1991 Chart Methodology Change** : In 1991, Billboard magazine fundamentally changed how it calculated America's flagship pop charts, specifically the Hot 100 and album chart. This shift, driven by new barcode scanning technology called SoundScan, aimed to make the charts significantly more accurate.
"1991 was the year Billboard magazine changed the way it calculated America's flagship pop charts."
**Pre-SoundScan Manual Tabulation System (1980s)** : Through the end of the 1980s, Billboard's charts operated on a manual tabulation system. This involved contacting record retailers and asking for ranked lists of best-selling albums and singles, without employing specific sales counts. This system was "ripe for manipulation" and corruption, as detailed in books like Frederick Danins' *Hitmen*, where labels reportedly bought off retailers and DJs to influence chart reports.
"Through the end of the 1980s, the Billboard charts still operated under a manual tabulation system."
**Pre-SoundScan Data Problem** : The old manual chart system suffered from a "simple math problem" due to limited data. Billboard lacked precise sales information, meaning a top album could have sold vastly different quantities (e.g., 50,000 vs. 500,000 copies) without the magazine knowing the magnitude of the hit.
"a top album may have sold 50,000 copies, or 500,000, but not even Billboard had that information."
**Pre-SoundScan Bias Against Rap and Country** : The old chart system revealed a significant genre-based and racial/cultural bias. Record retailers and radio DJs "simply reported fewer rap or country titles than they did rock or pop titles," whether intentionally or unconsciously, leading to an unfair representation of these genres' popularity.
"Whether intentionally or unconsciously, record retailers and radio DJs simply reported fewer rap or country titles than they did rock or pop titles."
**SoundScan Conversion for Album Chart (May 1991)** : In May 1991, Billboard began converting its album charts to the new SoundScan system, which tallied actual music sales at retail counters accurately, piece by piece, via barcode scanners. This led to immediate, drastic changes in chart performance for albums like *New Jack City* and N.W.A.'s *Efil4zaggin*.
"In May 1991, just weeks before their album dropped, Billboard began converting its flagship charts to the new sound scan system."
**Hot 100 Modernization Challenges** : The Hot 100 singles chart was more complex than the album chart, being a hybrid that historically incorporated at least two data pools: sales of singles and radio airplay. Modernizing it required not just SoundScan sales data but also accurate airplay tallying.
"But the Hot 100 is a more complicated chart, a hybrid."
**Broadcast Data Systems (BDS) Integration (1990)** : Since 1990, Billboard had been testing Broadcast Data Systems (BDS) technology to address the need for accurate airplay data for the Hot 100. BDS tracked song plays on major radio stations, capturing what was *actually* played, circumventing issues of stations reporting false plays or being bribed by labels.
"Since 1990, the magazine had been testing a parallel technology by broadcast data systems or BDS, which tracked song plays on the nation's biggest radio stations."
**Hot 100 SoundScan Reboot (November 30, 1991)** : Half a year after the album chart conversion, Billboard relaunched the Hot 100 on November 30, 1991, incorporating SoundScan for sales and BDS for airplay. This "new Hot 100 gave Billboard Readers Whiplash," causing singles to scatter across the chart and revealing which had been propped up by inflated airplay.
"On November 30th, 1991, Billboard relaunched the Hot 100, and PM Dawn was sitting at number one."

# instrument - SoundScan

## curiosities
**Pivotal Technology for Chart Accuracy (1991)** : SoundScan was a barcode scanning technology introduced in 1991 that revolutionized Billboard's chart calculations by accurately tallying actual music sales, piece by piece, at retail counters for the first time.
