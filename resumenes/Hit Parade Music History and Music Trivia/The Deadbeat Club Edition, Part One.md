## albums
**The B-52's (1979) - Self-titled Debut Album** : This album was produced by Island founder Chris Blackwell at his Compass Point studio in the Bahamas and was released in the summer of 1979. It featured a lean nine songs, including new wave party tracks like "Dance This Mess Around" and "52 Girls," along with a re-recording of "Rock Lobster." Despite the band's surprise that it didn't sound "fuller or more expensive," Blackwell intentionally kept it stripped down and minimal. The album was a critical and commercial success, placing seventh in the Village Voice's Pazz and Jop Poll at the end of 1979, making it the only album by a new band in the top 10 of that critics' poll, excluding the reissued U.S. Clash album. It spent over a year on Billboard's album chart and went gold by the fall of 1980 for sales of half a million copies, eventually achieving platinum status. "Chris really wanted to keep it stripped down and very minimal."
**Wild Planet (1980) - Second Album** : Released in late 1980, this album was also executive produced by Chris Blackwell. It rode the charts for about half a year before achieving gold status in 1981, indicating strong commercial performance similar to its predecessor. However, "Wild Planet" generated only one charting single, "Private Idaho," which had a minor hit status. "Wild Planet... would ride the charts for about half a year before going gold in 1981."
**Mesopotamia (1982) - Third Album (EP)** : This album was intended to be a full-length release and was produced by David Byrne of Talking Heads, but it became a "mess" due to label pressure and Byrne's attempts to push the band towards an overdubbed, percussion-driven sound, reminiscent of his work with Brian Eno. The sessions were aborted midway, resulting in an EP of only six tracks. Crucially, none of the songs from "Mesopotamia" spawned music videos, preventing the band from capitalizing on the emerging MTV explosion. "Rather than being a triumph, the album was a mess, done in by label pressure, and Byrne's own attempts to move the band toward a heavily overdubbed, percussion-driven sound better suited to talking heads."
**Whammy (1983) - Full-length Comeback Album** : Released as the band's full-length comeback album, "Whammy" was a decent seller, reaching the top 30 on the charts. While it took years to be certified, it eventually achieved gold status. The album included a glossy music video for the single "Legal Tender," which featured catchy synthesizers echoing current pop sounds, but the single only peaked at number 81 on the Hot 100. "Whammy was a decent seller. It reached the top 30, and while it would not be certified for years, it ultimately went gold."

## songs
**Rock Lobster (1978) - DB Records Version** : This original version, recorded in early 1978 and released by tiny independent label DB Records in Atlanta, served as a blueprint for new wave. It featured three vocalists, Fred Schneider's post-punk "sprechgesang," and Kate Pierson's and Cindy Wilson's high girl group harmonies and sea creature noises, some invented by Schneider (like the "bikini whale"). The lyrics were a gender-flipped reimagining of beach party culture, and Pierson played Farfisa organ, while Ricky Wilson played a uniquely post-punk surf guitar riff on a Mosrite electric guitar. The single was a sensation by indie standards, selling roughly 20,000 copies, and inspiring John Lennon who noted its similarity to Yoko Ono's music. "That's the original version of Rock Lobster, issued in 1978 by the tiny independent label DB Records based in Atlanta."
**Rock Lobster (1979) - Warner Brothers Version** : This re-recorded version appeared on the band's self-titled debut album and was also released as a Warner Brothers single in the summer of 1979. It was longer and harder-edged than the DB Records version, expanding from four and a half minutes to nearly seven minutes, a length suitable for album-oriented rock (AOR) FM radio in the late 70s. The track concluded with a powerful "gale force raven" adorned by screams from Fred and Cindy, with Ricky tearing into a slamming guitar solo. In the UK, it reached number 37 in August 1979, while in America, it took longer to break, peaking at number 56 on the Hot 100 in May 1980. "Where the DB Records version was speedy and only four and a half minutes long, the Warner Brothers version was nearly seven minutes long."
**Dance This Mess Around (1979)** : This was one of the strutting dance tracks featured on The B-52s' self-titled debut album, contributing to its identity as a collection of new wave party tracks. "the strutting dance this mess around"
**52 Girls (1979)** : This song was a skeletal and punky track included on The B-52s' self-titled debut album, showcasing the band's raw, stripped-down sound. "the skeletal punky 52 girls."
**Private Idaho (1980)** : This shimmying party track was the only charting single from the band's second album, "Wild Planet," released in late 1980. It achieved a minor hit status, reaching number 74 on the Hot 100. A decade later, it inspired the title of Gus Van Sant's film "My Own Private Idaho," starring River Phoenix. "Private Idaho, a shimmying party track, which, a decade later, would inspire the title of the Gus Van Sant River Phoenix movie My Own Private Idaho."
**Legal Tender (1983)** : This was the seventh single released by The B-52s, and only their third minor hit overall, peaking in the low 80s on the charts. It was built around catchy synthesizers, aligning with the sound of contemporary pop music. A glossy music video was produced for this song, marking the band's first official MTV video, released with their 1983 album "Whammy," but the single could only manage a Hot 100 peak of number 81. "Just seven spots below the B-52s, who were peaking in the charts low 80s, with their seventh single, and only third minor hit overall, legal tender."

## curiosities
**Band Name Origin** : The B-52s named themselves after a collection of letters and numbers, designating both a 1950s military bomber and, more specifically, a 1960s bouffant hairstyle, which became a signature look for the band's female members. "a collection of letters and numbers, designating both a 1950s military bomber and, more to the point, a 1960s Bufant Heritage, the B-52s."
**Athens, Georgia Origin and Scene** : The band formed in 1977 at a Chinese restaurant in Athens, Georgia, after consuming a potent rum cocktail. Members Kate Pierson and Fred Schneider, transplants from New Jersey, moved to Athens, while Keith Strickland, Ricky Wilson, and Cindy Wilson were Athens natives. Pierson described Athens as not a likely place for post-punk, stating it "really had nothing happening" and was a "farmer's town" with a university that felt separate. Cindy Wilson noted the university made the town "real liberal," and Schneider added, "it's such a loose town, the wilder, the better." "Their formation, one night at a Chinese restaurant in Athens that served a potent rum cocktail, happened far from the punk mecca of New York City."
**Eclectic Musical Influences** : While aware of punk acts like the Sex Pistols (Kate Pierson regretted missing their only Atlanta show), The B-52s' influences spanned much wider. They loved classic pop, especially in contrast to the dominant chart acts of 1976 like Captain & Tennille and Barry Manilow. Fred Schneider mentioned listening to The Velvet Underground alongside James Brown, Motown, and citing Martha and the Vandellas as his favorite band. They also gravitated towards late 50s and early 60s music, girl groups like The Ronettes, kitschy pop like Sam the Sham and the Pharaohs, and three-chord garage pop like Question Mark and the Mysterians, all of which infused their music and later influenced New Wave itself. "their tastes were too eclectic for the radio."
**John Lennon's Inspiration** : In one of his final interviews before his murder in 1980, John Lennon told Rolling Stone that he heard "Rock Lobster" by The B-52s in Bermuda and thought it sounded "just like Yoko's music." Lennon was wowed by the band's freshness and gender parity, which inspired him to record again and collaborate with Yoko Ono as a peer on their album "Double Fantasy," his first album in five years and final recording. Kate Pierson and Cindy Wilson were major fans of Yoko Ono, and their sea creature sounds on "Rock Lobster" were direct homages to Ono's vocal style. "It sounds just like Yoko's music."
**Mid-Decade Crisis and Hiatus** : After 1983, The B-52s went on a long hiatus, which "nearly ended their existence as a band." The reasons for this hiatus would not be revealed for another two years, marking a period of difficulty for the group despite their earlier successes. "That's when the B-52's went on a long hiatus, for reasons that would not be revealed for another two years."
**Gender Fluidity and Camp Presentation** : Front people in The B-52s presented "unapologetic, sui-generis sexual identity" and a "sense of camp" in their performances, long before they came out publicly or terms like non-binary or transgender were commonly understood. This pioneering approach to gender presentation in a rock context showcased a diverse way to be alternative, much like the New York Dolls who performed in drag years earlier. "They were living gender fluidity in a rock context decades before terms like non-binary or transgender were commonly understood in popular American culture."

## albums
**Chronic Town (1982) - EP** : This was R.E.M.'s first non-single release and their first on IRS Records, a quasi-independent label. The EP, elliptically titled, continued Michael Stipe's open-to-interpretation lyrics, a deliberate feature of the band's sound. Signing with a label like IRS, which had more limited resources, was by design for the band, as they prioritized avoiding debt and maintaining total control over their creative output. "Chronic Town was also R&M's first release on IRS Records, a quasi-independent label founded by Miles Copeland."
**Murmur (1983) - Debut Album** : Released in the spring of 1983, "Murmur" was R.E.M.'s full-length debut album, produced by Don Dixon and Mitch Easter. It opened with a slower version of "Radio Free Europe" and featured the album's centerpiece ballad, "Talk About the Passion," where Michael Stipe offered clearer romantic lyrics despite his generally cryptic style. The album's cover depicted Kudzu, a weed common in the Southeastern U.S. "Murmur" charted surprisingly well, debuting on Billboard's album chart in mid-May 1983 and peaking at number 36 in mid-August, an atypical success for an independent album with a shy, hard-to-decipher singer. Critics in Rolling Stone named it the 1983 Album of the Year, surpassing "Thriller," "Synchronicity," and "War." "REM's full length 1983 debut album, produced by the team of Don Dixon and Mitch Easter."
**Reckoning (1984) - Sophomore Album** : This album was quickly recorded after the success of "Murmur." It was previewed by the performance of a new song, "South Central Rain," on NBC's "Late Night with David Letterman" in October 1983, which would eventually be included on "Reckoning." "South Central Ring served as a preview of REM's quickly recorded sophomore album Reckoning."

## songs
**Radio Free Europe (1981) - Hib-Tone Version** : This was R.E.M.'s debut single, released in 1981 on the short-lived Athens label, Hib-Tone. It was characterized by a "jittery" speed, almost punk in nature. Michael Stipe's lyrics were nearly incomprehensible, with phrases like "raving station, calling on in transit," a feature that fans quickly noted. The B-side, "Sitting Still," was also difficult to parse. "R&M issued its first single in 1981 on the short-lived Athens label, Hibitone."
**Sitting Still (1981)** : Released as the B-side to R.E.M.'s debut single, "Radio Free Europe," in 1981, this song also appeared on their 1983 full-length debut album, "Murmur." Its lyrics were even harder to parse than "Radio Free Europe," with its pre-chorus seemingly referring to a woman named Katie Buying or possibly barring a kitchen, reinforcing Michael Stipe's impressionistic and open-to-interpretation lyrical style. "The singles B-side, a song called Sitting Still, would also wind up on the 83 album, but it was even harder to parse."
**Radio Free Europe (1983) - Murmur Version** : This version of the song appeared as track one on R.E.M.'s full-length 1983 debut album, "Murmur." In contrast to the jittery speed of its original 1981 Hib-Tone single release, this album version was considerably slower. The single for this version reached number 78 on the Hot 100 in mid-August 1983, a notable achievement for an atypical sound released by an independent label. "This slower version of Radio Free Europe was track one on Merma."
**Talk About the Passion (1983)** : This ballad served as the centerpiece of R.E.M.'s debut album, "Murmur." It showcased Michael Stipe's ability to offer "a clear romantic lyric" when he chose to, contrasting with his more characteristic cryptic and impressionistic lyrical style. "when he wanted to, he could offer a clear romantic lyric, as on the album's centerpiece ballad, talk about the passion."
**South Central Rain (1983/1984)** : This new song, performed on NBC's "Late Night with David Letterman" on October 6, 1983, didn't have a formal title at the time of its TV debut ("too new to be named"). It would eventually be given the "arch title" "South Central Rain," a phrase actually sung in the song, though it could have easily been called "Sorry." This track served as a preview of R.E.M.'s quickly recorded sophomore album, "Reckoning." "That new track would eventually wind up with the arch title South Central Ring. A phrase actually sings in the song."

## curiosities
**Athens, Georgia Origin and Ties** : All four members of R.E.M. had moved either to Athens or nearby Macon, Georgia, in their teenage years, establishing even stronger ties to the Athens scene than The B-52s. They were known as "perhaps the most famous force in ever to emerge from Athens, Georgia." "R&M's ties to the Athens scene were even stronger than the B-52's."
**Shared Love of Deep Cuts and Formation Story** : Guitarist Peter Buck, in his early 20s, worked at an Athens music shop called Wuxtry Records. He met future singer Michael Stipe in 1979 when Stipe repeatedly bought deep-cut albums Buck had set aside for himself. They bonded over a shared love of The Velvet Underground and CBGB rock legends Television. "Buck and future singer Michael Steip met in 1979, when Buck was a clerk at an Athens music shop called Wuckstree Records."
**Peter Buck's "Jangle" Guitar Style** : Peter Buck's signature guitar style, characterized by "broken arpeggiated chords," was later termed "jangle," a rock critic term from the 70s. He picked up this sound from 70s power pop acts like Alex Chilton's Memphis band Big Star. While some critics compared his sound to Roger McGuinn of The Byrds, Buck disputed this, claiming more influence from the country rock version of The Byrds led by Graham Parsons, and Parsons' later solo work with Emmylou Harris. "Peter Buck plays guitar like a guy who worked in a record store."
**Intuitive and Novel Musicianship** : Early producers Mitch Easter and Don Dixon noted the intuitive, almost naive playing of Peter Buck and Mike Mills. Dixon described Mills' bass parts as "not traditional" and Buck's guitar playing as using "what he knew about guitar, which was kind of limited, to really great effect." This approach, similar to Ricky Wilson's unusual guitar tunings for The B-52s, allowed them to create something novel from their relative inexperience. "Mike played bass parts that were not traditional bass parts, and Peter used what he knew about guitar, which was kind of limited, to really great effect, unquote."
**Michael Stipe's Lyrical Style** : Michael Stipe, a student of punk and post-punk with admiration for British art punk band Wire, developed an impressionistic lyrical style. His images drew heavily, though indirectly, from Patti Smith's 1975 debut album "Horses," which he considered his favorite album of all time. His open-to-interpretation lyrics were a deliberate "feature, not a bug" for the band, often making his phrases almost incomprehensible to listeners who noticed this immediately upon the release of "Radio Free Europe." "For R&M, Steip's open-to-interpretation lyrics were a feature, not a bug."
**Preference for Indie Labels and Control** : When R.E.M. began to attract music business attention, they had two key desires: "never be in debt" and "total control over everything we did." They found IRS Records, a quasi-independent label, to be perfect for this, as it wouldn't spend much money on anyone anyway, aligning with their desire for creative and financial autonomy. "We wanted to never be in debt, and we wanted to have total control over everything we did."
**Peter Buck's Disdain for Synth-Pop** : As New Wave's commercial success surged in the U.S. charts, fueled by MTV and dominated by UK synthesizer bands like The Human League and A Flock of Seagulls, Peter Buck openly scoffed at bands like A Flock of Seagulls in early interviews. This highlighted R.E.M.'s distinct branch of post-punk new wave, rooted in Americana rather than synth-pop. "In his earliest interviews, the opinionated Peter Buck openly scoffed at bands like The Seagulls."
**Shy Stage Presence and TV Debut** : In live performances, Michael Stipe was known to hide behind his hair, which covered his forehead and sometimes half his face. During their national television debut on NBC's "Late Night with David Letterman" on October 6, 1983, Stipe even hid behind Peter Buck when David Letterman approached the band to greet them, reinforcing his shy public persona. "When REM played NBC's Late Night with David Letterman, Stype actually hid behind Peter Buck when Letterman approached the band to say hi."

# genre - Athens Rock

## curiosities
**R.E.M. as the Face of the Scene (1983-1985)** : During the mid-1980s (1983-1985), R.E.M. was recognized as the leading band and "the face" of the Athens Rock music scene, while their predecessors, The B-52s, were mostly out of the public eye. "REM were the face of Athens Rock, and their older siblings, the B-52s, were off the radar almost entirely."

# genre - Synth Pop

## curiosities
**Dominance on Top 40 Radio in Mid-80s** : In the mid-1980s, Top 40 radio was heavily dominated by synth pop, which made it challenging for bands like R.E.M. to gain traction and secure radio play, despite their growing popularity on other charts. "on top 40 radio, still then dominated by synth pop, REM were nowhere to be found."

# label - IRS Records

## curiosities
**Efforts to Promote R.E.M.** : IRS Records made significant efforts to elevate R.E.M.'s profile, including producing the music video for "Can't Get There from Here" with subtitles and creating the MTV show "IRS Presents the Cutting Edge" as a promotional platform for the band and others. "IRS's efforts to get REM to the next level didn't bear much fruit."
**"IRS Presents the Cutting Edge" TV Show** : IRS Records produced a television show for MTV titled "IRS Presents the Cutting Edge," which showcased a variety of independent and college-friendly rock bands, including R.E.M. and the Red Hot Chili Peppers, as an alternative avenue for promotion. "IRS records was so intent on improving the band's profile, the video for can't get there even featured subtitles."

**Songwriting Inspiration vs. Force** : A general philosophy on songwriting suggests that true inspiration is key, and it's difficult to force a song if it doesn't come together quickly. This idea is echoed by Sky Saxon of the Seeds. "You can't really expect to be inspired or hit the right thing all the time, rather than force it and really push something to try to make it right song."
**Rapid Songwriting Principle** : A specific songwriting philosophy, attributed to Sky Saxon of the Seeds, posits that if a song does not come out "right" within approximately 20 minutes, it is not worth writing. "if it doesn't come out right in about 20 minutes really, it doesn't work."
