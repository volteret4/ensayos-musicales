**Document (1987) - Pivotal Top 10 Album** : Powered by the single "The One I Love," this album became R.E.M.'s first top 10 album in early November 1987. It quickly achieved gold certification less than two months after its release and platinum status within four months. It marked the band's first collaboration with producer Scott Litt, who would go on to produce several R.E.M. albums, and helped galvanize the band's rock sound, particularly the drums of Bill Berry. "Document was a pivotal record for the band in more ways than one."
**Green (1988) - Acclaimed Major Label Debut** : R.E.M.'s first major label album, released in 1988, was acclaimed by critics for successfully broadening the band's sound without compromising their identity. **Out of Time (1991) - Chart-Topping Blockbuster** : This seventh studio album, released in the spring of 1991, achieved the "seemingly impossible" by topping the Billboard charts, outselling albums by contemporary pop stars Michael Bolton and Mariah Carey. Kate Pierson of The B-52s contributed vocals to two prominent tracks, including "Me in Honey," where her voice provides the album's final sound. "It topped the Billboard charts, outselling albums by Michael Bolton and Mariah Carey."
**Automatic for the People (1992) - Quadruple Platinum Success** : Released in 1992, this album continued R.E.M.'s commercial success in the 90s, matching the quadruple platinum sales achieved by its predecessor, *Out of Time*. "After Out of Time, R.E.M. came back with one smash album after another, including 1992's Automatic for the People, which matched Out of Times quadruple platinum sales."
**Monster (1994) - Chart-Topping & Record Deal** : This album, released in 1994, brought R.E.M. back to the top of the album chart. "Monster, which took R.E.M. back to the top of the album chart, and empowered them to resign to Warner Music with a blockbuster $80 million accordion for the show."

## songs
**Fall on Me (1986)** : The lead single from R.E.M.'s 1986 studio album, *Life's Rich Pageant*, this song served as an elegy for the environment, lamenting the condition of the sky due to acid rain and expressing anger at the planet's degradation by corporate interests. Featuring moving harmony vocals by Mike Mills, its lyrics were noted as Michael Stipe's most direct to date. Although it only reached number 94 on the Hot 100, it was a sizable radio hit, climbing to number 5 on the Album Rock Chart, marking R.E.M.'s first top 10 hit on any Billboard chart. "It was an elegy for the environment, mourning the state of the sky in the age of acid ringing, and angry at the ruin of the planet at the hands of corporate interests."
**Superman (1986)** : Released as the second single from *Life's Rich Pageant*, this song was a cover of a 1969 track by the obscure Texas hippie rock band The Clique. Peter Buck and Michael Stipe, known for their crate-digging, revived this song, with Mike Mills taking on the lead vocal and Stipe providing backing vocals. Following "Fall on Me," R.E.M.'s version reached number 17 on the Album Rock Chart in the fall of 1986. "Coming on the heels of Fall on me, REM's Superman reached number 17 on the album rock chart in the Fall of 1986."
**Ages of You (1987)** : An early track by R.E.M. that had been omitted from their *Chronic Town* EP, this song found renewed attention when it was included on the 1987 rarities compilation, *Dead Letter Office*. It spent a month on the album rock list and managed to scrape the top 40 of the rock charts, indicating the band's growing presence on rock radio even with older material. "An early track by the band that had been left off the chronic town EP called Ages of U, spent a month on the album rock list and even scraped the rock charts top four."
**The One I Love (1987)** : Released at the end of summer 1987 as the lead single from *Document*, this song was widely perceived as romantic despite Michael Stipe describing it as "incredibly violent" and "about using people over and over again" in a *Musician* magazine interview, noting its very direct lyrics and the phrase "a simple prop to occupy my time." It became the band's long-awaited top 40 breakthrough, peaking at number 9 on the Hot 100 by December 1987, and holding the number 2 spot on the Album Rock Chart for a month. "It was also, at last, the band's top 40 breakthrough."
**It's the End of the World as We Know It (and I Feel Fine) (1988)** : Released as the second single from *Document* in early 1988, this song was considered "radio unfriendly" due to its stream-of-consciousness lyrics, primarily written by Michael Stipe with contributions from other band members including Peter Buck. Inspired by Bob Dylan's "Subterranean Homesick Blues," it packed 614 words into just over 4 minutes, nearly twice as many as Dylan's song. It peaked at number 69 on the Hot 100 and number 16 on Album Rock Tracks but became an MTV favorite, despite its video (directed by James Herbert) not featuring the band. The song, which references comedian Len Bruce and rock critic Lester Bangs, has become an R.E.M. staple and radio perennial, being the third most played R.E.M. track on US terrestrial radio in 2017 with nearly 28,000 spins. "It's the end of the world as we know it and I feel fine was about as radio unfriendly as a song gets."
**Orange Crush (1989)** : The lead-off single from R.E.M.'s major label debut album *Green*, this track was described as perhaps R.E.M.'s toughest to date. It contained a direct reference to Agent Orange, the herbicide chemical used in the Vietnam War, which has been linked to disease and death among soldiers; Michael Stipe, having been raised in a military family, learned about the agent from his father, who served in Vietnam. Although not issued as a commercial single, making it ineligible for the Hot 100, it was a radio smash, topping album rock playlists for two weeks and alternative playlists for two months, and reaching number one on the Modern Rock Chart less than three months after the chart's launch. "Orange Crush was perhaps REM's toughest track to date, a reference to Agent Orange, an herbicide chemical used widely in the Vietnam War that has been blamed for the disease and death of countless soldiers, both Vietnamese and American."
**Stand (1989)** : As the follow-up to "Orange Crush," this song presented a completely different side of R.E.M.'s sound, essentially functioning as a dance record. Michael Stipe literally instructs the listener on how to perform the dance within the lyrics, and its music video depicted multicultural groups of dancers performing the steps in unison. On the Modern Rock Chart, it almost immediately replaced "Orange Crush" at number one, taking over in January 1989 after Julian Cope briefly held the top spot for one week. More significantly, "Stand" also became a pop hit, entering the top 40 in February 1989 and breaking into the top 10 by April, peaking at number 6, making it R.E.M.'s first major pop hit since "The One I Love." "Stand was, in essence, a dance record, REM's style."
**Turn You Inside Out (1989)** : This raucous track was one of several songs from the *Green* album that continued to generate hits at Rock Radio during the first half of 1989, even after "Stand" was the only Top 40 hit. "That included the raucous turn you inside out."
**Pop Song 89 (1989)** : A cheeky pop song from the *Green* album, this track served as a meta commentary on the perceived flimsiness of pop lyrics. It was particularly memorable for its music video, which Michael Stipe described as a statement about his feminism and fluid views on gender. In the clip, Stipe appears topless, wearing only a colorful leotard, shimmying alongside three topless female dancers in identical leotards, though on MTV, black bars were used to cover all chests for broadcast, creating a potent statement of solidarity and gender parity. "Pop song 89 was most memorable for its video. A statement from Michael Steip about his feminism and his fluid views on gender."
**Losing My Religion (1991)** : This smash hit was the driving force behind R.E.M.'s 1991 album *Out of Time*, becoming the band's biggest hit ever. It reached number 4 on the pop charts and number 1 on the Modern Rock Chart. "It was powered by the band's biggest hit ever, the number four pop, number one modern rock smash, losing my religion."
**Shiny Happy People (1991)** : Released as the follow-up single to "Losing My Religion" from the *Out of Time* album, this ebullient pop song was another collaboration featuring Kate Pierson of The B-52s. It showcased counterpoint vocals by Pierson, Michael Stipe, and Mike Mills, and reached number 10 on the charts in the summer of 1991. "Kate contributed vocals on two of the most prominent tracks on Out of Time, R.E.M.'s 7th studio album."

## curiosities
**Early Years and Independent Success** : Formed in Athens, Georgia, R.E.M. developed a subgenre of indie rock characterized by its cracking rhythm section, Michael Stipe's elliptical lyrics, and Peter Buck's jangling guitar. Their first LP in 1983 garnered critical acclaim, topped critics' polls, and sold remarkably well for a band considered too independent and eccentric for top 40 radio, achieving a level of success unique for acts without major radio hits by the mid-1980s. "In their very first LP in 1983, R-E-M were wowing audiences, topping critics' polls, and selling remarkably well for a band that was too independent and eccentric for top 40 radio."
**Mid-1980s Status and Influence** : By the mid-1980s, R.E.M. had achieved widespread critical acclaim, respectable sales for their albums (which reached the middle rungs of Billboard's album chart), and had developed sonic fingerprints that were beginning to influence other acts. They had released three full-length albums and an EP, and their label had "much bigger plans" for them, with the expectation that they would become platinum sellers and pop hitmakers by the dawn of the 1990s. "Red spread critical acclaim, respectable sales, and sonic fingerprints that were beginning to rub off on other acts."
**Michael Stipe's Political Stance (1986)** : For the first time, starting with their 1986 album *Life's Rich Pageant*, Michael Stipe and R.E.M. decided to wear their hearts on their sleeves, becoming more openly political in their music. "And for the first time, the band and particularly Michael Steip decided to wear their hearts on their sleeves and to be more openly political than ever before."
**Shift to Major Label Warner Brothers (1988)** : Despite having achieved platinum status on the independent IRS Records, a label with limited resources, R.E.M. made the move to a major label in 1988, choosing Warner Brothers Records. Warner was renowned among mega-majors for being an "artist's first label" and was respected for its handling of adventurous acts such as Prince and Paul Simon. "Amazingly, REM were still on an independent label. They were now a platinum level band on IRS records, a label with limited resources. It was long past time for them to sign to the majors."
**Michael Stipe's Coming Out** : On his own terms, Michael Stipe eventually came out publicly, initially identifying as bisexual, and later, in his preferred words, as a "queer artist." "On his own terms, Michael Steip would also eventually come out. First as bisexual, then as in his own preferred words, a queer artist."
**Bill Berry's Departure (1997)** : Drummer Bill Berry left R.E.M. in 1997, yet the band continued recording for another decade and a half after his departure. "R.E.M. would continue recording for another decade and a half, sticking it out even after drummer Bill Berry left the band in 1997."
**Rock and Roll Hall of Fame Induction (2007)** : In 2007, R.E.M., with Bill Berry included as a core member, was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in their very first year of eligibility. Michael Stipe remarked on the occasion about the band's early aspirations, stating, "When we first put the band together, the only thing we really wanted to do was play New York City, and I would say that we've done it tonight." "In 2007, R.E.M. as a forcem, including Bill Berry, was inducted into the rock and roll hall of fame in their very first year of eligibility."

**Bouncing Off the Satellites (1986) - Post-Tragedy Release & Chart Underperformance** : Largely completed before Ricky Wilson's death in October 1985, this album was released in September 1986, almost a year after the tragedy. It became the B-52's worst-performing album, peaking at number 85 on the charts and failing to generate any radio hits. Despite its commercial struggles, the album, with its proudly campy side, found its warmest reception within the gay community, particularly in dance clubs. "When it finally reached record stores in September of 1986, the album would be the B-52's worst performer, peaking at number 85 on the charts and generating no radio hits."
**Cosmic Thing (1989) - Comeback and Quadruple Platinum Success** : Recorded in 1988 and released in 1989, this album marked the B-52s' major comeback after a period of hiatus and mourning following Ricky Wilson's death. Half of the album was produced by Nile Rodgers, while the other half was handled by Don Was. It became the group's best-performing album on the charts, reaching a new album chart peak of number 4 in March 1990 and eventually outselling R.E.M.'s *Green*, achieving quadruple platinum certification for sales of four million copies. "Cosmic Thing wound up being the title track of the B-50 Choo's fifth album, recorded in 1988 and released in 1989."
**Good Stuff (1992) - Trio Album Success** : Released in 1992 after Cindy Wilson took a hiatus from the band, this album was recorded as a trio. "Released in 1992, the album Good Stuff was a considerably smaller hit, but it did about as well on the charts as their early 80s albums, reaching the top 20 and going gold."

## songs
**Summer of Love (1986)** : Featured on the *Bouncing Off the Satellites* album, this track became a serious club hit, peaking at number 3 on Billboard's Club Play Chart. It highlighted the band's embrace by the gay community, particularly in dance clubs, despite the album's overall poor commercial performance. "The track Summer of Love was a serious club hit, peaking at number 3 on Billboard's Club Play Chart."
**Girl from Ipanema Goes to Greenland (1986)** : This single from *Bouncing Off the Satellites* featured a music video that received modest play on MTV. Musically, it demonstrated the group's adjustment to 80s techno pop, adeptly meshing their colorful kitsch and Kate Pierson's high-pitched vocals with pulsating synthesizers, and even included Ricky Wilson's angular guitar deep in the mix. Lyrically, it was an homage to Antonio Carlos Jobim's jazz classic "The Girl from Ipanema," though it sounded nothing like it. The song performed best in clubs, making it into the top 10 of Billboard's Club Play Chart. "The B-52's track received only modest MTV play. Again, the song did best in clubs, making the top 10 of Billboard's Club Play Chart."
**Cosmic (1988)** : This appropriately spacey song was recorded by the B-52s for the soundtrack of the forthcoming 1988 movie *Earth Girls Are Easy*. The track eventually became the title track for their fifth album, *Cosmic Thing*, released in 1989, and marked the first time the reunited band worked with producer Nile Rodgers. "The reunited B-50 Chooch, who wound up recording an appropriately spacey song for the film called Cosmic."
**Channel Z (1989)** : Released as the first official single from the *Cosmic Thing* album, this was the most overtly political song the B-52s had ever released, with Keith Strickland describing it as an environmental anthem. Its dystopian lyrics depicted a fictional radio station, the titular "Channel Z," promising "all static all day forever" in a world ravaged by corporate greed and human garbage. Produced by Don Was, the track featured a funky guitar riff played by Strickland that echoed both the late Ricky Wilson and their new collaborator Nile Rodgers. It topped the Modern Rock Chart for three weeks in the summer of 1989. "Channel Z was the first official single from the album Cosmic Thing and the most overtly political song the B-52s had ever released."
**Love Shack (1989)** : This song became the B-52s' biggest hit ever, described as the "ultimate party record" and a triumph after years following "Rock Lobster." It showcased the talents of every band member, with Kate Pierson, Cindy Wilson, and Fred Schneider all taking solo vocal lines, and Keith Strickland displaying his versatility with funky guitar licks as the band's new instrumental leader. The call-and-response lyrics incorporated influences from James Brown, Motown, Punk, New Wave, and Garage Rock, while proudly embracing the band's campy and all-embracing style. Its video featured a multicultural array of dancers, including a young drag performer named RuPaul in her first-ever appearance. The song rapidly ascended the charts, hitting the Modern Rock Top 10 in September 1989, topping that chart two weeks later for a month, becoming the B-52s' first-ever top 40 hit on the Hot 100 in late September, and peaking at number 3 just before Thanksgiving 1989. It rode the Hot 100 for 27 weeks and was certified gold. "Love Shack was not only the ultimate party record, it was a triumph, a payoff for all the years after Rock Lobster and a showcase for the talents of every member of the B-52s."
**Roam (1990)** : Released less than a month after "Love Shack" peaked, this song was another smash hit from the *Cosmic Thing* album, produced by Nile Rodgers and specifically showcasing the vocals of Kate Pierson and Cindy Wilson with a sound described as "big as the great outdoors." It achieved even greater success on the pop charts than on alternative radio, reaching number 6 on the Modern Rock Chart but matching "Love Shack's" peak position of number 3 on the Hot 100 in March 1990. "Rome was another smash, actually a bigger hit on the pop charts than at alternative radio."
**Deadbeat Club (1990)** : The final single released from the *Cosmic Thing* album, this was considered the most poignant of their hits, peaking at number 30 on the Hot 100 in the spring of 1990. The song served as an homage to Athens, Georgia, and the Bohemians, queer kids, and "deadbeats" the band fondly remembered from their days before they even formed. The music video featured a brief cameo appearance by Michael Stipe of R.E.M. In its verses, Kate and Cindy harmonized, offering wistful reminiscences of old friends and the joys of carefree youth. "Deadbeat Club, which peaked at number 30 on the hot 100 in the spring of 1990, was an homage to Athens, Georgia itself."
**Good Stuff (1992)** : The title track from the 1992 album *Good Stuff*, this song was a modest pop hit, reaching number 28 on the Hot 100. However, it achieved significant success on alternative radio, topping the Modern Rock Chart for four weeks. "The album's title track Good Stuff was only a modest pop hit, reaching number 28 on the Hot 100, but it topped the modern rock chart for four weeks."

## curiosities
**Formation and Early Sound (1977-1980)** : The B-52s formed in 1977 and were signed to a major label by 1979, achieving hit albums filled with "shimmying party tracks" by 1980. Their unique sound was a "retro-futurist form of new wave," characterized by a combination of three vocalists, a skeletal rhythm section, and the driving, quirky surf guitar of Ricky Wilson. "The B-52s formed in 1977. They were signed to a major label by 1979, and they were scoring hit albums full of shimmying party tracks by 1980."
**Mid-1980s Stagnation** : By the mid-1980s, the B-52s had yet to score a Top 40 hit or achieve a breakthrough on album rock radio, and had not released an album in over two years. Their albums typically reached the middle rungs of Billboard's album chart, indicating a plateau in their commercial success prior to the changes of the late 80s. "As for the B-52s, they had yet to score a Top 40 hit or break through at album rock radio. And by 1985, they had it issued an album in more than two years."
**Ricky Wilson's Death and Its Impact (1985)** : On October 12, 1985, Ricky Wilson, the band's lead guitarist and pioneer of their open tuning guitar sound, died at age 32 from complications related to AIDS, making him one of the disease's earliest famous victims, just ten days after Rock Hudson's passing. Wilson had been openly gay in his private life for over a decade and had come out to Keith Strickland in their teenage years, but his HIV positive status, diagnosed in 1983 during *Whammy* sessions, was a secret known only to Strickland, as Wilson preferred the band not to worry. His death devastated the band, with Cindy Wilson describing a "long catatonic state" and the band initially believing they couldn't continue without him. "Ricky Wilson's death shocked the band. Cindy Wilson said she went into a long catatonic state."
**Post-Ricky Wilson Hiatus & Lack of Touring (1986)** : Following Ricky Wilson's death, the band was so devastated that they declined to tour for their album *Bouncing Off the Satellites*, released in September 1986. This decision resulted in minimal promotion from Warner Brothers, causing the album to be off the charts in just 15 weeks, an even shorter run than their "ill-fated" *Mesopotamia* EP. The band would not record again for another two years, entering what was described as a "wilderness period" in the mid-80s. "But they were so devastated from the loss of Ricky Wilson that they declined to tour for the album, which consequently received minimal promotion from Warner Brothers."
**Decision to Reform (1988)** : In the three years following Ricky Wilson's death, the band went on an extended hiatus out of respect for him, with Cindy Wilson particularly mourning her brother. The ultimate decision to reform largely rested with Keith Strickland, Ricky's best friend since high school, who initially doubted their ability to continue without Ricky, given his crucial role as primary songwriter and often sole guitarist. Strickland eventually felt he "wanted to write some more music," which, coupled with the band's realization of "how precious life was," provided the catalyst for their return. "I didn't really think we were going to continue. We didn't feel like there was any way we could because he was so important to the band as a songwriter."
**Keith Strickland's Transition to Guitarist (1988)** : Following Ricky Wilson's death, drummer Keith Strickland, Ricky's best friend and collaborator on open tunings, stepped up to take over lead guitar duties, utilizing the unusual open tunings they had pioneered together in the 70s. For live performances and recordings, the band would hire session players to handle drums and other instruments, allowing Strickland to lead instrumentally. "Strickland, while an adept guitar player in his own right, had mostly been behind the drum kit for the band's first decade. He would have to step out and step up, taking over lead guitar, using the unusual open tunings he helped Ricky pioneer in the 70s."
**Pre-1988 Chart Performance** : Before the creation of the Modern Rock Chart in 1988, the B-52s had never scored a Top 40 hit on the Hot 100, with their handful of entries peaking in the charts' bottom half. Although they had some success in clubs and received early play on some rock stations, Billboard had never recorded any B-52s hits on its Album Rock Chart. "In all that time, the band had never scored a top 40 hit. Their handful of hot 100 hits had peaked in the charts bottom half."
**Kate Pierson's Collaboration with Iggy Pop (1990)** : In late 1990, Kate Pierson extended her vocal talents beyond the B-52s by recording a duet with punk legend Iggy Pop titled "Candy." This collaboration became Iggy Pop's only Top 40 hit ever, peaking at number 28 in early 1991. "Kate Pearson was especially in demand as a vocalist, even outside of the B-52's. In late 1990, she recorded a duet with punk legend Iggy Pop, candy that became his only top 40 hit ever, peaking at number 28 in early 1991."
**Cindy Wilson's Early 90s Hiatus** : Following the dizzying popularity and demands after the success of *Cosmic Thing*, Cindy Wilson took a hiatus from the B-52s in the early 1990s due to exhaustion. This led to their follow-up album, *Good Stuff*, being recorded as a trio. "Exhausted by the demands placed on them after their big comeback, Cindy Wilson took a hiatus from the band in the early 90s."
**Film and Soundtrack Appearances (1990s)** : After achieving significant success, the B-52s entered a long hiatus but continued with occasional soundtrack work, contributing a track to *The Rugrats Movie* in 1998. They also made a cameo appearance in the 1994 film *The Flintstones*, portraying a prehistoric rock band named The BC-52's. "The band did occasional soundtrack work, contributing a track to 1998's Rugrats movie, and even making a cameo appearance in the 1994 film The Flintstones, as prehistoric rock band The BC-52's."
**Members' Coming Out** : In their personal lives, three of the four remaining members of the B-52s eventually came out. Keith Strickland came out as gay in the early 1990s. "In their personal lives, three of the four remaining members eventually wound up coming out. Keith Strickland came out as gay in the early 90s"

# genre - Alternative Rock

## curiosities
**New Subculture and Billboard Chart** : The evolution of popular music in the mid-1980s saw the formation of a whole new alternative rock subculture. This growth was further solidified and tracked by Billboard's launch of a dedicated chart, the Modern Rock Chart, in September 1988. "The culture eventually came to them. And it was helped along by the formation of a whole new alternative rock subculture. And even a Billboard chart to track it."
**Sound of Alternative Radio (1988-1989)** : In 1988 and 1989, the sound prevalent on alternative radio was characterized as danceable, kitschy, and woman-friendly, distinct from the more male-fronted songs dominating mainstream rock stations. Examples include Siouxsie and the Banshees' "Peekaboo" and The Primitives' "Crash." **Athens, Georgia Music Scene** : Athens, Georgia, described as a college and farming town 60 miles outside of Atlanta, was a significant incubator for an eclectic variety of bands, particularly nurturing multiple branches of the post-punk family tree. It notably launched two of the biggest bands ever to emerge from the state, R.E.M. and The B-52s. "The college and farming town, 60 miles outside of Atlanta, launched an eclectic variety of bands, and it helped nurture multiple branches of the post-punk family tree."
**AIDS Impact on Music Community (1985)** : In 1985, AIDS was a disease still shrouded in secrecy and fear, referred to as the "disease that dare not speak its name." Ricky Wilson of The B-52s was one of its earliest famous victims, dying just ten days after Hollywood legend Rock Hudson, highlighting the devastating and often hidden impact the epidemic had on public figures and the wider community. "In 1985, AIDS was still the disease that dare not speak its name."
**Billboard's Modern Rock Tracks Chart (1988 Launch)** : Billboard Magazine launched this new chart in September 1988, marking its first-ever chart designed to track airplay at college radio stations, as well as certain commercial rock stations that were beginning to be identified as "Alternative." This chart was created to specifically track bands that, by and large, were considered too quirky for mainstream pop or album rock radio. "In September of 1988, Billboard Magazine launched a new chart called Modern Rock Tracks. It was the magazine's first-ever chart to track Airplay at College Radio, as well as certain commercial rock stations that were just starting to be called Alternative."
**Billboard's Album Rock Chart vs. Modern Rock Chart** : For most of the decade leading up to 1988, the Album Rock Chart tracked airplay at more mainstream rock stations. The introduction of the Modern Rock Chart in September 1988 provided a distinct tracking mechanism for college and "Alternative" rock stations, allowing bands previously considered too quirky for mainstream radio to gain chart recognition. "For most of the past decade, the album Rock Chart had track Airplay at more mainstream rock stations. On the Modern Rock Chart, the first-ever number one song was Peekaboo, a catchy golf pop song by Suzy and the Banshees."
**Agent Orange as a Song Reference** : Agent Orange, an herbicide chemical widely used during the Vietnam War, was referenced in R.E.M.'s song "Orange Crush." The chemical has been blamed for causing disease and death in countless soldiers, both Vietnamese and American, and Michael Stipe, having been raised in a military family, learned about its impact from his father who served in Vietnam. "Orange Crush was perhaps REM's toughest track to date, a reference to Agent Orange, an herbicide chemical used widely in the Vietnam War that has been blamed for the disease and death of countless soldiers, both Vietnamese and American."
**Producer Don Gehman's Influence** : Don Gehman produced R.E.M.'s 1986 album *Life's Rich Pageant*, which became the band's first gold album and a radio-friendly crossover. **Producer Nile Rodgers's Impact** : Nile Rodgers, co-founder of Chic with Bernard Edwards, created irresistible guitar lines and metronomic bass lines that were influential on a slew of rock bands. After Chic's hiatus in the early 80s, Rodgers established himself as a highly sought-after producer, shaping the sound of numerous artists including David Bowie, INXS, Duran Duran, Madonna, Laurie Anderson, and Depeche Mode, effectively defining the sound of new wave by fusing rock with disco rhythms. "Nile Rogers was the co-founder of Sheik. The and Sheik bandmate Bernard Edwards created irresistible guitar lines and metronomic bass lines that were adopted by a slew of rock bands."
**Producer Don Was's Diverse Work** : Don Was was an eclectic producer who was about to win a Grammy for producing Bonnie Raitt's 1989 Album of the Year, *Nick of Time*, when he worked with The B-52s on half of their *Cosmic Thing* album. As a pop musician, he was also the co-leader of the collective Was (Not Was), which was scoring its first top ten hit with "Walk the Dinosaur." Was was noted for his extensive collaborations with Black performers, complementing Nile Rodgers's work with white rock acts, making both ideally suited for The B-52s' blend of rock, pop, and funk. "He was about to win a Grammy as the producer of 1989's album of the year Nick of Time by Bonnie Raite."

**Comparison of Word Count in Songs** : Bob Dylan's 1965 song "Subterranean Homesick Blues" packed 322 words into two and a half minutes, inspiring R.E.M.'s "It's the End of the World as We Know It," which "out-Dylanned Dylan" by fitting 614 words into just over four minutes. In comparison, Billy Joel's 1989 hit "We Didn't Start the Fire" contained 518 words over a nearly minute-longer duration, making R.E.M.'s song remarkably verbose. "By that yardstick, REM may have out-dillened Dylan. It's the end of the world as we know it packs 614 words into just over 4 minutes, nearly twice as many as subterranean homesick blues."
