## albums
**Elvis Presley (1956) - Debut Album** : Presley's first album, which contained both "Don't Be Cruel" and "Hound Dog." It marked a pivotal moment in his career as a national star and emerging sex symbol.

## songs
**Hound Dog (1956)** : Presley's cover version was his third number one single, though it was initially the B-side to "Don't Be Cruel." It was recorded in the same session as "Don't Be Cruel," reportedly after Presley's management insisted he capture his live favorite in the studio, despite his initial reluctance. The song spent 11 weeks atop the bestsellers chart in 1956, a record that held for 36 years, and is enshrined in the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame as one of the 500 songs that shaped Rock and Roll. Presley began performing "Hound Dog" live after seeing Freddie Bell and the Bell Boys' comedic 1955 cover, which replaced the original's racy lyrics with a complaint to a literal canine. His teen audience, however, received it as dangerous, not comedy. On its single release in the summer of 1956, the song was prominently featured on the single cover, even more so than the A-side. "You ain't nothing but a hound dog. The cry of the time."
**Don't Be Cruel (1956)** : Initially positioned as the A-side of Presley's third number one single in 1956, this song was presented to him in the studio as a new composition by Brooklyn songwriter Otis Blackwell. Both "Don't Be Cruel" and its B-side, "Hound Dog," are now considered legendary. Rolling Stone ranks "Don't Be Cruel" among the 200 greatest singles in Rock and Roll. While "Hound Dog" became more "viral" due to TV appearances, "Don't Be Cruel" was the more popular record among radio DJs, topping the "Most Played by Jockeys" list for much of August through October 1956. "Don't Be Cruel. Do I know it's true? Bay of the info main."

"Of course given Elvis' status as a national star and emerging sex symbol, his teen girl audience didn't receive the song as comedy."
**TV Variety Show Appearances** : Elvis was frequently invited onto TV variety shows and asked to perform "Hound Dog." He performed it on The Milton Berle Show and, a month later, famously sang to a droopy basset hound on The Steve Allen Show. "When Elvis was invited onto TV Variety shows, he was asked to do Houndog, performing that hit on The Milton Burleshow."
**Single Cover Prominence** : The single cover for his 1956 release, which contained "Don't Be Cruel" and "Hound Dog," featured a still of Elvis singing to his "new canine friend" (from his Steve Allen Show performance) and listed "Hound Dog" more prominently than "Don't Be Cruel." "So popular was this TV version that, when the single was pressed in the summer of 56, the single cover featured a still of Elvis singing to his new canine friend."

# curiosity

**Loss of the B-side in the Digital Era** : The 21st-century transition from physical to digital music led to the conceptual loss of the B-side, which was historically a "filler" or "throwaway" track on the flip side of a single's prime A-side. This loss is considered one of the biggest for pop fans, alongside the communal joys of record stores and the educational value of liner notes. "But one of the biggest losses, not just as collector's items, but also conceptually as a way of framing how and why songs become hits, was the loss of the B-side."
**Definition of a Single's B-Side** : On a 7-inch 45 RPM single, the A-side contained the "prime time product" or "big radio hit," while the B-side was supposedly "filler," a "throwaway," or an "afterthought," recorded sometimes in minutes just to fill space. Despite this intention, many B-sides unexpectedly became hits throughout chart history. "On the 7-inch 45 RPM single, the A-side was where the prime time product went, the song you were pushing, the big radio hit, the B-side was supposedly filler, a throwaway, an afterthought."
**Billboard Chart Evolution (1956-1958)** : In August 1956, Billboard did not yet have the Hot 100 chart. Instead, it used separate charts for tracking "bestsellers in stores," "most played in jukeboxes," and "most played by Jockeys." The "bestsellers" chart was considered the flagship chart before the Hot 100 was launched in 1958. "A time when Billboard didn't even have a hot 100 yet, but the King of Rock and Roll was dominating what was then called the best sellers in stores chart."
**Billboard Hot 100 Charting Policy (Mid-1960s)** : As of the mid-1960s, Billboard Hot 100 rules stated that A-sides and B-sides of singles charted in tandem but separately. If a B-side garnered enough reports from radio programmers, it would chart in its own slot, distinct from its A-side. "On the Billboard Hot 100, as of the mid 60s, the rule was that A sides and B sides of singles charted in tandem, but separately."
**Billboard Hot 100 Data Measurement** : The core methodology of Billboard's Hot 100 has always been to measure at least two primary data points: radio airplay and sales of singles. These two pools of data are averaged to rank the biggest hits in the USA, and generally, the biggest sellers align with the most played records. "the secret sauce of billboards hot 100 is that it has always measured at least two things radio, airplay and sales of singles billboard averages these two pools of data to rank the biggest hits in the USA and most of the time the biggest sellers in the most played records are aligned with each other."

# curiosity
**Billboard B-Side Policy Changes (1950s-1990s)** : Billboard's policy regarding B-sides changed roughly every decade or two from the 1950s through the 1990s, often quietly. This created definitional problems for charting, as record buyers purchased two songs while radio could only play one. The magazine struggled with whether to combine A and B sides into a single chart position (UK style) or track them separately, leading to confusion and policy reversals, notably in late 1969. "billboard couldn't make up its mind either the magazine changed its B side policy roughly every decade or two sometimes quietly."
**Impact of 1969 Billboard Rule Change** : At the end of 1969, Billboard changed its policy to combine A and B sides of 45s into a single Hot 100 position. This rule change, which directly benefited The Beatles, led to a "flurry of two-sided hits" once the music business realized both sides of a single could boost chart fortunes. "For reasons that are lost to history billboard decided at the end of the 1969 chart year to begin combining A and B sides of 45s into a single hot 100 position."
**1980s B-Side Chart Exclusion** : From the summer of 1984 to around 1987, B-sides were essentially barred from the Hot 100. This wasn't due to an explicit policy, but because payola-fueled 80s pop radio stations were not reporting them to Billboard if the label indicated it wasn't the A-side, regardless of how popular the B-side was. "In the Incerum, B sides were essentially barred from the chart, not out of any explicit policy, but because payola fueled 80s pop radio stations were not reporting them to the charts, no matter how massive those B sides were."
**1998 Billboard Rule Change - Non-Retail Hits** : By the end of 1998, Billboard changed its Hot 100 policies again, for the first time adding non-retail hits (e.g., airplay) to the pool. This change converted the Hot 100 to a chart where only one song could occupy any single position at any time, thus preventing future chart longevity feats like Jewel's extended run. "By the end of 1998, Billboard changed hot 100 policies again, adding non-retail hits to the pool for the first time ever."
**Digital Era - All Songs as Standalone Singles (2000s)** : The invention of downloadable music in the early 2000s fundamentally changed the concept of A and B sides, as all songs became purchasable individually ("a la carte"). This effectively eliminated the "free B side" model, ensuring that only one song could occupy each chart slot, and transforming all songs into standalone singles. "the invention of downloadable music in the early aughts meant that all songs were purchasable all a card, one song at a time."
**Digital Era - Double A-sides on Steroids (2000s)** : Despite the shift to digital, the music business still employs strategies akin to double A-sides. Superstar artists, including Taylor Swift, Lil Wayne, The Weeknd, and Ed Sheeran, often release "floatillas of early singles" from new albums, frequently dropping two key tracks simultaneously. This serves as a "live focused group" to determine which song becomes the "stickiest hit." "Often, two key tracks will drop together, a kind of double A side on digital steroids."
**The Guesswork of Hit Making** : Despite extensive industry experience and the "pre-sold" status of artists like Elvis, The Beatles, Prince, Madonna, and Beyoncé, hit making remains largely a matter of guesswork. Labels often underestimate the popularity of certain tracks (B-sides or non-prioritized songs) while overestimating others, demonstrating the unpredictable nature of popular culture. "so much of hit making remains guesswork."
**William Goldman's "Nobody Knows Anything" Quote** : Screenwriter William Goldman's immortal line from his Hollywood memoir, *Adventures in the Screen Trade*, states, "nobody knows anything. Not one person in the entire motion picture feel knows for a certainty what's going to work. Every time out, it's a guess and if you're lucky and educated one." This quote, when applied to popular music, illustrates the weekly unpredictability of the charts, where even superstar singles can "crash and burn." "quote, nobody knows anything.
