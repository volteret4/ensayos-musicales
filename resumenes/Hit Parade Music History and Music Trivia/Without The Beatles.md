# artist - Peter and Gordon

## members

## labels

## songs
**A World Without Love (1964)** : This song was a Lennon-McCartney composition, but not by The Beatles. It was penned by Paul McCartney, who wrote it by himself as a teenager and deemed it unworthy of The Beatles, with John Lennon particularly disliking it. It was even rejected by fellow Liverpool musician Billy J. Kramer. Peter Asher, whose sister Jane Asher was dating Paul McCartney, asked McCartney if he and Gordon Waller could have the song, and Paul finished writing it for them by adding its missing bridge. The duo brought it to producer Norman Newell, who intentionally mimicked the popular Liverpool or Mersey sound. The song featured chiming guitars, dewy vocal harmonies, and a Hammond organ bridge. It topped the Billboard Hot 100 in late June 1964, spending eight weeks in the Top 10 and filling a market gap when The Beatles had no new product due to filming "A Hard Day's Night." "a world without love sounded instantly familiar. The chiming guitars and dewey vocal harmonies read as an unabashed fab-for pastiche."
**Nobody I Know (1964)** : This McCartney-penned song was another "Beatles-like" track that Peter and Gordon took to the Top 20, reaching number 12 before the end of 1964. "Before 1964 was over, Peter and Gordon returned to the Top 20 with two more songs written by McCartney."
**I Don't Want to See You Again (1964)** : Also written by Paul McCartney, this song reached number 20 in 1964, further capitalizing on Peter Asher's connection to McCartney and the Beatles' sound. "Both of which were similarly Beatles' songs."

## curiosities
**Connection to Paul McCartney** : Peter Asher's sister, Jane Asher, began dating Paul McCartney in April 1963, leading McCartney to live in the Asher family home in London for several years, treating it as his own. This close relationship was highly beneficial for Peter's pop duo, Peter and Gordon, as McCartney supplied them with songs. "McCartney's close relationship with the Asher's was very helpful to Peter."
**"Fab Four Reject" Song** : Their number one hit, "A World Without Love," was not technically a Beatles cover but a song Paul McCartney had written and the Beatles deemed unworthy of release, with John Lennon particularly disliking it. This allowed the previously unknown duo to record a smash. "Peter and Gordon's number one song wasn't technically a Beatles cover. It was a Fab Four reject."
**Beatles Sound Pastiche** : Peter and Gordon, along with producer Norman Newell, deliberately emulated The Beatles' "Mersey sound" for "A World Without Love." They incorporated elements like chiming guitars, dewy vocal harmonies, and a Hammond organ bridge, which The Beatles themselves used in "Mr. Moonlight" that same year. "Even for those hearing the song for the first time in 1964, a world without love sounded instantly familiar."
**Market Gap Success** : "A World Without Love" reached number one precisely when Beatlemania was cresting but The Beatles themselves had no new singles, being occupied with filming "A Hard Day's Night." This allowed Peter and Gordon to "fill a market gap" and satisfy a Beatles-hungry public, after which they "passed the baton back" to The Fab Four. "This is when Peter and Gordon's single entered the Top 10 and, to borrow a term from economics, filled a market gap."
**Disbandment and Continued Connection** : Peter and Gordon disbanded in 1968, the same year Paul McCartney and Jane Asher broke up. However, Peter Asher's relationship with The Beatles continued as he was named head of artists and repertoire (A&R) for their Apple Records label and recruited James Taylor that same year. "But Peter's relationship with the Beatles continued."

"Shooting a hard day's night kept the Beatles out of the studio for most of the spring of 1964."

## curiosities
**Lennon-McCartney Songwriting Agreement** : Throughout the 1960s, any song written by either John Lennon or Paul McCartney was published under the joint songwriting entity Lennon-McCartney, even if one wrote it entirely alone or with minimal input from the other. This agreement meant "A World Without Love," solely penned by McCartney, bore the joint credit. "Despite the absence of John Lennon and the other two Beatles yesterday, like all songs written by either John or Paul in the 1960s, was published under the songwriting entity, Lennon McCartney."
**Mid-1964 Market Gap** : The Beatles' absence from the studio for most of the spring of 1964, while filming "A Hard Day's Night," created a rare interlude in their chart dominance. During this period, their singles began slipping, and a "market gap" was filled by Peter and Gordon's "A World Without Love," which became a number one hit. "This is when Peter and Gordon's single entered the Top 10 and, to borrow a term from economics, filled a market gap."
**Apple Records A&R** : When The Beatles formed their Apple Records label in 1968, Peter Asher (of Peter and Gordon) was appointed head of Artists and Repertoire (A&R), and in this role, he recruited a young James Taylor to the label. "When the group formed its Apple Records label that year, Peter Asher was named head of artists and repertoire or ANR, and he recruited a young James Taylor to Apple that same year."
**Challenge for Song Interpreters** : Due to The Beatles' immense popularity, self-contained songwriting, and meticulous production (often with George Martin), other artists found it challenging and perilous to cover their songs. The public repeatedly showed a preference for The Beatles' originals, explaining why only three Lennon-McCartney covers ever reached number one on the Hot 100. "Once the Fab Four had laid down their George Martin produced carefully crafted versions, other artists approached these songs at their peril."
**"Fab Forest" Catalog Singularity** : The Beatles' catalog holds a unique place in the public imagination, making it extremely difficult for other musicians to cover their songs and achieve the same level of chart success as the originals. The quirky nature of the three non-Beatles Lennon-McCartney number ones reinforces this. "The fact that all three are curiose, even the one by Elton John is a bit quirky, tells us something about the singular place the Fab Forest catalog holds in the public imagination."

## songs
**Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds (1975)** : Elton John's cover was the first hit version of this John Lennon-penned Beatles track from "Sergeant Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band." Lennon provided backing vocals and guitar under the pseudonym "Dr. Winston O'Bregge." While the original had a reputation as a drug anthem, Elton's version turned whimsical, playing into his "glittery, cuddly, Captain Fantastic persona." It includes a "head scratching reggae version of the chorus" with audible John Lennon "plinky white boy toasting," further establishing it as a novelty. The song reached number one in early January 1975, just seven weeks after Lennon's "Whatever Gets You Through the Night." However, it had a remarkably quick burn on the charts, staying on the Hot 100 for only 14 weeks. "But in Elton's hands, Lucy turns whimsical. It plays off of and boosts Elton's glittery, cuddly, captain fantastic persona."
**Whatever Gets You Through the Night (1974)** : Elton John played piano and sang harmony vocals on this "abulliant track" written by John Lennon. Elton was so confident it would be Lennon's first solo number one that he bet Lennon on it, making him promise to join Elton on stage if it topped the charts. It did indeed become Lennon's first solo number one on the Hot 100 in November 1974. "Elton played piano and sang harmony vocals on an abulliant track that Lennon had written called Whatever gets you through the night."
**Philadelphia Freedom (1975)** : This song was a non-album single, released immediately after "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds," and also became a number one hit. "Philadelphia Freedom" spent 21 weeks on the Hot 100, significantly longer than "Lucy," and is played about six times as frequently on Oldies Radio as "Lucy" today. "Lucy was issued as a non-album single and it was followed immediately by another one off number one hit, the smash Philadelphia Freedom."
**Empty Garden (1981)** : Six years after John Lennon's death, Elton John released this "more somber, elijayak tribute" to his friend in 1981, marking a different kind of remembrance than his earlier quirky collaboration. "Elton gave his friend a more somber, elijayak tribute with his 1981 hit Empty Garden."

## curiosities
**Friendship and Collaboration with John Lennon** : Elton John met John Lennon in the summer of 1974 during Lennon's "Lost Weekend" period. Their friendship led to a collaboration where Elton played on Lennon's "Whatever Gets You Through the Night," and Lennon contributed to Elton's cover of "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds," even appearing live with Elton. "Elton Jean met John Lennon at a great time for Elton's career and a strange moment for Lennon."
**Bet with John Lennon** : Elton John was convinced that John Lennon's song "Whatever Gets You Through the Night," on which Elton played, would be Lennon's first solo chart-topper. He bet Lennon it would reach number one and made him promise to perform on stage with him if it did. The song did top the charts, and Lennon made good on the bet. "Elton was so confident in fact that he bet Lennon it would go to number one."
**Madison Square Garden Appearance (1974)** : As a result of a bet with Elton John, John Lennon made a surprise appearance at an Elton John show at Madison Square Garden on Thanksgiving in 1974. This performance, occurring one year before Lennon's recording sabbatical and six years before his assassination, sadly turned out to be Lennon's last live appearance. "And the former Beatles made good on the bet by appearing at an Elton Jean show at Madison Square Garden on Thanksgiving in 1974."
**"Imperial Phase" Success** : Elton John was at the "apex of his imperial phase" in the mid-1970s, which explains the existence and success of his cover of "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds." His enormous popularity and string of hits allowed him to take creative liberties that resonated with the public. "The fact that Elton was at the apex of his imperial phase explains why this Lucy cover even exists."
**"Good Housekeeping Seal of Approval"** : Elton John's public "bromance" with John Lennon acted as a "good housekeeping seal of approval" for his cover of "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds," lending authenticity and excitement to the track despite its novelty elements. "Elton's bromance with John Lennon was a kind of good housekeeping seal of approval on his cover of Lucy."

## songs
**Medley Intro Venus, Sugar Sugar, No Reply, I'll Be Back, Drive My Car, Do You Want to Know a Secret?, We Can Work It Out, I Should Have Known Better, Nowhere Man, You're Going to Lose That Girl, Stars on 45 (1981)** : This track was a disco medley of mostly Beatles songs, created by Dutch studio musicians (Bach Mayes, Hans Vermuleb, Opie Hoistens) trying to sound like The Beatles. It originated from an unauthorized French Canadian DJ bootleg. Willem Van Kuten legitimized the idea, commissioning Yop Egermont and Martin Duser to re-record the segments using sound-alike artists. The medley includes snippets of The Shocking Blue's "Venus" and The Archies' "Sugar Sugar," along with eight Beatles songs, all spliced together "analog style" over an "insidious, infectious clap track." The intro, an original melody, features pigeon-English lyrics that give the song its name and command dancers to "boogie like disco" but "not to forget the oldies." It topped the Dutch charts in February 1981, achieved number one or two positions internationally, and reached number one in America, where it was issued with a 41-word title. "The record is, well, tacky, but also admirably guileless."

## curiosities
**Origin from Bootleg Mix** : The concept for Stars on 45 originated from an unauthorized 12-inch dance single called "Let's Do It in the 80s Great Hits" by French Canadian DJs Michel Gendro and Paul Recher. This bootleg blended current disco tracks with older songs, including original recordings of Beatles songs, all mixed over a clapping beat. "What was remarkable about Let's Do It in the 80s Great Hits was that it lurched from very current Disco tracks by the likes of the Gap Band or Lips Incorporated to songs that were at least a decade old, all mixed over a clapping beat."
**Willem Van Kuten's Inspiration** : Willem Van Kuten, owner of the copyright to The Shocking Blue's "Venus," discovered the unauthorized DJ 12-inch containing his song and a long Beatles medley. Rather than suppressing it, he was inspired to create a legitimate, authorized medley using "sound-like artists" to replicate the original hits. "Van Kouten was more charmed than angered by the unlicensed Montreal 12-inch record, and he was also inspired, particularly by the long Beatles medley in the middle of the record."
**Dutch Studio Musicians** : Van Kuten enlisted Yop Egermont and Martin Duser, who recruited established Dutch singers for the project. Bach Mayes (from Smile) provided a John Lennon impression, Hans Vermuleb (from Sandy Coast) took on Paul McCartney's vocals, and Opie Hoistens (from Rainbow Train) was a "passable" George Harrison. "These singers impersonations of the Fab Four were not precise, but fairly respectable."
**Analog Splicing and Clap Track** : The re-recorded segments of various songs were spliced together using analog techniques and combined with an "unremittingly chipper clap track" that distinctively dated the song to 1980, reflecting the disco era's production style. "The tracks that Egermont's team re-recorded were then spliced together, analog style, against an unremittingly chipper clap track."
**"Unhip Homage to 70s-Style Disco"** : The song's intro, an original melody, explicitly instructed listeners to "boogie like disco" while also commanding them "not to forget the oldies," creating a surreal mashup of late disco-era production with classic 60s songs. "What is amusing about stars on 45, however, is its unhipp-omage to 70s-style disco."
**Two Versions and Global Success** : Egermont and Duser initially created a nearly 10-minute version of Stars on 45, but Dutch DJs preferred the four-minute Beatles segment. In response, a tighter, sub-five-minute mix focused on the Beatles segment was released on a 45 RPM single, topping Dutch charts in February 1981 and achieving number one or two in England, Australia, Germany, and Spain before its US release. "Dutch DJs began to focus on the four-minute Beatles segment. In response, Egermont and Duser came up with a tighter, sub-five-minute mix that focused on the Beatles segment and fit on one side of a 45 RPM single."
**Longest Song Title on Billboard Hot 100** : For its US release, a subsidiary of Atlantic Records called Radio Records, fearful of litigation, issued the song with a 41-word title listing every covered song. This remains the longest title of a hit in Billboard Hot 100 history to reach any position, let alone number one. "This remains the longest title of a hit in Billboard Hot 100 history in any position, let alone number one."
**Interruption of Kim Carnes' Reign** : "Stars on 45's Medley" improbably reached number one in June 1981, briefly interrupting Kim Carnes' "Bette Davis Eyes," which was shaping up to be the Song of the Summer and Top Hit of the Year, spending five weeks at number one before being displaced, then returning for another four weeks. "In between, it was interrupted by this crazy collection of Dutch beetle imitators."

## songs

## curiosities
**Medley Craze Catalyst** : Stars on 45's popularity was so significant that it sparked a widespread medley craze in the music industry. Over the subsequent 18 months, numerous kitschy medleys flooded the charts, with some even mimicking Stars on 45's signature relentless clap beat. This phenomenon showcased the industry's tendency to imitate successful ideas. "Indeed, Stars on 45 proved so popular it even spawned a FAD of its own, a medley craze."
**Unique Timing Jackpot** : The producers of Stars on 45 achieved significant chart success due to exceptionally fortuitous timing, specifically coinciding with the global outpouring of grief following John Lennon's assassination in December 1980, and the concurrent aerobics craze in America in 1981. This unique convergence of events allowed their medley, recorded months prior, to resonate deeply with the public. "And the stars on 45 producers hit the ultimate timing jackpot in the saddest way possible."
**Post-Craze Decline** : By the summer of 1982, follow-up medleys released by Stars on 45, as well as by Meco, failed to chart on the Hot 100, signaling the clear end of the medley craze. Despite this, Stars on 45 had already secured a follow-up hit, preventing them from being classified as a one-hit wonder. "By the summer of 1982, follow-up medley's by both Meco and the Stars on 45 missed the hot 100, and it was clear the medley craze was done."
**Chinsy Clap Beat Deftness** : Compared to medleys quickly assembled by other artists like The Beach Boys and The Beatles to capitalize on the craze, Stars on 45's distinctive "chinsy sounding clap beat" appeared more skillfully executed. This was primarily because other medleys often lacked a unified beat, leading to awkward and jarring song-to-song juxtapositions. "They actually made Stars on 45's chinsy sounding clap beat seem a bit more deft."

**Lennon/McCartney Number One Hits Rarity (1981)** : Stars on 45's hit in 1981 marked only the third, and to date, last single featuring John Lennon and Paul McCartney songwriting credits to ever top the Hot 100. This highlights a surprising rarity of number one hits directly attributed to their joint songwriting, despite The Beatles' immense legacy. **Disco Backlash Context (1981)** : By 1981, two years after disco's peak, the mirror ball exploding fever pitch of the disco backlash was in full force. This intense anti-disco sentiment made the success of a disco-influenced track like Stars on 45, which even mentioned disco in its opening line, particularly surprising and challenging. "Not to mention the fact that Stars on 45 reached number one in 1981, two years after Disco's peak, the mirror ball exploding fever pitch of the disco backlash was in full force."
**Wistful Whimsical Beatles Nostalgia (1981)** : Following John Lennon's assassination in December 1980, the initial somber global grief transitioned by 1981 into a more "wistful whimsical Beatles nostalgia." This shift in public sentiment, even embraced by former Beatles, created a cultural context that favored nostalgic reinterpretations of Lennon's legacy and contributed to the success of songs like Stars on 45 and George Harrison's tribute. "By 1981, however, the memorials turned more toward wistful whimsical Beatles nostalgia, even by former Beatles themselves."
**Aerobics Craze (1981)** : Fueled by Jane Fonda's 1981 book *Workout*, the aerobics craze became a dominant cultural phenomenon in America by mid-1981, even appearing on the cover of Time Magazine. This nationwide fitness trend created a perfect environment for up-tempo, catchy music with a strong beat, like Stars on 45, allowing it to resonate deeply with a broad demographic of record buyers. "Fueled by the release of Jane Fonda's 1981 book, Workout, by mid-81, The Aerobics Cray's was on the cover of Time Magazine, and dominating American culture."
**Public's Grief Baton Pass (1981)** : In the spring of 1981, as John Lennon's final single "Watching the Wheels" peaked at number ten, the Stars on 45 medley simultaneously broke into the top ten. This parallel chart success indicated that the public was symbolically "passing the baton" from Lennon himself to other musical acts that could help them process their grief and nostalgia for his legacy. "In effect, the public was passing the baton from John Lennon himself to other acts that could help them grieve."
**Medley Craze Phenomenon (1981-1982)** : The immense popularity of Stars on 45 triggered a widespread "medley craze" across the music industry, with numerous kitschy medleys hitting the charts over 18 months in 1981 and 1982. This trend saw various artists, from the Royal Philharmonic Orchestra to the estates of deceased legends and even The Beach Boys and The Beatles themselves, releasing their own medley compilations. "Indeed, Stars on 45 proved so popular it even spawned a FAD of its own, a medley craze."
**Hit Making Rules: Timing** : The success of various songs, particularly those with Lennon/McCartney connections, is attributed to precise timing rather than inherent musical qualities. Whether it was Peter and Gordon finding a niche for a Beatles-like sound, Elton John riding his own peak with a Lennon cover, or Stars on 45 benefiting from Lennon's death and the aerobics craze, timing proved to be the crucial factor in achieving chart success. "If the first three rules of real estate are location, location, location, the first three rules of hit making are timing, timing, timing."
**Rarity of Modern Lennon/McCartney Hits (2016)** : Despite The Beatles' enduring popularity nearly 50 years after their breakup, there have been remarkably few new chart hits directly featuring Lennon/McCartney songwriting credits. The last top 10 cover of a Beatles song was Tiffany's "I Saw Him Standing There" in 1988, and a 2016 number one hit by Rae Sremmurd, "Black Beatles," is explicitly stated as not being a Lennon/McCartney composition. "As popular as the Beatles continue to be nearly 50 years after their breakup, there have been remarkably few Lennon McCartney hits."
