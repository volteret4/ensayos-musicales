# artist - Slade

## members
Jimmy Lea (bassist)
Noddy Holder (vocalist)

## genres
Glam Rock

## songs
**Come On Feel the Noize (1973)** : This March 1973 UK number one smash was a loud, brash, and catchy glam rock anthem. Slade were known for misspelling their song titles, and this one was spelled "Cum On Feel The Noize" (C-U-M, Noize with a Z). It was later covered by the 80s hair metal band Quiet Riot, making it familiar to some American audiences. "Slades March 1973 UK number one smash come on feel the noise."

**Squeeze Me, Please Me (1973)** : A number one follow-up to "Come On Feel the Noize" in 1973, this song was described as possibly even louder. Consistent with Slade's quirky style, half of its title was misspelled with K's, double U's, and Z's, similar to their previous hit. "Slades number one follow up, squeeze me, please me, was possibly even louder."

**Merry Xmas Everybody (1973)** : This holiday classic reached the top of the United Kingdom's official charts company Singles Chart on December 15, 1973, becoming a quintessentially British hit. The song's origin stems from bassist Jimmy Lea being challenged by his mother-in-law in 1973 to write a song that sold better than Bing Crosby's "White Christmas." Lea combined a verse melody from his shower with an old chorus melody vocalist Noddy Holder had from 1967, and Holder completed the lyrics with a hopeful message for recession-hit Britain. Despite being a Christmas song, it lacks traditional Christmas sonic signifiers like chiming bells or children's choruses, and notably ends with a heavy metal scream. It debuted at number one and outsold Wizzard's competing Christmas single by hundreds of thousands of copies, making it a perennial UK holiday classic that has never appeared on the US Hot 100. "Merry Christmas everybody. debuted on the UK chart all the way up at number one."

## curiosities
**US Market Performance** : Slade never broke big in America, with their only top 20 hit being "Run Run Away" in 1984. In contrast, they were megastars in their home country a decade earlier during the height of glam rock, scoring half a dozen UK number one hits from 1971 to 1973. "They never broke big here."

**Song Title Misspellings** : Slade frequently misspelled the titles of their songs, a characteristic element of their identity. Examples include "Cum On Feel The Noize" (with C-U-M and a Z for noise) and "Squeeze Me, Please Me" (with K's, double U's, and Z's). This practice was also applied to their Christmas hit, "Merry Xmas Everybody." "Slayed like to misspell the titles to their songs."

**Origin of Merry Xmas Everybody** : The band's creation of "Merry Xmas Everybody" was surprisingly indirect, sparked by bassist Jimmy Lea's mother-in-law in 1973 who sarcastically suggested he write something like Bing Crosby's "White Christmas" because it sold better than any of Slade's hits. Annoyed, Lea combined existing melodic ideas with Noddy Holder's lyrics, which aimed to uplift Britain amidst a recession and miner strike. "if one band member's mother-in-law hadn't said something snippy, they might never have written Merry Christmas everybody."

# curiosity

**UK Pop Chart Following** : In the United Kingdom, pop charts are followed more avidly than in any other country in the world. A larger proportion of British citizens feel a more proprietary ownership of their pop charts compared to Americans with their Billboard charts. This intense interest peaks particularly around the holidays, where the Christmas number one competition has become an annual national pastime. "in no country in the world are the pop charts followed so avidly as they are in Great Britain."

**UK Christmas Number One Tradition** : For 45 years (as of the context of the transcript), England has made a sport of predicting and influencing what song will top the charts the week of Christmas. This competition generates wagers in pubs, bookies, and betting parlors, and is extensively covered in UK newspapers, on television, and on BBC Radio 1 in the run-up to the holiday. The tradition originated organically in the early 1970s when two UK rock bands, Slade and Wizzard, simultaneously issued holiday singles to cheer up a recessionary Great Britain, unintentionally starting the fierce annual contest. "For 45 years now, all of England, I mean the whole country, not just chart-obsessed music nerds like me, has made a sport of what song would top the charts the week of Christmas."

**Nature of UK Christmas Number One Winners** : While some UK Christmas number ones are predictable best-selling Christmas songs, just as often the victor is a straight-up pop blockbuster that simply peaks at the right time. Cover songs are notoriously successful, and there have been "freak years" where an unlikely novelty song takes the crown, only to be rarely heard from again outside of commemorative shows. The "flukiness" of the UK chart, driven purely by sales for much of its history, is a key factor in this phenomenon. "often the winner is a fluke."

**UK Chart Mechanics vs. US Hot 100** : For most of its history, the British chart measured only single sales, with radio airplay never factored in until streaming was added in the 2010s. In contrast, the US Hot 100 has always been a hybrid chart combining sales and radio airplay, with radio acting as a tempering force. This fundamental difference makes it much more difficult for novelty or seasonal singles to top the US charts, leading to the unique "flukiness" of the UK Christmas number one. "For most of its history, the British chart has only measured one thing, sales of singles."

**Christmas as a Peak Sales Week in UK** : In England, which notably does not celebrate Thanksgiving and therefore lacks a Black Friday, the week leading up to Christmas has historically been the top music sales week of the year in old-school record retailers. This concentrated period of high sales, combined with the UK chart's sales-only metric, allowed songs timed right for Christmas to debut straight in at number one. "if that single was timed right for Christmas, which in the age of the old school record retailer, was often the top music sales week of the year in England."

**Cultural Impact of Christmas Number One** : The UK Christmas number one contest has evolved into a national treasure and an inside joke, even inspiring the funniest subplot in "Love Actually," the top-grossing British holiday movie of all time. This demonstrates its deep cultural entrenchment and broad appeal beyond just music fans. "The whole contest has become such a national treasure slash in joke."

**Emergence of Deliberate Competition (1970s-1980s)** : After the initial 1973 Slade vs. Wizzard competition, patterns emerged for UK Christmas number ones through the rest of the 1970s. Hits didn't always have to be about Christmas but often benefited from being "grand or sentimental." By the 1980s, the contest was firmly established, watched closely by the Fleet Street press, with higher stakes and a mix of well-established acts, novelty records, and even school choirs taking the crown. "By the 1980s the pattern was set the UK Christmas number one was a thing the contest was watched closely by the Fleet Street press and the stakes grew higher the types of winners would differ from year to year."

**1984 as a "Thunderdome" Competition** : The year 1984 marked a pivotal moment when the UK public recognized the Christmas number one had become a "thunderdome level competition." Three epic singles—Frankie Goes to Hollywood's "The Power of Love," Wham!'s "Last Christmas," and Band Aid's "Do They Know It's Christmas?"—went head-to-head. Band Aid's eventual victory, blocking both Frankie and Wham!, cemented the perception of the Christmas number one as a "blood sport." **The X-Factor - UK Christmas Number One Dominance (2005-2008)** : The X-Factor, a British reality competition show created by Simon Cowell and premiered in fall 2004, established a pattern where its winner's debut single was intended to be released for Christmas and consistently achieved the UK Christmas number one. For four consecutive years, starting with Shane Ward in 2005, followed by Leon Lewis (2006), Leon Jackson (2007), and Alexandra Burke (2008), X-Factor winners dominated this chart position without suspense. "Cowell intended for each winner's debut single to be released just in time for Christmas."
**X-Factor Christmas Singles - Characteristics** : The X-Factor Christmas number one singles were typically "gushy, faux modest, secretly triumphant, inspirational reality show Pablo" with titles like "A Moment Like This" and "When You Believe." Notably, none of these songs were actual Christmas records, although this wasn't unprecedented for UK Christmas number ones. The predictability of these singles was seen as having "sucked the last remaining bits of fun out of the Christmas number one." "They were all gushy, faux modest, secretly triumphant, inspirational reality show Pablo with titles like A Moment Like This and When You Believe."
**Public Sentiment Against X-Factor Dominance** : The predictable and "smug success" of Simon Cowell's protégés topping the UK Christmas charts for four consecutive years began to "rank the British public." This widespread discontent fueled campaigns like John Mortar's, which aimed to disrupt The X-Factor's monopoly and bring back the traditional "fun" of the annual chart competition. "The predictability and the smug success of Simon Cowell whose latest protege could be faithfully relied upon to top the charts began to rank the British public."
**Digital Downloads Added to UK Chart (2005)** : The invention of the commercial digital download in the early 2000s, pioneered by Apple's Steve Jobs and iTunes, revolutionized music acquisition. By 2005, digital download sales were added to the Official Charts Company (OCC) chart, marking the first truly new data stream on the UK chart in roughly half a century. This frictionless and massive new method of "voting" for a hit fundamentally changed the dynamics of the British hit parade. "By 2005, digital download sales had been added to the chart, the first truly new data stream on the UK chart in roughly half a century."
**John Mortar's Christmas Number One Campaigns** : John Mortar, a high-fi technician from Essex, England, initiated a series of campaigns to challenge The X-Factor's dominance of the UK Christmas number one spot. In 2009, he successfully led a campaign for Rage Against the Machine's "Killing In The Name," which achieved number one by motivating over 500,000 downloads, partly by adding an altruistic element for the homeless charity Shelter. "And it was all started by one man on his laptop named John Mortar."
