## albums
**Weird Al Yankovic in 3-D (1984)** : Al's first LP to crack the top 40, this album, like its lead single "Eat It," was certified gold. In April 1984, it reached its peak of number 17 on the album chart, notably one position higher than The Police's blockbuster 1983 album *Synchronicity*. "Weird Al Yankovic in 3D, Al's first LP to crack the top 40."
**Dare to Be Stupid (1985)** : This album achieved gold certification within six months of its release. It featured "deep cuts" and "style parodies," such as the Devo homage "Dare to Be Stupid." "The album, Dare to Be Stupid, was gold within six months."
**Even Worse (1988)** : This LP reached the top 30 on the album charts and was certified gold. "Al's LP even worse hit the top 30 and went gold."
**UHF soundtrack (1989)** : This soundtrack album underperformed on the charts following the box office failure of Al's film *UHF*, which was hyped by Orion Pictures as a potential summer blockbuster but flopped. "the UHF soundtrack had underperformed on the charts."
**Running with Scissors (1999)** : This album achieved gold status and eventually went platinum, largely fueled by Al's Star Wars homage, "The Saga Begins," which was set to the tune of Don McLean's "American Pie." "1999's Running with Scissors went gold and eventually platinum."
**Straight Outta Lynwood (2006)** : Driven by the viral video for "White & Nerdy," this album became Weird Al's first ever top 10 hit album, debuting at number 10. "His 2006 album, Straight Out of Linwood, had been his first ever top ten hit, driven by the video for White and Nerdy."
**Alpocalypse (2011)** : As a follow-up to *Straight Outta Lynwood*, this album debuted at number nine on the charts, a bit higher than its predecessor. Al commissioned videos for every track on the album, including his Lady Gaga parody "Perform This Way," as part of his multi-single, multi-video strategy. "Al Pockelips debuted at number nine on the charts, a bit higher than Straight Out of Linwood."
**Mandatory Fun (2014)** : This album debuted at number 1 on the Billboard 200 album chart, marking the first number 1 album in Weird Al's three-decade career. Selling over 100,000 copies in its first week, it was also the first comedy album of any kind to top the chart in 51 years since Allan Sherman's *My Son, the Nut* in 1963. Al strategically deployed all of its multiple music videos in a single week to create an event around the album's release. "The collection debuted on the Billboard 200 album chart at number 1."

## songs
**My Bologna (1979)** : This was a parody of The Knack's "My Sharona," serving as an homage to lunch meat. Recorded by 19-year-old Al Yankovic in a bathroom across from the college radio station at California Polytechnic State University, it launched his recording career and became a smash on the Doctor Demento Show, which claimed it was the most requested song of 1979. Doug Fieger, lead singer of The Knack, appreciated the homage and helped Al get it released on The Knack's label, Capitol Records. "My Belona, an homage to lunch meat, would not only launch Al Yankovik's recording career."
**Another One Rides the Bus (1980)** : This song was a parody of Queen's "Another One Bites the Dust," recorded live in Doctor Demento's studio with Al on accordion and John Bermuda Schwartz pounding on Al's accordion case as a "self-style drummer." Released on former disco label TK Records, it became Weird Al's first Billboard hit, reaching number 104 on the Bubbling Under the Hot 100 chart in March 1981. The song's popularity led to Al's invitation to perform it on Tom Snyder's NBC program *The Tomorrow Show*. "Released on former disco label TK Records, Another One Rides the Bus became Weird Al's first Billboard hit."
**I Love Rocky Road (1982)** : A food-related parody of Joan Jett and the Blackhearts' "I Love Rock 'n Roll," this song helped Al secure a permanent signing to Scotti Brothers Records. It also spawned one of his earliest music videos, which featured a leather-clad Al attempting to appear "badass" while making a grand entrance in an ice cream parlor to demand his favorite flavor. The single later charted in Billboard, bubbling under the Hot 100 at number 106. "I love Rocky Road, helped get Al a permanent signing to a label, Scotty Brothers Records."
**Ricky (1983)** : This parody of Toni Basil's "Mickey" served as an homage to the TV show *I Love Lucy*. The video, shot mostly in black and white, presented a "madcap but letter-perfect parody" of the *Lucy* show, featuring future *Simpsons* voice actress Tress MacNeille as Lucille Ball and Weird Al doing his best Ricky Ricardo impression. The video went into regular rotation on MTV, leading the song to reach number 63 on the Hot 100 in the spring of 1983, less than six months after Toni Basil's original "Mickey" had topped the chart. "Ricky reached number 63 in the spring of 1983."
**Eat It (1984)** : A parody of Michael Jackson's number one hit "Beat It" from his best-selling album *Thriller*, this song targeted the biggest pop sensation on the planet and was recorded and released at the height of "Jackson Mania." After securing Michael Jackson's approval, Al parodied the original song's video shot-for-shot for the first time, recreating Jackson's iconic clip, including the "warring West Side Story-like gangs," and even featuring some dancers from Jackson's original video. It was a smash on MTV and became too big for radio to ignore, reaching number 12 on the Hot 100 and going gold, marking Al's last top 40 hit of the 1980s. "Eat It reached number 12 on the Hot 100 and went gold."
**King of Suede (1984)** : This song from the *Weird Al Yankovic in 3-D* album was a parody of The Police's "King of Pain." Al transformed the original into a sales pitch from a proprietor of cowhide, and it reached number 62 on the charts in May 1984. "King of Swade reached number 62 in May of 84."
**I Lost on Jeopardy (1984)** : This track, also from *Weird Al Yankovic in 3-D*, was an homage to the TV game show *Jeopardy*, parodying the Greg Kihn Band's 1983 number two hit. Al recorded the song while the original NBC version of *Jeopardy*, hosted by Art Fleming, was on a long hiatus. He enlisted Don Pardo, the show's original announcer and *Saturday Night Live* announcer, to record a "hilarious spoken word interlude" calling Al a "complete loser." Pardo, Art Fleming, Dr. Demento, and original songwriter Greg Kihn all made cameos in the music video, which was a smash on MTV and other video shows, though the song only made it to number 81 on the Hot 100 in the summer of 1984, preceding the return of the game show with Alex Trebek by months. "The clip was a smash on MTV and other televised video shows, but the song only made it to number 81 on the Hot 100 in the summer of 1984."
**Like a Surgeon (1985)** : This parody of Madonna's chart-topping "Like a Virgin" just missed the top 40, peaking at number 47. It was featured on his album *Dare to Be Stupid*. "Like a surgeon just missed the top 40 peaking at number 47."
**Fat (1988)** : A parody of Michael Jackson's "Bad," this single only reached number 99 on the charts, though its album *Even Worse* performed better. Al parodied both the song and its video. "The single only reached number 99."
**Smells Like Nirvana (1992)** : Released in the spring of 1992, just weeks after Nirvana's "Smells Like Teen Spirit" had peaked, this parody marked Weird Al's return to the top 40 for the first time since "Eat It" in 1984. Al specifically keyed in on Kurt Cobain's "elliptical, at times indecipherable lyrics" for this parody, and after getting permission from a "very flattered" Cobain, he proceeded to create a parody about Nirvana itself. The song climbed the charts so fast, peaking at number 35, that it leapt two spots over Nirvana's own follow-up hit, "Come as You Are." "Released in the spring of 1992, just weeks after Smells Like Teen Spirit had peaked on the hot 100, Smells Like Nirvana returned Weird Al to the top 40 for the first time since Edith in 1984."
**Amish Paradise (1996)** : This song was a parody of Coolio's smash hit "Gangsta's Paradise." Coolio was initially very protective of his reputation and sounded "genuinely pissed" at Al for "deflating his street cred," believing his original song was "too serious." Al apologized, having recorded it thinking Coolio's management had signed off, and they had "squashed the beef" by 1996. "Kulio sounded genuinely pissed at Al for deflating his street cred."
**The Saga Begins (1999)** : This Star Wars homage, set to the tune of Don McLean's "American Pie," fueled the success of Al's *Running with Scissors* album, which went gold and platinum. Despite the album's success, the single itself did not appear on the Hot 100. "It was called The Saga Begins."
**White & Nerdy (2006)** : This song was a parody of Houston rapper Chamillionaire's number one hit "Ridin' Dirty," offering a different kind of "street tale" about a geek who wants to hang with gangsters but is "too white and nerdy." Al rapped with "legitimately dazzling flow," matching Chamillionaire's triple-time beat for beat and packing dozens of jokes. The accompanying video was a "tour de force," recreating the look and feel of the original clip with a "Geeky Al standing in for the original star." This marked a turning point as the song could be shared on YouTube and purchased digitally, leading it to debut at number 28 on the Hot 100 in mid-October 2006, pole-vaulting to number 9 one week later, becoming Al's first-ever top 10 hit and eventually his first-ever platinum single, despite limited radio airplay. "White and nerdy pole vaulted to number 9, becoming Al's first ever top 10 hit."
**Perform This Way (2011)** : This Lady Gaga parody was one of the tracks for which Al commissioned a video for his *Alpocalypse* album, demonstrating his multi-video strategy. "Lady Gaga parody performed this way."
**Tacky (2014)** : A parody of Pharrell Williams' smash hit "Happy," this video "aped the look of the original clip" and was one of the multiple videos Al deployed in a single week for his *Mandatory Fun* album release. "Some of the videos, like his parody of Ferell Williams Smash Happy, called Tacky, aped the look of the original clip."
**Word Crimes (2014)** : This song was Al's parody of Robin Thicke's hit "Blurred Lines," transformed into a "Grammarian's lament." It became the most shared video from his *Mandatory Fun* album during its debut week, and brought Weird Al back to the top 40, hitting number 39 in August 2014. "Eventually, the most shared video, his parody of Robin Thicks hit Blurred Lines, turned into a Grammarian's lament called Word Crimes, became the most shared video."
**The Hamilton Polka (2018)** : This Broadway medley was a collaboration between Al and Tony Winning Broadway composer Lin-Manuel Miranda. It topped the digital sales chart for one week, hilariously becoming "Poca music's first ever digital chart topper." However, due to downloads counting less for the charts than streaming at the time, it did not make the Hot 100. "The Hamilton Poca topped the digital sales chart for one week, making it, hilariously, Poca music's first ever digital chart topper."

## curiosities
**Early Musical Beginnings** : Alfred Yankovic began home recording his own comical songs, played on a quarter-inch tape recorder, years before his breakthrough. "Al Yankovik had already spent years listening to the Doctor Demento Show, and home recording his own comical songs played on a quarter-year."
**Doctor Demento Show Discovery** : In 1976, Al passed a tape of some of his songs to radio host Doctor Demento when Demento visited Al's high school, leading Demento to play a couple of Al's songs on his show. By 1980, Al was a star on the program. "Demento played a couple of Al's songs on the show."
**Moniker Origin** : The name "Weird Al Yankovik" was formally debuted on the actual label of his "My Bologna" 45 single, but it was a moniker given to him by fellow Cal Poly students a couple of years earlier. "Al formally debuted his full performing name, Weird Al Yankovik, a moniker given to him by fellow Cal Poly students a couple of years earlier."
**Television Presence** : Al understood how to present himself on television, characterized by his shaggy hair and Frank Zappa-like mustache, combined with a "rabid rock and roll attack" on the accordion, an "unrock instrument." "Weird Al knew how to present himself on television, with his shaggy hair and frank zappa-like mustache, and his rabid rock and roll attack on the accordion and unrock instrument."
**Pre-MTV Breakthrough** : Weird Al's initial breakthrough via the Doctor Demento Show predated the 1981 launch of MTV, highlighting his early success independent of music video's widespread influence. "It should be noted that Weird Al's initial breakthrough via the Demento Show predated the 1981 launch of MTV."
**Parody Approval Policy** : Despite parodies being protected as fair use in U.S. law, Al insists on receiving the original artist's blessing to avoid misunderstandings and facilitate the apportionment of royalties on songs he did not originally write. Most artists are flattered by his parodies. "Al insists on receiving the original act's blessing to ensure there are no misunderstandings and to ease the process of apportioning royalties on songs Al did not originally write."
**Film Career Setback** : His 1989 film *UHF*, which he wrote and starred in, was hyped by Orion Pictures as a potential summer blockbuster but "flopped at the box office" during a movie season dominated by hits like *Batman* and *Indiana Jones*, proving "momentarily fatal to his career." "His 1989 film UHF written by Anne Starring Al was hyped by Orion Pictures as a potential summer blockbuster, but it flopped at the box office."
**Shifting Parody Strategy** : At the end of the 80s, after his film flop and a four-year gap since a regular studio album, Al briefly considered parodying Michael Jackson again for his 1991 album but decided against it to avoid deepening his reputation as primarily a "Jackson parodist," instead turning his attention to Nirvana. "But he didn't want to deepen his reputation as primarily a Jackson parodist."
**Album-Oriented Fanbase** : In the late 1980s, Weird Al's fanbase increasingly opted for his albums rather than his singles, valuing his "deep cuts" which included "style parodies" and ingenious "Poca medley's" (accordion covers of multiple hits). This trend meant that as long as an album contained at least one parody of a chart-topping song, it could be relied upon to go gold. "Increasingly, his fanbase was springing for his albums, not his singles."
**Digital Era Resurgence** : The mid-2000s launch of legal commercial music downloading by Apple and video sharing by YouTube significantly boosted Weird Al's career and novelty music in general, changing the possibilities for hit making by enabling digital purchases and widespread video sharing. "The mid-Auth's launch of both legal commercial music downloading by Apple and video sharing by YouTube would change the possibilities for hit making."
**Multi-Video Album Strategy** : For his 2011 album *Alpocalypse*, Al commissioned videos for every track, and for his 2014 album *Mandatory Fun*, he deployed all of its multiple music videos in a single week to turn the album's release into an "event," allowing the marketplace to decide the most popular tracks rather than picking a lead single. "Al began issuing tracks for his next album years before it was even complete."
**Post-Mandatory Fun Career Trajectory** : Six years after his *Mandatory Fun* triumph (as of the late 2010s), it remains his last issued album. In interviews, Al has indicated he might not release albums anymore, choosing instead to focus on individual singles and videos, a sensible strategy given his improbable success in a changing music landscape. He continues to tour. "In interviews in the late 10s, Al has indicated he might not release albums anymore, focusing on individual singles and videos."
**Enduring Happiness and Legacy** : As he tours the country, Weird Al looks happier and prouder of his "unprecedented career" than ever, a sentiment shared by his fans. The narrator speculates that with modern digital platforms like TikTok, his chart-topping career could potentially enter its fifth decade, allowing him to rap into his 70s. "As Weird Al tours the country this summer, he looks happier and prouder of his unprecedented career than ever."

# curiosity

**Doctor Demento Show Influence** : The Doctor Demento Show was a significant platform for novelty music, with host Demento playing early Weird Al Yankovic songs and claiming "My Bologna" as his most requested song of 1979. Al spent years listening to the show and appeared as a guest, solidifying the show's role in his early career. "Al Yankovik had already spent years listening to the Doctor Demento Show."
**U.S. Law on Parodies (Fair Use)** : In U.S. law, parodies are protected under the principle of "fair use," meaning they can be created and distributed without requiring direct permission from the original copyright holder. However, Weird Al Yankovic still sought artists' blessings to ensure smooth royalty apportionment and avoid misunderstandings. "Parodies are protected in U.S. law as fair use."
**Radio Bias Against Novelty Songs** : In the 1980s, novelty songs faced a "radio bias" that contributed to their difficulty in reaching the upper echelons of the Hot 100, often requiring "truly enormous" cultural phenomena to break into the Top 40 due to "regimented top 40 radio playlists." This bias meant that even follow-ups to massive hits struggled to gain significant airplay. "In part, of course, the radio bias against novelty songs was to blame."
**Billboard's Bubbling Under the Hot 100 Chart** : This chart was used by *Billboard* magazine to list songs that ranked just below the Hot 100, such as Weird Al Yankovic's "Another One Rides the Bus" (reaching #104 in 1981) and "I Love Rocky Road" (bubbling under at #106). It served as an indicator of songs with significant, but not mainstream top-tier, popularity. "reaching number 104 on the magazine's doubling under the Hot 100 chart in March of 1981."
**MTV's Impact on Music Videos and Parody (1981)** : The launch of MTV in 1981 and its popularization of the music video in America significantly changed the music landscape. It created a visual dimension for hit songs, particularly New Wave, which "lent themselves to parody" and allowed artists like Weird Al Yankovic to leverage both "comic and visual potential" for his parodies, though his initial breakthrough predated MTV. "But once that video channel took off and popularized the music video in America, the kinds of new wave songs that became hits lent themselves to parody."
**Gold Record Sales Countering Novelty Song Handicap** : Gold record sales proved crucial for novelty songs like Weird Al Yankovic's "Eat It." Because the Hot 100 averaged both sales and airplay, strong sales could "help overcome the handicap novelty songs have on the radio" due to the general radio bias against them, allowing them to chart higher. "Because the Hot 100 averages both sales and airplay, those gold record sales helped overcome the handicap novelty songs have on the radio."
**Novelty Recording Landscape in Late 1980s** : In the late 1980s, very few novelty singles beyond those by Weird Al Yankovic were successful hits, indicating a challenging environment for the genre outside of Al's unique brand. "Very few novelty singles besides Al's were hits at all."
**Rap's Formative Tracks as De Facto Comedy Records** : The history of rap music, from "Rapper's Delight" to "Baby Got Back," is "littered with songs that skirt the line of novelty." Many formative tracks, including Doug E. Fresh and Slick Rick's "The Show," Biz Markie's "Just a Friend," and Digital Underground's "The Humpty Dance," were "built as de facto comedy records," blurring the lines between serious rap and comedic music. "Many of rap's formative tracks are built as de facto comedy records."

"numerous comedians tried to record deliberate rap parodies and none was a major chart success."
**Digital Evolution of the Music Business (Mid-2000s)** : The mid-2000s saw a "major boost" to Weird Al and all novelty musicians with the launch of legal commercial music downloading by Apple (iTunes Music Store) and video sharing by YouTube. These technological changes revolutionized hit making possibilities, enabling album cuts, holiday songs, and other "ephemera" to chart based on single sales and providing new avenues for music promotion. "The mid-Auth's launch of both legal commercial music downloading by Apple and video sharing by YouTube would change the possibilities for hit making."
**Download Sales Added to Hot 100 (2005)** : In 2005, *Billboard* began including download sales in the Hot 100 chart calculations. This change made it possible for album cuts, holiday songs, and other non-traditional singles to achieve chart success based on digital sales in a way they never had before. "Download sales were added to the Hot 100 in 2005."
**YouTube's Launch as a Promotion Tool (2005)** : The launch of YouTube in 2005, even before its views counted for the charts, fundamentally changed the possibilities for music promotion. It quickly demonstrated the power of music and comedy as "potent viral content," with "Lazy Sunday" becoming its "first major hit." "And the launch of YouTube that same year, even though it did not count for the charts back then, changed the possibilities for music promotion."
**RIAA Platinum Single Certification** : "White & Nerdy" achieved the Recording Industry Association of America's (RIAA) platinum single certification, making it Weird Al Yankovic's first-ever single to reach this sales milestone, despite limited radio airplay. "White and nerdy was eventually certified by the Recording Industry Association of America as Weird Al's first ever platinum single."
**Billboard Hot 100 Reboot to Include YouTube Views (2013)** : In 2013, *Billboard* again rebooted the Hot 100 to include YouTube views for the first time. This change had an immediate impact, notably with Baauer's "Harlem Shake" debuting at number one, and soon benefited actual novelty songs like Ylvis's "The Fox (What Does The Fox Say?)" "As we discussed in prior episodes of Hip-Head in 2013, Billboard rebooted the Hot 100 again to include YouTube views for the first time."
**Downloads Count Less in Streaming Era** : In the current music industry landscape, downloads count less for the charts than they once did, as the industry increasingly shifts towards streaming music. This trend impacted the chart performance of releases like "The Hamilton Polka," which topped digital sales but didn't make the Hot 100. "But at a time when downloads count less for the charts than they once did, as the music industry moves towards streaming music, the Hamilton Poca didn't make the hot 100."
**Blurring Lines of Novelty in Modern Hits** : In the 2010s, the lines between novelty and "normal" hits became increasingly unsettled. Songs like Lil Nas X's "Old Town Road" (often called "the biggest novelty hit of all time"), Lizzo's "Truth Hurts" (fueled by memes), and Roddy Ricch's "The Box" (propelled by TikTok videos) illustrate a "greater premium placed on recording artists who can bring the snarky, the goofy, the kitschy, the funny" to achieve chart success. "the lines between novelty and normal hits have been unsettled at best."
**Billboard 200 Album Chart Including Official YouTube Videos** : In the 2020s, *Billboard* began counting official YouTube videos for its album chart. This change suggests that a multi-video album launch by an artist like Weird Al could lead to even greater album and single success in the future. "In the 2020s, Billboard even started to count official YouTube videos for its album chart."
