## albums
**Stankonia (2000) - Game-Changing Album** : Released in the fall of 2000, this album was a pivotal moment for Outcast, transforming them from leading figures in Southern Hip Hop to one of America's biggest pop acts. It sold more copies than almost any Atlanta rap album before it, solidifying Atlanta's position as a rap scene. "And it would change the game, selling more copies than almost any Atlanta rap album that came before it."
**Southern Play Elistic Cadillac Music (1994) - Landmark Debut Album** : Regarded as a landmark, this album was the first Atlanta rap album to compete with East and West Coast rap titans. It made timely nods to low rider and G-funk culture, but its lyrical perspective and musical motifs, often played on live instruments, took a deliberately Southern point of view. It debuted at number 20 on the Billboard album chart in May 1994, riding the chart for half a year, and eventually went platinum about a year after its release. "Southern Play Elistic, as it is now often called for short, is still regarded as a landmark, the first Atlanta rap album to go toe-to-toe with the Titans of East and West Coast rap."
**ATL-I-E-N-S (1996) - Sophomore Smash Album** : This album's title is a portmanteau of "aliens" and the three-letter code for Atlanta, pronounced "ATL-Ians," which Andre explained was inspired by their "status in the hip hop game" as "rap's aliens." It was a smash upon impact, debuting at number two on the Billboard 200 album chart in September 1996 and going platinum out of the box, later certified double platinum. The album showcased a savvy mix of spacey sonics, rapping, and elements of Neo Soul. "ATL-Ians was a smash on impact, debuting at number two on the Billboard 200 album chart in September 1996 and going platinum out of the box."
**Aquemini (1998) - Acclaimed Third Album** : Another portmanteau title, Aquemini combined Big Boy's zodiac sign (Aquarius) and Andre's (Gemini). While none of its singles were major chart hits, the album remains one of Outcast's most acclaimed LPs, with both the album and its lead single making the year-end top 10 of the Village Voice's Pazz & Jop Critics poll for the first time for the duo. Despite lacking big hits, Aquemini debuted at number 2 on the album chart, went platinum within a month, double platinum by the following summer, and rode the album chart for 10 months, longer than any previous Outcast album. "But Aquemini remains one of Outcast's most acclaimed LPs."

## songs
**The Way You Move (2004)** : This Big Boy-fronted song replaced the Andre-powered "Hey Ya!" at number one on Billboard's Hot 100 chart in the week ending February 14th, 2004, marking a rare instance of an artist replacing themselves at the top. This achievement coincided with Outcast's significant success at the Grammys that same week, representing a culmination of their decade-long journey. "The big boy fronted the way you move, replaced the Andre powered Hey Yaw, a top billboards flagship chart."
**Hey Yaw (2004)** : This Andre-powered song topped Billboard's Hot 100 chart and was replaced at number one by Outcast's own Big Boy-fronted track, "The Way You Move," in the week ending February 14th, 2004. This self-replacement at the chart's peak happened in the same week Outcast were celebrating major Grammy wins. "replaced the Andre powered Hey Yaw, a top billboards flagship chart."
**Players Ball (1993)** : Outcast's debut single, originally released for the compilation "A LaFace Family Christmas" in 1993, appeared to be an on-trend gangster rap track, but its lyrics subtly referenced the holiday season by describing an annual gathering of pimps known as "A Players Ball" happening on Christmas Day. The song's verses mention eggnog, a "hololuia" chorus, and lyrical allusions to Christmas carols, while the backing track featured sleigh bells. "Maybe we should call Players Ball, the diehard of debuts, an action story that's secretly also a Christmas story."
**Players Ball (1994) - Re-recorded Single** : Issued as a single in the winter of 1994, this version of Outcast's debut track proved to be their breakthrough and a sleeper hit after L.A. Reed approved a subtle re-recording of the chorus. Sleepy Brown's original line, "The Players Ball is happening on Christmas Day," was changed to "The Players Ball is happening all day, every day," making it more suitable for mainstream play beyond the holidays. It debuted on the R&B chart and Hot 100 in February 1994, peaking in May at number 12 R&B, number 37 pop (an actual top 40 hit), and number 1 on Billboard's Hot Rap Singles chart. "Issued as a single in the winter of 1994, this version of Players Ball proved to be Outcast's breakthrough and a sleeper hit."
**Southern Play Elistic Cadillac Music (1994)** : This was the title track from Outcast's debut album and became a number 9 rap hit, narrowly missing the R&B top 40. The song contributed to the album's success, which rode the Billboard album chart for half a year. "These included the album's title track, a number 9 rap hit that just missed the R&B top 40..."
**Get Up, Get Out (1994)** : This song was a number 13 rap hit and number 59 R&B hit, important primarily because it was the first single to feature members of Goodie Mob, including a long showcase for the rapping of CeeLo Green and Big Gip. Five years later, Macy Gray remade it as "Do Something" on her debut album "On How Life Is." "Outcasts, get up, get out, was the first single to feature these Goodymob members, including a long showcase for the Malifluis rapping of Sealo, who later proved an equally adept singer."
**Elevators (Me & You) (1996)** : This track marked a significant stylistic shift for Outcast, abandoning 90s gangster rap aesthetics for a spaceier, weirder, and cooler sound. Built by Outcast themselves completely free of samples, its languid beat and spacey rapping distinguished them from East or West Coast rap. Despite initial skepticism from LaFace Records president L.A. Reed, Outcast took the song to radio themselves, and it became a sizable hit, peaking at number 1 on the rap singles chart, number 5 R&B, and number 12 on the Hot 100 in summer 1996, leading Reed to trust their instincts for single choices thereafter. "On elevators, me and you, Outcast made the cutting edge mainstream, with its languid beat built by Outcast themselves, completely free of samples, and its spacey rapping, elevators firmly distinguished Dre and Big Boy from either East Coast or West Coast rap."
**Jazzy Belle (1997) - Remixed Single** : This song was the third single from the "ATL-I-E-N-S" album, showcasing the album's elements of Neo Soul. It was remixed in early 1997 to incorporate soulful backing vocals provided by LaFace co-founder Kenneth Babyface Edmonds, further deepening Outcast's exploration of R&B sounds. "Particularly on the album's third single, Jazzy Bell, which was remixed in early 97 to add soulful backing vocals by LaFace co-founder Kenneth Babyface Edmonds."
**In Due Time (1997)** : Featured on the soundtrack to the 1997 movie "Soul Food," this stopgap single saw Outcast delving even deeper into an R&B sound. It included some of the earliest singing by Goodie Mob rapper CeeLo Green, who provided the song's melodic hook, complementing Dre's and Big Boy's rapping. "The song, Indu Time, featured some of the earliest singing by Goodie Mob rapper, Silo Green, who provided the song's melodic hook alongside Dres and Big Boy's Rapping."
**Rosa Parks (1998)** : The first single from "Aquemini," this song prophetically blended hip hop with country elements, notably featuring a harmonica breakdown played by Andre's stepfather, Pastor Robert Hodev. The track, cryptically named after the Civil Rights legend, led to a legal shadow over Outcast for years, as the actual Rosa Parks sued for defamation, confused by the song's invoking of her name and distressed by its language, with the case settled in the mid-2000s, six months before her death. The song also highlighted the distinct personalities of Big Boy's street-wise delivery and Andre's aphrofuturist "freakiness." "Rosa Parks was prophetic for Outcast in a number of ways."
**Chonky Fire (1998)** : The final track on the "Aquemini" album, this song included a sample of Andre's now-immortal 1995 Source Awards speech, where he famously declared, "The South Got Something To Say." This inclusion served as a form of self-mythologizing for the duo, affirming their role as avatars of Southern Hip Hop. "Aquemini's final track, Chonky Fire, included a sample of Andre's now-already immortal 1995 Source Awards Speed."

## curiosities
**Grammy Win Leading to Demise (2004)** : Outcast's enormous chart and cultural success, culminating in a very rare Grammy award win in 2004, effectively brought about the duo's self-imposed demise. They were the last rappers to win such an award at that point. "And this win effectively brought about Outcast's self-imposed demise."
**Source Awards Booing and Andre's Prophetic Speech (1995)** : At the 1995 Source Awards in New York, Outcast were booed by the audience when they won Best New Rap Group, becoming collateral damage in the brewing East Coast-West Coast rap feud. Despite the "Bronx cheers," Andre Benjamin delivered what is considered one of the most enlightened speeches of the evening, concluding with the prophetic line, "The South Got Something To Say," which became a rallying cry for Southern Rappers and profoundly influenced future Atlanta rap titans like Killer Mike, Ludacris, and T.I. "It was at that moment that Outcast's Andre Benjamin gave perhaps the most enlightened speech of the evening, taking raspberries and making raspberry lemonade."
**Initial Audition for L.A. Reed (1992)** : Antoine Patton and Andre Benjamin, then only 17 and having met at 16 in an Atlanta mall, auditioned for L.A. Reed in 1992. Reed, despite admitting he "didn't really know anything about rap," was charmed and impressed by their personality and flow, especially Andre's ebullient, dazzling, triple-time cadence, which he had picked up listening to New York MCs like A Tribe Called Quest and Das EFX. "Outcast, first auditioned for LA Read in 1992."
**Name Change from Miss Fitz** : The duo chose the name Outcast after discovering that their initially preferred moniker, "Miss Fitz," was already taken by a punk band. "They chose the name Outcast after learning that their preferred moniker, Miss Fitz, was already taken by a punk band."
**Development of Persona and "Sex Appeal" (1993)** : After their debut on a TLC remix, LaFace Records sent Outcast into the studio in 1993, but not for a full album immediately. L.A. Reed felt the duo needed time to develop their persona and their "sex appeal" before recording a complete project, leading to their first single being for a Christmas compilation. "Read felt the duo needed time to develop their persona and their, quote, sex appeal."
**Early Connection with Gangster Rap and Puffy Combs (1994)** : The music video for "Players Ball" was directed by Sean Combs (Puff Daddy), a young New York producer and budding mogul who was launching his Bad Boy label and the career of The Notorious B.I.G. Outcast later opened concerts for Biggie, establishing a "tenuous arms length connection between gangster rap and Outcast," despite their deliberately Southern point of view in their debut album. "In a move that would prove prophetically ironic, the video was directed by Sean Combs, the young New York producer and budding mogul, then known as Puff Daddy."
**The Dungeon Family Collective** : Outcast and Goodie Mob, along with the Organized Noise production team (including Rico Lee), formed a loose Atlanta collective called The Dungeon Family. Named after Organized Noise's basement recording studio, "The Dungeon," this collective was instrumental in "world building" and creating a true Atlanta hip-hop scene from scratch. "Together, Outcast and Goodymob formed a loose Atlanta collective, who called themselves the Dungeon family, named after the basement studio of organized noise producer Rico Lee."
**Stylistic Evolution and Personal Changes (1996)** : Between their first and second albums, Andre and Big Boy significantly changed their artistic approach, largely abandoning 90s gangster rap aesthetics. Andre grew out his hair, gave up cornrows, occasionally wore a turban, went vegan, sober, and briefly tried celibacy. Big Boy became a father, which he said grounded him as an artist. These personal evolutions led to a record that redefined Atlanta rap as spaceier, weirder, and cooler. "But for the partnership of Big Boy and Dre, it was the beginning of the end."
**L.A. Reed's Trust in Single Choices (1996)** : After Outcast successfully pushed for "Elevators (Me & You)" to be released as a single against LaFace Records president L.A. Reed's initial doubts, and it became a hit, Reed never again doubted their instincts. According to Big Boy, Reed "let them choose their singles from then on." "L.A. Reed never doubted Outcast instincts again. According to Big Boy, he let them choose their singles from then on."
**Rosa Parks Lawsuit (Mid-2000s)** : The song "Rosa Parks" led to a legal dispute where the actual Civil Rights legend, Rosa Parks, sued Outcast and their label for defamation, feeling confused by the song's use of her name and distressed by its language. The case was finally settled in the mid-2000s, just six months before Parks's death. "The case was finally settled in the mid 2000s, just six months before Parks's death."
**Visualizing Distinct Personalities in Rosa Parks Video** : The music video for "Rosa Parks" explicitly depicted the melding of Andre's and Big Boy's distinct personalities and artistic visions. The opening seconds show them trading ideas, with Big Boy suggesting "cars and pimps" and Andre proposing "spacey with futuristic type things," to which Big Boy concludes, "alright then, let's do both of them." "The video for Rosa Parks makes this plain."

# label - LaFace Records

## curiosities
**Founding and Mission (Late 80s - Early 90s)** : Founded by Antonio L.A. Reed and Kenneth Babyface Edmonds, LaFace Records was envisioned as a "Motown-like label" at the turn of the 1990s. Its primary focus was on generating crossover R&B hits that appealed equally to pop and Black radio, evolving into a "rap conversant label" rather than a hardcore rap label. "So it made sense that Reed and Edmonds would form their own Motown-like label. They called it LaFace Records."
**Early Roster and Success (Early 90s)** : By the early 1990s, LaFace Records was generating smashes with rising stars, including Pebbles and, most notably, TLC, who became their biggest seller of the decade with their blend of sultry R&B and a dash of rapping. The label's success with artists defining Atlanta's sound burnished its reputation as a "red hot center of black music in the 90s." "Reed's included the Oakland-born Atlanta-based Perry Reed, aka Pebbles, who was then married to L.A. Reed."
**First Rap Act: Outcast (1992)** : Outcast became LaFace Records' first-ever rap act, having auditioned for L.A. Reed in 1992. This move marked a departure for a label better known for urban pop and crossover R&B, and ultimately helped redefine its artistic scope. "So, to boost its hip-hop cred, Le Face ordered up a remix of the track, and that remix included the latest sign-ees to the Le Face roster, the label's first-ever rap act."
**Christmas Compilation (1993)** : In 1993, LaFace released "A LaFace Family Christmas," a compilation album that included Outcast's debut single "Players Ball," along with more overtly jolly material from TLC and Toni Braxton. The album was considered a "lark" for the label and was not a big hit, peaking at number 186 on the Billboard album chart. "Recorded for the compilation, A LaFace Family Christmas."

# concert - Source Awards

## curiosities
**East Coast vs. West Coast Feud (1995)** : The 1995 Source Awards in New York City became a flashpoint for the escalating East Coast versus West Coast rap feud, serving as the event where tensions reached a peak. The New York audience repeatedly booed West Coast artists, leading to fiery acceptance speeches, most notably by Suge Knight, who dissed Puff Daddy and invited Tupac Shakur to join Death Row Records. "But when Southern Raps leading duo actually won the prize, that's when the New York crowd unleashed more of the same Bronx cheers they had been throwing at the California Poutine."
**Outcast's Booing and "The South Got Something To Say" (1995)** : Outcast, despite winning the Best New Rap Group award at the 1995 Source Awards, were booed by the New York audience, who were caught up in the East Coast-West Coast rap tensions. In response, Andre Benjamin delivered a profoundly prophetic speech, concluding with the iconic line, "The South Got Something To Say," which became a rallying cry and cemented Southern rap's future influence. "And to win it in, Outcast! It was at that moment that Outcast's Andre Benjamin gave perhaps the most enlightened speech of the evening, taking raspberries and making raspberry lemonade."

# curiosity

**Atlanta as a Music Capital ("Hot Lanta")** : For much of the 20th century, Atlanta was a major center for country music. By the 1970s and 80s, it hosted diverse sounds from R&B groups like The SOS Band to folk rockers like The Indigo Girls. The instrumental track "Hot Lanta" from The Almond Brothers Band's 1971 album helped popularize this nickname, solidifying Atlanta's identity as a capital of music, paving the way for its later transformation into a hip-hop factory. "But this track helped popularize and institutionalize the Hot Lanta nickname, and indeed the idea of Atlanta as a capital of music."
**L.A. Reid and Babyface Hit Factory (Late 80s - Early 90s)** : Antonio L.A. Reed and Kenneth Babyface Edmonds, initially born in the Midwest but settled in Atlanta, became the "premiere purveyors of crossover R&B" in the late 1980s. Their hit factory generated songs that worked equally well on pop and Black radio, translating R&B and New Jack Swing for a wider, mainstream audience, much like Motown did in the 1960s. "Though L.A. and Babyface were both born in different parts of the Midwest, once they settled in Atlanta and began writing and recording together in the late 80s, they became the premiere purveyors of crossover R&B."
**Atlanta Rap Before Outcast (Early 90s)** : Before Outcast, Atlanta rap was diverse, hard to pin down to a single sound, and less focused on "street cred" than on "irresistible pop hooks." Acts like Kris Kross, Tag Team, and Arrested Development found multi-platinum success in the early 1990s with pop-friendly, socially conscious, or Miami Bass-influenced sounds, but they didn't constitute a cohesive "scene" sonically, with their Atlanta heritage often being coincidental. "Indeed, this was the situation with Atlanta rap to that date. It was hard to pin down to a sound and less focused on street cred than on irresistible pop books."
**The Dungeon Studio** : A basement recording studio where Outcast recorded their debut album with the Organized Noise production team. This studio became the namesake for the "Dungeon Family" collective, symbolizing the grassroots origin of Atlanta's hip-hop scene. "Andre and Big Boy recorded their debut album with the organized noise production team in a basement recording studio known as The Dungeon."
**World-Building of Atlanta Hip-Hop Scene (Mid-90s)** : The Dungeon Family collective, composed of Outcast, Goodie Mob, and the Organized Noise production team, was actively "world building" in the mid-1990s. They were essentially creating a true Atlanta hip-hop scene from scratch, crafting a unique sound and identity for the region. "These producers and rappers were, in essence, world building, creating a true Atlanta hip-hop scene, essentially from scratch."
**Demand for Dungeon Family Producers (Late 94 - 95)** : By late 1994 and 1995, the Dungeon Family collective had garnered enough demand that they began producing for acts outside their immediate orbit. This included LaFace Records inviting Organized Noise to write and produce "Waterfalls" for TLC's second album, "Crazy Sexy Cool." "By late 94 and 95, the Dungeon family collective was in enough demand that they began producing for acts outside of their immediate orbit."
