# curiosity

**Musicians and Political Endorsements** : The effectiveness of musicians endorsing political candidates is frequently debated, with a clear one-to-one connection between endorsement and electoral success rarely observed. A notable exception was Frank Sinatra's endorsement of John F. Kennedy in the extremely close 1960 presidential campaign. "I'm not sure that there's ever been a clear one-to-one connection between a musician endorsing a candidate."
**Musicians Opposing Political Campaign Song Use** : It is a recurring phenomenon for musicians, particularly liberal artists, to publicly request that Republican political candidates cease using their songs. High-profile instances include Rihanna issuing a cease and desist to President Trump for "Don't Stop The Music" and Bruce Springsteen's 1984 objection to Ronald Reagan's use of "Born in the USA." "mostly when music makes headlines connected to political campaigns, it's usually over an artist, usually a liberal artist, telling usually or a Republican candidate, please stop using my song."
**SoundScan's Transformative Impact on Music Charts** : SoundScan is described as a "BCAD moment" and a definitive "dividing line" in the history of music chart formulation. Its introduction fundamentally revolutionized how record sales were tallied, providing significantly more accurate data than the previous reliance on anecdotal reports from retailers. "sound scan, because it really is... the BCAD moment, the dividing line for how the charts work."
**Pre-1990s Charting Limitations** : Prior to the widespread adoption of SoundScan technology in the 1990s, Billboard's ability to accurately compile charts was hampered by technological limitations. These included the absence of barcode scanners in retail stores and insufficient computing power to precisely tally individual record sales at the cash register. "Right through the end of the 1980s, they just did not have the technology, either the barcode scanners in retail stores, nor the computing power... to properly tally sales of records at the cash register."
**Record Retailer Bias (Pre-SoundScan)** : Before the advent of SoundScan, record retailers exhibited a "built in bias" in their sales reporting, often favoring mainstream rock and pop titles. This systemic bias led to the underreporting of sales for genres like hip-hop (e.g., N.W.A albums) and country, even when these genres were among a store's better sellers, skewing chart accuracy. "if you called up, you know, your average record retailer, he would sort of lean toward, you know, the same sort of rock and pop titles. And he maybe wasn't paying enough attention to the fact that like, you know, an NWA album was actually among his better sellers."
**Modern Chart Data Includes Streaming** : Contemporary music chart data has evolved significantly to include comprehensive streaming figures from major platforms such as Spotify, Apple Music, and YouTube. This integration provides an extremely accurate and real-time reflection of music consumption patterns. "Now the data is so, so, so good because you can, you know, you're getting streaming from things like Spotify and Apple Music. And like you said, YouTube."
**Sampling in Early 90s Rap** : The early 1990s rap scene was characterized by its extensive use of samples from earlier hit songs. Notable examples include Vanilla Ice's "Ice Ice Baby" (sampling Queen and David Bowie), PM Dawn's "Set Adrift on Memory Bliss" (sampling Spandau Ballet), and Kris Kross's "Jump" (sampling The Jackson Five). Sir Mix-A-Lot's "Baby Got Back" was a rare, successful exception to this trend, containing no samples. "big early 90s rap hits that were built around prominent samples of earlier hits."
**Teen Pop Boom of the Late 1990s** : The late 1990s witnessed a "teen pop renaissance" where the Hot 100 charts were dominated by boy bands and female teen artists. These acts often performed "frothy hits" primarily crafted by Swedish pop songwriters and producers like Max Martin, who fundamentally reshaped the sound of pop radio and hit creation. "In the late 90s, the charts underwent a teen pop renaissance with boy bands and teen girls taking over the Hot 100, singing frothy hits mostly written by Swedish pop craftsman, foremost among them, Max Martin."
