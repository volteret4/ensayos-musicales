## albums
**Demo Album (Early 1970s) - San Francisco Demo After New York Shows** : After a series of performances in New York City, Sylvester won the chance to record a demo album back in San Francisco. **Albums with The Hot Band (1970s) - Two Rock-Era Records** : With his manager, Sylvester assembled a backing group of straight white men called The Hot Band. They covered songs such as Procol Harum's "A Whiter Shade of Pale". After two albums, The Hot Band left Sylvester because his diva behavior had alienated them. "After two albums, The Hot Band broke away from Sylvester, whose diva behavior alienated the group."
**Sylvester (1977) - Self-Titled Fantasy Records Solo Debut** : Sylvester signed to Fantasy Records for his solo debut. To cross over to a mainstream audience, he appeared on the cover in male attire, though he still sang in falsetto. The album produced his first chart hit, "Down, Down, Down". "Sylvester presented himself on the cover of his self-titled debut album in Male attire, in an effort to cross over with a mainstream audience."
**Step II (1978) - Acclaimed Breakthrough Album** : On his second LP, Sylvester made his gender presentation deliberately inscrutable, alternating glittery female attire on stage with butch signifiers like leather pants. The album was his breakthrough: he grew bigger with all audiences as he became queerer. It spawned "Dance (Disco Heat)" and "You Make Me Feel (Mighty Real)", reached No. 28 on the pop album chart and went gold. "He got bigger on the charts with all audiences when he got queerer."
**Stars (1979) - Deeper into Electronic Dance Music with Patrick Cowley** : Sylvester moved quickly to record this follow-up to Step II and worked even more closely with Patrick Cowley, pushing further into electronic dance music. The first single was a cover of the torch-song classic "I (Who Have Nothing)". "They went deeper into electronic dance music."

"Dance, Perenthase's Disco Heat, not only topped the Disco chart, it climbed to number 4 on the R&B chart and even number 19 on the pop chart by November of 78."
**You Make Me Feel (Mighty Real) (1978) - High-Energy Dance Classic and Queer Landmark** : Written by Sylvester with songwriter James Wirrick, it was technically the flip side of "Dance (Disco Heat)" but was treated as the A-side in clubs and on the disco chart. It reached No. 1 on Billboard's disco chart for the week ending August 19, 1978. Its secret weapon was Sylvester's friend, producer Patrick Cowley, who was inspired by Donna Summer's Euro-disco classic "I Feel Love" and remixed the single into a synthesized frenzy. Sylvester's vocal captured the lyrics' liberated euphoria. The song reached No. 20 on the R&B chart and No. 36 on the pop chart in February 1979. Jimmy Somerville's cover reached No. 5 in the UK in 1990, and Byron Stingily of Ten City took his version to No. 1 on the Dance Club Play chart in 1998. "A high energy dance classic and a queer landmark."
**I (Who Have Nothing) (1979) - Final Pop Top 40 Hit** : The first single from Stars was a cover of a torch-song classic. Ben E. King's original reached No. 29 in 1963, and Tom Jones's more theatrical version hit No. 14 seven years later. Sylvester turned it into a dance-floor drama and club smash. It peaked at No. 40 in May 1979, just barely making it his third straight pop Top 40 hit and his last week ever in the pop Top 40. "Sylvester's hands, I, who have nothing, became a dance floor drama and another club's smash."
**Do You Wanna Funk (1982) - Megaton Club Hit with Patrick Cowley** : A collaboration with Patrick Cowley on Cowley's Megaton label that reached No. 4 on the club chart. "Among their collaborations were the 1982 hits Do You Want A Funk, a number four club hit."
**Don't Stop (1982) - Club Chart Hit** : Another 1982 collaboration with Patrick Cowley, which reached No. 3 on the club chart. "don't stop, which hit number three on the club chart."

## curiosities
**Birth and Early Life in Watts** : Sylvester was born Sylvester James Jr. in Watts, California, in 1947, and later used only his first name. He had star quality from a very young age. He clashed with his mother and stepfather, who disapproved of his sexual identity and feminine presentation. He was raised singing gospel in the Pentecostal Church. "He eventually went just by a mononym, his first name, Sylvester."
**Star of The Cockettes in San Francisco** : In his early twenties Sylvester moved to San Francisco, where he became the star attraction of the Cockettes, a multiracial drag ensemble. "he became the star attraction of the cockets, a multi-racial drag ensemble."
**Male Pronouns and Gender Non-Conformity** : Sylvester never hid his homosexuality or his voice, but he insisted on male pronouns. Music historians suspect that if he had lived into the 21st century he might have used she/her or they/them. He was always sparkly and dressed to the nines, even when fronting The Hot Band. The transcript notes that "cross-dressing singer" was the term of art at the time. "Through it all, Sylvester did not hide either his homosexuality or his voice, though he insisted on male pronouns."
**Long Road to Dance Music** : Sylvester tried gospel, folk, R&B and rock before settling on disco. After The Hot Band left, it took three years and several more lineup changes among his accompanists before he turned to dance music. "It took three years and several more lineup changes in Sylvester's accompanists before he finally pivoted toward dance music."
**Martha Wash and Izora Rhodes as Backing Singers** : The backing singers Sylvester eventually agreed to work with were Martha Wash and Izora Rhodes, described as big-voiced plus-size vocal divas. They later renamed themselves Two Tons of Fun and then The Weather Girls. "the backing singers Sylvester eventually agreed to work with were a pair of big voiced plus-size vocal dinos named Martha Wash and Izzora Rhodes."
**Performed at Harvey Milk's Birthday Party** : Despite presenting in male attire for mainstream crossover, Sylvester became a regular performer at gay clubs and events, including the birthday party of San Francisco gay politician Harvey Milk. "including the birthday party of iconic San Francisco gay politician Harvey Milk."
**Pitchfork on Defying Categorization** : Pitchfork later wrote that Sylvester refused to be categorized and brushed off attempts to pin him down. He is quoted dismissing the question of being gay. "look dear, being gay means absolutely nothing except to straight people."
**Casey Kasem's American Top 40 Commentary** : Casey Kasem had little to say about Sylvester while his hits ran on American Top 40. In the last chart week of "I (Who Have Nothing)", however, Kasem described Sylvester posing for his high school yearbook photo dressed as a woman. He told listeners that since his early teens Sylvester had adored women blues singers of the early 1930s, collecting records by Ethel Merman, Billie Holiday and Bessie Smith and learning to mimic their styles. By high school, Kasem said, Sylvester dressed like his favorite singers for parties, talent contests and beauty contests, which he often won. "This is Casey in Hollywood and this song is by the man who posed for his high school year book photo dressed as a woman."
**Move to the Dance Charts in the 1980s** : After the spring of 1979, Sylvester lived on the dance charts. He kept working with Patrick Cowley, signed to Cowley's Megaton label and continued to score disco chart hits. "As the 70s turned to the 80s, he became a denizen of the dance charts."
**Death from AIDS and Self-Planned Funeral** : Sylvester died of AIDS-related complications in December 1988, six years after Patrick Cowley. He had planned his own funeral in San Francisco's Castro district down to the kimono he wore in his coffin, and it was packed with mourners. "which Sylvester had planned himself down to the kimono outfit he wore in his coffin, was packed with mourners."
**Pivot Point for Queer Acceptance on the Charts** : Sylvester is described as a proto-trans icon who lived his truth while climbing the pop, R&B and dance charts at a pivotal moment for both pop and queer liberation. He embodied a new archetype for LGBTQ identity on the charts. After him, drag performers and queer singers of various kinds found greater pop acceptance, even as homophobia and the AIDS crisis kept many from coming out. "Sylvester was a pivotal figure in the main streaming of LGBTQ and gender non-conforming artists on the charts."

# genre - Rock and Roll

## curiosities
**A Black and Proudly Queer Art Form** : Rock, rhythm and blues and most pop music are described as not only culturally Black but proudly queer art forms. Queer performers were there at the dawn of rock and roll, at the height of glam rock and singer-songwriter pop, in disco, synth-pop, new wave and even alternative rock. "most of the pop music we consume is not only a culturally black art form, but a proudly queer art form."

# genre - Disco

## curiosities
**Pioneered by Gay DJs, Tastemakers and Club Patrons** : Disco was a far more fertile arena than glam for 1970s LGBTQ creativity. The scene and subculture were pioneered by gay DJs, tastemakers and club patrons. The seamless DJ 12-inch mix was essentially invented by proto-remixer Tom Moulton (rendered "Tom Jones" in the transcript) in a gay Fire Island nightclub. "the whole disco scene and subculture were fundamentally pioneered by gay DJs, taste makers, and club patrons."
**Queen of Disco and Gay Anthems** : The title Queen of Disco went at different times to both Gloria Gaynor and Donna Summer. Both recorded dance anthems that spoke to gay audiences, "I Will Survive" and "I Feel Love", which then crossed over to the mainstream. "disco artists of any orientation knew they were catering to a club audience rooted in the gay community."

# curiosity

**November 1980 Chart Convergence** : The week Diana Ross's "I'm Coming Out" peaked at No. 5, Queen's "Another One Bites the Dust", sung by the closeted Freddie Mercury, sat at No. 4. The episode presents this as a summary of LGBTQ chart history: pride alongside prudence, celebration alongside hesitation. "self-expression side by side with self-denial, pride plus prudence, celebration plus hesitation."
**First Fully Out Gay Performer with a Number One Album** : The new millennium saw the first fully out gay performer to have a No. 1 album. The artist is teased but not named in this part of the episode. "the new millennium saw the first fully out gay performer with a number one album."
**Coming Out Felt Impossible in the 50s and 60s** : For LGBTQ stars of the 1950s, coming out felt impossible, and this mostly continued into the nominally more progressive 1960s. "So obviously for 50s LGBTQ stars coming out felt impossible."
**Sexual Identity Is Not an Exact Science** : Charts are numerical and specific, while gender and sexual identity sit on a spectrum. Pinpointing anyone's sexual identity, even someone as seemingly out as Little Richard, is not an exact science. "pinpointing anyone's sexual identity. Even someone as famous and seemingly out as Little Richard is not an exact science."
**Greater Freedom in the 21st Century** : In the 21st century, LGBTQ artists have more freedom than ever and feel freer to be themselves at earlier career stages, though there is still far to go. "Happily in the 21st century, LGBTQ artists have felt freer to be themselves, and at earlier career stages."
**Mainstream Acceptance on American Top 40 in 1979** : Casey Kasem's description of Sylvester's cross-dressing history while counting down "I (Who Have Nothing)" is offered as an example of what mainstream acceptance sounded like at the time. "This is what mainstream acceptance sounded like in 1979."
