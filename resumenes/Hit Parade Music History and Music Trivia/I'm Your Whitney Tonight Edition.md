## albums
**Whitney Houston (1985) - Debut Album** : Released on Valentine's Day, 1985, this self-titled LP initially found success within the R&B market before crossing over to pop. By the end of 1985, it had become the year's sleeper hit, rising into the top 10 on the Billboard album chart and achieving double platinum status. In 1986, it was named the top black album and the top album of any genre by Billboard, marking the first time a black female artist had the year's top album, and the first LP by a black woman to generate three number one pop hits. "If Whitney Houston, the album, had generated no further hits, it would already rank among the most successful debut albums ever."

**Whitney (1987) - Record-Setting Second Album** : Released in June 1987, this album debuted at number one on the album chart, stunning the music business and making Whitney Houston the first female artist to achieve a number one debut on the album chart and the only woman to do so before the Soundscan system was implemented in the early 90s. This was only Houston's second album, setting a record for the youngest artist to have America's top album out of the box in the pre-computerized era, demonstrating cross-cultural dominance. "In June of 1987, it stunned the music business by entering the chart on top."

## songs
**Home (1983) - Merv Griffin Show Debut** : Houston performed this song from the musical *The Wiz* during her auspicious television debut on the Merv Griffin Show in 1983, introduced by Clive Davis. The track, previously sung by Stephanie Mills on Broadway and Diana Ross on film, was a deep cut primarily known and resonant with black radio audiences, signaling that Houston was first pitched largely to African Americans despite Davis's crossover dreams. "The song Whitney was singing that day on Merv Griffin was best known by serious R&B fans."

**Hold Me (1984) - Formal Recorded Debut** : This duet with Teddy Pendergrass served as Houston's formal recorded debut and was a top 5 R&B hit in the summer of 1984. It primarily found success within the R&B charts, peaking at number 46 on the Hot 100, and helped reinvigorate Pendergrass's status on black radio after his car accident. "Hold Me, Build as a duet of Teddy Pendergrass with Whitney Houston, was a top 5 R&B hit in the summer of 1984."

**You Give Good Love (1985)** : Chosen as the first single from her self-titled debut album, this smoldering mid-tempo soul number, produced by Kashif, topped Billboard's R&B chart (then called Hot Black Singles) in the spring of 1985. Arista Records spent the entire first half of 1985 promoting it to Top 40 Radio, eventually leading it to reach number three on the Hot 100 in the summer of 1985, proving an impressive start for a new R&B star. "The choice of You Give Good Love as the first single from the Whitney Houston LP was a signal to black audiences."

**Saving All My Love For You (1985)** : Written by veteran white songwriters Jerry Goffin and Michael Masser, this song, sung from the perspective of a mistress, became Houston's first number one on both the R&B and pop charts in the fall of 1985. Houston's vocal performance infused the track with both youthful innocence and soulful grit, helping it achieve its full potential. "It became Houston's first number one on both the R&B and pop charts in the fall of 1985."

**Thinking About You (1985)** : An uptempo funk and dance song, this track was promoted exclusively to Black Radio by Arista Records. It reached number 10 on Hot Black Singles in the closing weeks of 1985 but did not make the Hot 100 at all, indicating a specific targeting of the R&B audience during the album's initial success. "Arista took this moment to promote an uptempo funk and dance song thinking about you exclusively to Black Radio."

**How Will I Know (1986)** : This song was strategically saved by Arista for Houston's third pop single and fourth R&B single, serving as the fulcrum for her career by hybridizing R&B and pop elements. Originally written by George Merrill and Shannon Rubikam for Janet Jackson, it was acquired by Arista and produced by Narada Michael Walden, who rearranged it, added a new verse, and changed its key and tempo, with Sissy Houston contributing backing vocals. The sleek track vocalized like a soul song but played like New Wave pop, became Houston's first video to receive appreciable MTV play, and topped both the Hot 100 (first) and Hot Black Singles. "How Will I Know became the fulcrum for Whitney Houston's career, a sleek Frankenstein's monster that hybridized the R&B and pop elements of her sound."

**The Greatest Love of All (1986)** : Originally written in 1977 by Michael Masser and Linda Creed for a Muhammad Ali biopic and first recorded by George Benson, Houston's version became the biggest pop hit from her debut album, spending three weeks at number one on the Hot 100. Despite its origins as an homage to an African American sports figure, it peaked higher on the pop chart (number one) than on the R&B chart (number three), an ironic reversal from its reception in the 1970s. "The greatest love of all became the biggest pop hit from the Whitney Houston album, spending three weeks at number one on the Hot 100."

**I Want to Dance With Somebody (Who Loves Me) (1987)** : This song was a "sequel" to "How Will I Know," specifically requested by Arista, written by George Merrill and Shannon Rubikam, and produced by Narada Michael Walden, who initially found it "too country and western" and rearranged it to be funkier. It was Houston's poppiest song to date, released in May 1987, and quickly reached number one on the Hot 100 in just seven weeks, becoming her fourth straight pop number one, though it peaked at number two on the R&B chart. "I wanna dance with somebody who loves me, was in every way a sequel."

**Didn't We Almost Have It All (1987)** : Another torch ballad co-written by Michael Masser, this song reached number one on the Hot 100 in late September 1987. Like "I Want to Dance with Somebody," it peaked at number two on the R&B chart, continuing the trend of her pop chart dominance outstripping her R&B performance. "Didn't we almost have it all? Another torch-valid, co-written by Michael Masser, reached number one on the Hot 100 in late September 1987."

**So Emotional (1988)** : The third single from her *Whitney* album, this light synth funk track was written by veteran songsmiths Billy Steinberg and Tom Kelly, known for hits for Madonna, Cyndi Lauper, and Heart. It reached number one on the Hot 100 in January 1988, becoming Houston's sixth consecutive number one pop hit, while peaking at number five on Hot Black Singles. "The light synth funk track reached number one on the Hot 100 in January 1988."

**Where Do Broken Hearts Go (1988)** : Produced by Narada Michael Walden and co-written by future Broadway composer Frank Wildhorn, this song topped the Hot 100 in April 1988, marking Houston's seventh consecutive number one pop hit. This achievement broke a chart record, surpassing ties with The Beatles and The Bee Gees, solidifying her unprecedented pop chart dominance, though it peaked at number two on Hot Black Singles. "When Where Do Broken Hearts Go, another Narada Michael Walden production co-written by future Broadway composer Frank Wildhorn topped the Hot 100 in April 1988. Whitney Houston achieved yet another chart record that she holds to this day."

**Love Will Save the Day (1988)** : The fifth and final single from the *Whitney* album, this Latin-flavored dance track was produced by Jellybean Benitez. It notably performed better on the R&B chart, reaching number five, compared to its number nine peak on the Hot 100, making it an exception among the album's pop-dominant singles. "The Latin-flavored Jelly Bean Benitez produced Dance Track Love Will Save the Day."

**One Moment in Time (1988)** : Houston recorded this song as the theme for the 1988 Olympic Games in Seoul, South Korea, after her second album. It reached number five on the Hot 100 but charted more distantly at number 22 on the R&B chart, marking a significant sign of her ebbing black audience after her concentrated pop crossover. "This song reached number five on the Hot 100 and a more distant number 22 on the R&B chart."

**I'm Your Baby Tonight (1990)** : This song rose to number one on both Billboard's Hot 100 and its Hot R&B singles chart in the week ending December 1st, 1990, demonstrating a successful crossover that temporarily bridged her widening gap with R&B audiences. "I'm your baby tonight by Whitney Houston, rose to number one on both billboards Hot 100 and its Hot R&B singles chart."

## curiosities
**Death at 48** : Whitney Houston passed away at the age of 48, one day before the 2012 Grammy Awards, an event that profoundly shocked the music world and continues to prompt discussions and debates about her enduring legacy. "Her death at age 48, one day before the 2012 Grammy Awards, stunned the music world, and left reverberations that are still felt today."

**Rock and Roll Hall of Fame Controversy** : Houston's induction into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame was announced in 2020 but met with controversy from some critics who argued that her primary genre was pop, making her body of work only adjacent to rock. "That rock hall induction has come with its share of controversy, with some critics arguing that Houston is primarily a pop act, whose body of work is only adjacent to rock."

**Crossover Complications** : During her lifetime, Whitney Houston's significant crossover success to a mainstream audience created a complicated relationship with R&B, the African American audience, and the black musical tradition from which she emerged. "Whitney's successful crossover to a mainstream audience complicated her relationship to R&B, the African American audience, and the black musical tradition that spawned her."

**Royal Musical Lineage** : Whitney Houston was descended from gospel and R&B royalty, with her mother, Sissy Houston, being a gospel singer and her cousin, Dionne Warwick, a successful R&B and pop artist, positioning her within a rich musical heritage. "After all, Whitney was descended from gospel and R&B royalty."

**Merv Griffin Show Debut (1983)** : Even before releasing a recording, Houston made an auspicious television debut in 1983 on the Merv Griffin show, introduced by Arista Records President Clive Davis, who at the time verbalized his dreams of crossover success for his 19-year-old protégé. "Even before she had issued a recording, Houston had a very auspicious television debut in 1983 on the Merve Griffin show."

**Strategic Audience Pitch** : The campaign to launch Whitney Houston on the charts was subtly nuanced; while Clive Davis envisioned pop crossover, she was initially pitched largely to African Americans, with her debut performance of "Home" and first single "Hold Me" resonating strongly with black audiences. "She was first pitched largely to African Americans."

**"How Will I Know" - Family Backing Vocals** : For the recording of "How Will I Know," producer Narada Michael Walden invited Houston's mother, Sissy Houston, into the studio to sing backing vocals, contributing to the "pile-up of multi-tracked background vocals" heard on the track. "In fact, the pile-up of multi-tracked background vocals you hear on How Will I Know includes both Whitney and Sissy Houston."

**"How Will I Know" - MTV Video Landmark** : The video for "How Will I Know" was Houston's first to receive appreciable play on MTV. Described as "vintage MTV," it featured Houston strutting through a paint-splattered set with both white and black dancers, and even included an image of her "honorary aunt," Aretha Franklin, signaling an inclusive approach to crossover. "Indeed, its video was the first by Houston to receive any appreciable MTV play."

**"How Will I Know" - Ousting a Relative** : When "How Will I Know" hit number one on the pop chart, it ejected a song by her cousin, Dionne Warwick ("That's What Friends Are For"), marking the first time an artist had succeeded a blood relative at number one in eight years, a phenomenon not seen since The Bee Gees and Andy Gibb in 1978. "When it hit number one pop, Houston actually ejected a song by her cousin, Dion Warwick, the charity mega-single, that's what friends are for."

**Debut Album's Slow Rise** : The *Whitney Houston* LP topped the pop album chart for the first time in its 50th week, making it the slowest rise to number one since Fleetwood Mac's self-titled album a decade prior, indicating a painstaking and successful artist introduction by Arista Records. "In its 50th week, the slowest rise to number one since Fleetwood Max self-titled album a decade earlier."

**Ebbing R&B Audience** : "How Will I Know" was Whitney Houston's last song to top the R&B chart for over four years, signaling to black audiences around early 1986 that her sound was moving away from them, even as her pop success soared. "It would be the last Whitney Houston song to top the R&B chart for more than four years."

**Unprecedented Album Debut Record** : Houston's second album, *Whitney*, released in June 1987, debuted at number one on the album chart, making her the first female artist and the only woman to achieve this feat before the Soundscan system revolutionized chart reporting in the early 90s. This was particularly remarkable as it was only her second album, unlike other pre-Soundscan number-one debut albums which were later career efforts by established male artists. "This made Whitney Houston the first female artist to debut at number one on the album chart and the only woman to do it before sound scan."

**Soul Train Music Awards Booing (1989)** : At the 1989 Soul Train Music Awards, Whitney Houston, despite being at the zenith of her popularity and a nominee that night, was booed by large sections of the audience during a televised program honoring African American music. This incident has become a significant part of "Whitney Houston lore," documented in films like Kevin Macdonald's *Whitney*, which attributes the backlash to a "whitening" campaign by Reverend Al Sharpton and the perception by some Black radio programmers that her music had become "more pop than Urban." "As you can probably hear, the audience at this televised program, honoring achievement in African American music, is actually booing Whitney Houston, a nominee that night, then at the very zenith of her popularity."

**Perception of "Too Much Too Soon"** : Whitney Houston's rapid crossover success, which was unprecedented among black female artists and on par with the dominance of Michael Jackson, Bruce Springsteen, The Beatles, and Elton John, led to a perception that her achievement was "too much too soon." This possibly contributed to her being seen as "a bit too untouchable," leading to the backlash at the Soul Train Awards. "Whitney Houston's success was perceived as perhaps too much too soon."

**Meeting Future Husband at Soul Train Awards** : Observers noted that Whitney Houston reportedly took the 1989 Soul Train incident hard, and it was no coincidence that she met her future husband at the same awards show that night, suggesting a personal impact from the public rejection. "And observers say the fact that she met her future husband on the Soul Train Awards that same night was no coincidence."

"Even the Queen of Soul, Aretha Franklin, she built one of the longest R&B chart streaks in history, including 17 R&B number ones between 1967 and 1977."
