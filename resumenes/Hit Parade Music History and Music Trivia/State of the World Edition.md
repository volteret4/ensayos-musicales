## albums
**Janet Jackson (1982)** : This debut album, released when Janet was 16 and signed to A&M Records, was a modest hit on the R&B charts but lacked personality. It was overseen by five different producers handpicked by her father, Joe Jackson. "critics felt it lacked personality."
**Dream Street (1984)** : Janet Jackson's sophomore album was a synth-driven and scattershot effort that failed to produce any significant chart success. Even with production from her brother Marlon Jackson and vocals from Michael Jackson on "Don't Stand Another Chance," the album's material missed the Hot 100 entirely. "Dream Street, was similarly scattershot."
**Control (1986)** : This album marked a turning point in 80s pop, fusing Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis's steely R&B sound with Jackson's sharp wit and independent-minded lyrics. It was a de facto re-debut for Janet, acting as a roadmap for Black pop and R&B in the second half of the decade and notably diverging from the traditional Jackson family sound towards Prince's electro-funk. The album spun off five top five hits over a year-long run, peaked at number one on the Billboard album chart for two weeks in the summer of 1986, and sold over four million copies, eventually being certified Quintuple Platinum. "Control was a turning point in 80s pop."
**Rhythm Nation 1814 (1989)** : A mega-blockbuster album released in September 1989, it was arguably Janet Jackson's most ambitious work, bridging the music of the 80s and 90s across genres like New Jack Swing, Hip Hop, Lush Soul, and even Hard Rock. The album was a hit machine, opening and closing with a number one hit, generating four chart-toppers in total, and uniquely spanning hits across three calendar years. It tied the record for most top ten hits from a single album and, by one metric, actually bested it, with the potential to have generated an eighth smash hit on the Hot 100. "A watershed in pop, Rhythm Nation bridged the music of the 80s and 90s, and such genres as New Jack Swing, Hip Hop, Lush Soul, and even hard rock."

## songs
**Young Love (1982)** : Janet Jackson's debut single, which she lip-synced on Dick Clark's American Bandstand, peaked at number 64 on the pop charts. This early material, along with a follow-up single, did not achieve significant success, highlighting that music was initially a "backup plan" for the then-16-year-old artist. "The aforementioned young love peaked at number 64."
**Don't Stand Another Chance (1984)** : A song from Janet Jackson's sophomore album, "Dream Street," produced by her brother Marlon Jackson and featuring vocals from her brother Michael. Despite this family assistance, the synth-driven track failed to chart on the Hot 100, indicating Janet's early struggles to establish a chart presence. "it still missed the hot 100 entirely."
**What Have You Done for Me Lately (1986)** : This sassy, prickly, and irresistible track was the lead single from "Control" and gave Janet Jackson her first true chart smash, peaking at number one on Billboard's R&B Chart and number four on the Hot 100 in the spring of 1986. With its metronome beat, staccato synths, and Janet's tight, clipped vocals, it had a sound strongly reminiscent of Prince, who later covered the song live. "What happened done for me lately? The sassy, prickly and irresistible, what have you done for me lately? Was an instant success in the spring of 1986."
**Nasty (1986)** : The iconic and sassy second single from the "Control" album, which became a number three hit in the summer of 1986. This track further showcased Janet's independent attitude and the distinctive sonic touches of producers Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, contributing to the album's successful chart run. "In the summer of 1986 came the iconic even sassy or number three hit, nasty."
**When I Think of You (1986)** : A romantic, breezy, and danceable song that became Janet Jackson's first-ever Hot 100 number one hit in the fall of 1986. This single, from the "Control" album, demonstrated the broadened palette of her new sound and established her as a major pop star. "That was followed in the fall by Janet's first ever Hot 100 number one hit, the romantic, breezy and danceable when I think of you."
**Control (1987)** : The title track from Janet Jackson's album, which reached number five on the Hot 100 by the winter of 1987. In its accompanying music video, a grateful Janet notably called out her star producers, Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, acknowledging their crucial role in her career reboot. "the control album's title track reached number five, and a grateful Janet was calling out her star producers in the music video."
**Let's Wait a While (1987)** : A tender, pro-abstinence ballad that was the fifth single from the "Control" album, reaching number two on the Hot 100 in the spring of 1987. This song represented a softer side of Janet's material and marked the last time a single from her would be quite so chaste in its messaging. "Finally, in the spring of 1987, the tender pro-abstonance ballad, Let's Wait a While, reached number two."
**The Pleasure Principle (1987)** : An acclaimed dance track that became a number one R&B and club record, though it peaked at number 14 on the Hot 100 by the summer of 1987. This single signaled the end of the epic chart run of the "Control" album, which had exceeded all expectations and firmly established Janet Jackson as a top star. "By the summer of 1987, when the acclaimed dance track The Pleasure Principle, a number one R&B and club record, peedered out at number 14 on the Hot 100, it was clear the epic chart run of control was drawing to a close."
**Miss You Much (1989)** : This catchy dance-pop single, released in September 1989, was the first of seven singles from "Rhythm Nation 1814" to reach the top five on the Hot 100, setting an unprecedented chart record. Written by Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis specifically for Janet, it bridged the sassy teen pop sound of "Control" with the clattery, industrial New Jack Swing elements prevalent on the new album, debuting at number 42 on the Hot 100 and shooting to number one five weeks later. "Miss You Much was quite possibly the most pre-sold hit in Janet Jackson's career."
**Rhythm Nation (1989)** : The album's title song, released as its second single, served notice of the "Rhythm Nation 1814" album's scope and ambition, featuring impassioned lyrics about a generation uniting against prejudice. Its long-form music video, depicting Janet leading a precision dance troupe in military-style guard, won her a Grammy, her first, and its sonic elements by Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis echoed rap DJs like Public Enemy's Bomb Squad, subtly sampling Sly and the Family Stone. "Rhythm Nation The Song gave notice that Rhythm Nation the album was going to be a work of scope and ambition."
**Escapade (1990)** : One of the earliest songs written for the "Rhythm Nation" album, this track was a deliberate attempt by Janet, Jimmy Jam, and Terry Lewis to modernize the sound of vintage Motown party anthems like Martha and the Vandellas' "Nowhere to Run." It incorporated mechanized metallic sound effects, shared a sheen with "Miss You Much" and "Nasty," and famously included a shout-out to the city where it was recorded, debuting within the top 40 in January 1990 and reaching number one by March, where it stayed for three weeks. "Escapade was one of the earliest songs written for Rhythm Nation, crafted by the trio in an attempt to modernize the sound of vintage motowning."
**Alright (1990)** : The fourth single from "Rhythm Nation 1814," it was an old-school soul record updated with early 90s production, built on samples of early 70s funk songs like Lynn Collins' "Think (About It)" and BT Express' "Do You Like It." Its video recreated the look of a Busby Berkeley musical, featuring cameos by pre-rock legends Sid Sherees, The Nicholas Brothers, and Cab Calloway, debuting on the Hot 100 in April and peaking at number four in early June. "All right, was like an old school soul record updated with Turn of the 90s production."
**Come Back to Me (1990)** : As the summer single and centerpiece ballad of "Rhythm Nation," this track showcased Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis's elevated production, incorporating a lush string arrangement by Minneapolis arranger Lee Blaske at Janet's suggestion. It represented perhaps the most polished version of Jam and Lewis's "wet sound" production style, characterized by a pouring rain vibe, and reached number two on the Hot 100 by August. "come back to me was perhaps the most polished version of this inundated audio metaphor."
**Black Cat (1990)** : Possibly the most atypical hit in Janet Jackson's catalog, this rock song was built for heavy guitar, with Jackson herself devising the main riff and writing the track alone without Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis. Produced by former Time bandmate Jellybean Johnson, with guitarist Dave Barry contributing, Johnson encouraged Jackson to sing it "like a heavy metal queen," and it debuted within the top 40 in September 1990, becoming the album's third number one single six weeks later. "Jackson came up with the main guitar riff for self."
**State of the World (1989)** : One of several songs on "Rhythm Nation 1814" where Janet Jackson's lyrics directly addressed pressing social issues, drawing inspiration from conscious hip hop, rock, and soul, and the rhythms of New Jack Swing. This track reflected the album's ambition to combine romantic and socially conscious themes. "Many of Jackson's lyrics directly addressed issues like the Stockton Massacre, with song titles like State of the World and the heart felt living in a world they didn't mean."
**Living in a World They Didn't Make (1989)** : A heartfelt song from "Rhythm Nation 1814" that, like "State of the World," directly addressed social issues, spurred by Janet's reaction to events like the Stockton School Yard Massacre. It exemplifies the album's blend of romantic and socially conscious content, aligning with a broader trend of Afro-centrism and militant rap lyrics in the late 1980s. "Many of Jackson's lyrics directly addressed issues like the Stockton Massacre, with song titles like State of the World and the heart felt living in a world they didn't mean."
**The Knowledge (1989)** : A track from "Rhythm Nation 1814" where Janet Jackson demonstrated her versatility by rapping, showcasing the album's broader musical palette and the influence of contemporary hip-hop. This instance of rapping was a part of Jam and Lewis's experimental industrial phase, integrating new sonic elements and techniques. "And, on tracks like The Knowledge, Janet Jackson was even rapping."
**If (1993)** : A future hit for Janet Jackson, featuring a sampling technique developed by Jam, Lewis, and Jackson. For this track, they subtly but unmistakably sampled The Supremes, turning it into the backing track. "Jimmy and Terry turned into the backing track for Janet's 1993 hit, If."
**Got 'Til It's Gone (1997)** : A future hit from Janet Jackson's career, co-created with Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, which continued their established sampling technique. For this song, they looped Joni Mitchell, one of Janet's all-time favorite artists, into the track. "Whom they looped into the 1997 hit, got to let's go."

## curiosities
**Birth and Family Background** : Born Janet D'Amida Jo Jackson in 1966, she was the youngest of the Jackson family siblings. Despite being the "baby of the family," she seemed to possess the greatest potential for stardom, though her initial career path was somewhat undecided between singing and acting. "Born Janet D'Amida Jo Jackson in 1966. She seemed to possess the greatest potential."
**Early Acting Career** : Before committing fully to music, Janet Jackson had a significant career as a child star on television for half a decade. She played the recurring character Penny on the smash mid-70s sitcom "Good Times" and the long-running character Charlene, Willis's girlfriend, on "Different Strokes," even singing one of her songs on the latter show. "She emerged as a child star on the Smash mid-70s sitcom Good Times, playing recurring character Penny."
**A&M Records Signing and American Bandstand Debut (1982)** : At just 16 years old in 1982, Janet Jackson was signed to A&M Records, the artist-friendly label run by Herb Albert and Jerry Moss. She appeared on Dick Clark's American Bandstand to lip-sync her debut single, "Young Love," though at the time, she viewed music more as a backup plan to her acting career. "When Janet Jackson appeared on Dick Clark's American Bandstand in 1982, she was all of 16 years old, and had just been signed to A&M Records, the storied artist-friendly label run by Herb Albert and Jerry Moss."
**Breaking Away from Family Management (Mid-1980s)** : Feeling stifled by her father Joe Jackson's hands-on management, Janet realized she needed to treat her career as more than a "Jackson family brand extension." In the mid-80s, she quietly terminated business affairs with her family and sought to establish her own artistic identity, a challenging but necessary step for her independence. "I just wanted to get out of the house and get out from under my father, which was one of the most difficult things that I had to do."
**Creative Collaboration with Jimmy Jam & Terry Lewis** : Critical to rebooting her career, Janet decided to work with a single production team, choosing Minneapolis-based duo Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, whom she had met through their time with The Time. She traveled to Minneapolis to co-write music with them at their homegrown Flight Time studios, as they believed involving her in the creative process was key to igniting her excitement for being an artist. "Perhaps most important, Jimmy and Terry found out that the newly independent Janet would be willing to travel to Minneapolis and co-write music with them in their homegrown studio, Flight Time, named after their former 70s funk band."
**"Control" as a Statement of Independence** : Janet purposefully titled her third album "Control," indicating her newfound ownership of her destiny, and even opened the album with a spoken-word statement: "Control of what I say, control of what I do. And this time I'm gonna do it my way." This declaration solidified her artistic independence and marked a clear departure from her family's musical legacy. "And Janet called it, Control, a pointed title about taking ownership of her own destiny."
**"Rhythm Nation 1814" Title Meaning** : Janet Jackson explained that the "1814" in her album title "Janet Jackson's Rhythm Nation 1814" referred to the year the American National Anthem was penned by Francis Scott Key. This historical reference underpinned the album's themes of national unity and social consciousness. "She would later explain that 1814 was the year the American National Anthem was penned by Francis Scott Key."
**Inspiration for Album Interludes** : For "Rhythm Nation 1814," Janet Jackson, Jimmy Jam, and Terry Lewis incorporated short tracks called "interludes," some featuring brief bits of speech and others more ambitious audio collages. This concept was directly inspired by De La Soul's seminal Spring 1989 album, "Three Feet High and Rising," which had helped popularize the use of short skits on Hip Hop albums. "Here, Jackson, Jam, and Lewis were taking their cue from a then relatively new concept in the world of rap. Dayla Soles' Seminole Spring 1989 album, Three Feet High and Rising, helped popularize the concept of the short, Skit on Hip Hop albums."
**Response to Stockton School Yard Massacre (1989)** : A pivotal moment for Janet Jackson in shaping the themes of "Rhythm Nation 1814" was her January 1989 viewing of televised news reports about the mass shootings at Cleveland Elementary School, known as the Stockton School Yard Massacre. This then-rare American tragedy, involving the killing of elementary school children, profoundly influenced her and her collaborators to write songs addressing social issues. "What ultimately tipped the scales for Jackson was her January 1989 viewing of televised news reports about the mass shootings at a Cleveland Elementary School known as the Stockton School Yard Massacre."
**Sampling Technique Foreshadowed by "Rhythm Nation"** : The subtle sampling of Sly and the Family Stone's "Thank You (Falettinme Be Mice Elf Agin)" in Janet Jackson's "Rhythm Nation" single foreshadowed a key production technique for her future collaborations with Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis. This method involved building subsequent Janet albums around subtle but unmistakable classic samples, such as The Supremes for "If" (1993) and Joni Mitchell for "Got 'Til It's Gone" (1997). "And flashing forward in Janet Jackson's career, this single foreshadowed a sampling technique that Jam, Lewis and Jackson would pursue on their future collaborations."

## albums
**Off the Wall (1979)** : Michael Jackson's solo breakthrough album generated four top ten hits, making it the first album by a solo artist to achieve this feat, following Fleetwood Mac's "Rumors" and the "Saturday Night Fever" soundtrack. By the end of 1980, this success solidified Michael Jackson as one of the biggest artists in pop music. "Michael's late 1979 solo breakthrough, off the wall, generated four top ten hits, the first album by a solo artist to do so."
**Thriller (1982)** : This mega-blockbuster album eclipsed "Off the Wall" and set a new benchmark in chart history when seven of its nine tracks became top ten hits, all issued as retail singles from late 1982 to early 1984. The album featured two number one hits ("Billie Jean" and "Beat It"), three top five hits ("The Girl Is Mine," "Wanna Be Startin' Something," "Thriller"), and two other top ten hits ("Human Nature," "PYT"), totaling five top five hits out of a staggering seven top ten. "The album set a new benchmark when seven of its nine tracks became top ten hits, all of them issued as retail singles from late 1982 to early 1984."
**Bad (1987)** : Michael Jackson's follow-up to "Thriller" was another monumental success, with a stunning five of its singles reaching number one, setting a chart record that has been tied but remains unbroken. While the album achieved six top ten hits, it narrowly missed matching "Thriller's" perfect streak when its sixth single, "Another Part of Me," stalled at number eleven, although its seventh single, "Smooth Criminal," did reach number seven. "While a stunning five of Bad singles reached number one, yet another chart record of Michael's that has been tied but remains unbroken."

## curiosities
**Early Solo Success (1971-1980)** : Michael Jackson scored a handful of solo hits as early as 1971, but his serious chart dominance began at the dawn of the 1980s with his late 1979 breakthrough album, "Off the Wall." By the end of 1980, this album's four top ten hits had established him as one of pop's biggest artists. "Though Michael scored a handful of solo hits as early as 1971, his serious chart dominance began at the dawn of the 1980s."
**Chart Record Setter** : Michael Jackson's hit-making career set an even greater number of chart records than his family's group efforts, many of which he still holds today. His albums "Thriller" and "Bad" notably yielded seven and six top ten hits respectively, with "Bad" also setting a record of five number one singles. "Michael's celebrated hit making career, one that would set an even greater number of chart records, many of which he still holds today."
**Impact of "Leaving Neverland" Documentary** : The HBO documentary "Leaving Neverland" released allegations of sexual abuse against Michael Jackson, leading some to prefer not hearing his music. The podcast acknowledges and respects this sentiment while maintaining that his music is indispensable to discussing pop chart history. "In the wake of the allegations of sexual abuse made by the recent HBO documentary, Leaving Neverland, there are those who would prefer not to hear Michael Jackson's music."
**Chart Competition Avoidance with Janet Jackson** : Whether by happenstance or Jackson family design, Michael and his sister Janet generally avoided directly competing with each other on the charts. Michael's "Bad" album hit stores just weeks after Janet's last "Control" single dropped out of the top 40, allowing him to embark on his own string of hits from fall 1987 through summer 1988 without direct overlap. "Whether by happenstance or by Jackson family design, Michael and Janet generally avoided directly competing with each other on the charts."

## curiosities
**Origins in Minneapolis Funk (1970s)** : James Harris III (professionally known as Jimmy Jam) and Terry Lewis began their careers as a keyboardist and bassist, respectively, for a 70s Minneapolis funk band called Flight Time. This early experience in the Minneapolis music scene laid the groundwork for their future production and songwriting success. "James Harris III, known professionally as Jimmy Jam, and his partner, Terry Lewis, started as a keyboardist and bassist respectively for a 70s Minneapolis funk band called Flight Time."
**Dismissal from The Time (1983)** : Prince fired Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis from The Time in 1983 after they missed a gig with the band. Prince considered their outside production work for another act, the SOS Band, to be disloyal. "Prince fired them in 1983 after they missed a gig with the band. And, more disloyally in Prince's mind, he found out they were doing production work for another act."
**Breakthrough as Producers with SOS Band (1983)** : Following their dismissal from The Time, Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis achieved a breakthrough as producers with the SOS Band's song "Just Be Good to Me." This track became a sizable number two R&B hit in late summer 1983 and was the first example of their distinctive funk-driven electro-R&B style, establishing them as mid-80s R&B hitmakers. "Just be good to me was a number two R&B hit in the late summer of 1983 and a breakthrough for Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis as producers."
**Mid-80s R&B Hitmakers** : After their breakthrough, Jam and Lewis adapted their distinctive funk-driven electro-R&B sound for a range of artists, becoming prominent mid-80s R&B hitmakers. Their production credits during this period included successful work for Cheryl Lynn, Alexander O'Neal, Thelma Houston, and Cherrelle (known for "I Didn't Mean to Turn You On"). "Jam and Lewis became mid 80s R&B hitmakers for the likes of Cheryl Lynn, Alexander O'Neal, Thelma Houston, and I didn't mean to turn you on singer Cheryl."
**Creative Approach to Janet Jackson's Career Reboot** : Around 1985, Jam and Lewis expressed interest in working with Janet Jackson, believing her attitude was missing from her early records despite recognizing the quality of her voice. They aimed to involve her directly in the creative process to excite her about making music and being an artist, which proved critical to her career reboot with the "Control" album. "By involving her in the creative process, we thought we could get her excited about making a record and excited about being an artist."
**"Industrial Phase" Production (1989)** : By 1989, Jimmy and Terry Lewis entered what could be called their "industrial phase," characterized by an obsession with factory-like sounds of grinding gears and worrying motors. These unique sonic effects were incorporated into the production of Janet Jackson's "Rhythm Nation 1814" album, underlying its songs and adding richness and detail. "By 1989, Jimmy and Terry were entering what you might call their industrial phase, obsessed with the factory-like sounds of grinding gears and worrying motors."
**Development of the "Wet Sound" Production** : Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis had been perfecting their "wet sound" production style for over half a decade, a keyboard-driven vibe that translates the feeling of pouring rain into a pop song. They scored multiple hits refining this sound, including "Tender Love" by The Force M.D.'s (1986), "Human" by The Human League (1986), and "Can You Stand The Rain" by New Edition (1988), with Janet Jackson's "Come Back to Me" (1990) representing its most polished version. "Jam and Lewis had actually scored multiple hits, rebooting and refining this keyboard driven wet production sound, dating back some five years."
