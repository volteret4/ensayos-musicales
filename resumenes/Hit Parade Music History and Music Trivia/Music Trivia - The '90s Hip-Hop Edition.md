## albums
**Spirits Having Flown (1979)** : This album featured three consecutive number one hits for the Bee Gees as artists, marking their final chart-toppers in this capacity. The three number ones from this album were "Too Much Heaven", "Tragedy", and "Love You Inside Out". "That was the third of three straight number ones from the spirits having flown album after too much heaven and tragedy."

## songs
**Jive Talkin' (1975)** : A number one hit in the summer of 1975, this song was written by Barry Gibb while he was crossing the Julia Tuddle Causeway in Miami, Florida. The rhythm of the slats on the bridge, which connects Miami to Biscayne Bay, is said to have inspired the song's irresistible rhythm. The lore suggests that the specific "trick, a trick, a" sound heard at the beginning of the song only occurs if one is speeding over the bridge above the 55 mile an hour limit. This hit was a pivotal moment in the Bee Gees' career, marking a transition into their new phase as "disco kings". "In the Bee Gees career, this hit was a bridge to the Gibb's new phase as disco kings."
**Spicks and Specks (1966)** : This song is mentioned as an example of the Bee Gees' early Australian material from the 1960s, showcasing their sound before their later disco hits like "Jive Talkin'". "Spicks and specs, Bee Gees from Jive Talking Bee Gees, from later Bee Gees."
**I Was a Lover, A Leader of Men (c. 1963-1964)** : An early song from the Bee Gees' 1960s Australian period, this track already showcased a distinctive vocal quality in the Gibbs' voices, even when singing in a normal middle-range male voice, preceding their more famous falsetto. "You can already hear it's not just the falsetto. The Gibbs voices have a certain brain quality that, you know, even when they're singing in a normal middle-range male voice, it's got an a to it that comes out in their vocals."
**I Started a Joke (1968)** : Robin Gibb sang lead on this track, which along with "How Can You Mend a Broken Heart," demonstrated the Bee Gees' voices progressively reaching higher ranges throughout the 1960s, leading up to their full-blown falsetto leap in 1975. "It really had been ramping up all through the 60s on the songs that Robyn sang, lead on, like I started a joke on tracks like How Can You Mendebroke and Heart."
**How Can You Mend a Broken Heart (1971)** : A number one hit on the Hot 100 in 1971, this song is an example of the Bee Gees' vocal progression in the 1960s, where their voices were getting progressively higher on each hit before their full falsetto emergence. "It really had been ramping up all through the 60s on the songs that Robyn sang, lead on, like I started a joke on tracks like How Can You Mendebroke and Heart."
**Massachusetts (1967)** : A "pretty big hit" from the late 1960s for the Bee Gees, preceding their disco era, and notably shares its title with the state from which a trivia contestant was calling. "Massachusetts is the title of a pretty big bg sit from the late 60s."
**Too Much Heaven (1979)** : One of three consecutive number one hits from the *Spirits Having Flown* album in 1979. It was succeeded by "Tragedy" and then "Love You Inside Out." "That was the third of three straight number ones from the spirits having flown album after too much heaven and tragedy."
**Tragedy (1979)** : One of three consecutive number one hits from the *Spirits Having Flown* album in 1979, following "Too Much Heaven" and preceding "Love You Inside Out." "That was the third of three straight number ones from the spirits having flown album after too much heaven and tragedy."
**Love You Inside Out (1979)** : This song was the Bee Gees' last number one hit as artists, securing the third of three consecutive chart-toppers from their *Spirits Having Flown* album in 1979. It was replaced at the number one spot by Donna Summer's "Hot Stuff". The Gibbs continued to write number one songs for other artists in the 1980s. "Love You Inside Out was their last as artists."

## curiosities
**Early Vocal Style (1960s)** : In their 1960s material, including their Australian recordings, the Bee Gees initially aimed for a sound similar to the Everly Brothers. However, their unique vocal quality, described as having a "brain quality" and an "a to it" even in their middle-range male voices, began to emerge early on, before their renowned falsetto. "They had a tone that, okay, it's hard to hear when they were trying to sound like the Everly Brothers in 1960."
**Falsetto Evolution (1960s-1975)** : The Bee Gees' use of high-pitched vocals ramped up throughout the 1960s, notably in songs like "I Started a Joke" and "How Can You Mend a Broken Heart," where Robin Gibb sang lead. This progression culminated in a dramatic "leap over the cliff into full-blown falsetto in 75."
**Cultural Impact in Dark Times (1970s)** : The Bee Gees' chart dominance occurred during a challenging period in American history, marked by 1970s inflation, the post-Watergate era, and high crime rates. Their "high falsetto cheer" from Australia and the Isle of Man acted as a "balm" or a "happy place" for listeners during these tough times, serving as an "antidote to the toxicity" of the news. "There is probably something about the high falsetto cheer of these lads from Australia with a little bit of the Isle of Man that inherently acts as a bomb."
**Gibbs as Songwriters for Other Artists (1980s)** : After "Love You Inside Out" marked their final number one hit as artists in 1979, the Gibb brothers continued their songwriting success by penning number one hits for other prominent artists in the 1980s, including Barbara Streisand, Kenny Rogers, and Dolly Parton. "And while the Gibbs would write 80s number ones for Barbara Streisand and Kenny Rogers and Dolly Parton, Love You Inside Out was their last as artists."
**Nine Number Ones on Hot 100** : The Bee Gees achieved significant chart success with a total of nine number one hits on the Hot 100. These ranged from "How Can You Mend a Broken Heart" in 1971 to the numerous disco hits featured on the *Saturday Night Fever* soundtrack in 1977 and 1978, culminating with their 1979 hits from *Spirits Having Flown*. "I talked about all nine of the bg's number ones on the hot 100 from How Can You Man To Broken Heart in 1971 to the many disco hits on Saturday Night Fever in 1977 and 78."

**Julia Tuddle Causeway - Jive Talkin' Lore** : The rhythm of the slats on the Julia Tuddle Causeway, a bridge connecting Miami to Biscayne Bay, is famously believed to have inspired the "trick, a trick, a" sound at the beginning of the Bee Gees' 1975 hit "Jive Talkin'." According to popular lore, this specific rhythm is only audible when driving over the bridge at speeds exceeding the 55 mile per hour limit, suggesting the Bee Gees might have been speeding. "the rhythm that the Bee Gees use at the beginning of Give Talking clearly indicates they were speeding over the bridge because it only makes that trick, a trick, a sound if you go above the 55 mile an hour speed limit."

"A little bit of trivia when the movie came out, it was rated R. And so I couldn't actually see it despite the fact that my cousins were playing the soundtrack nonstop."
