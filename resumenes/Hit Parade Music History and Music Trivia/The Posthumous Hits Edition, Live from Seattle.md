**(Sittin' On) The Dock of the Bay (1968)** : This song was Redding's sole number one hit as an artist on both the Hot 100 and the R&B chart, and his only top 20 pop hit, becoming his perennial radio standard. He began writing it in the summer of 1967 in San Francisco after his Monterey Pop Festival performance, and recorded it in December 1967 with co-writer and guitarist Steve Cropper. Two days after recording, Redding died in a plane crash near Madison, Wisconsin. Three months later, for the week ending March 16, 1968, it hit number one, marking the first posthumous chart-topper in Hot 100 history. The song represented a deliberate attempt by Redding to cross over with a sparer, more folksy aesthetic, drawing influences from artists like Bob Dylan and The Beatles, a departure from his prior work on Stax and Volt. "Sitting on the dock of the bay was not only Redding's sole number one as an artist, on both the hot 100 and the R&B chart. It was his only top 20 pop hit and remains his perennial radio standard."
**Try A Little Tenderness (1966)** : This 1966 song was persuasively positioned as a civil rights era song of resistance. It was a "hard driving kind of rough sounding soul" song that served as a "show stopping, bring down the house closing song of his life sets." "a wonderful Atlantic piece about 1966's tri-a-little tenderness, in which you position that song very persuasively as a civil rights era song of resistance."
**(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction (1967)** : This song exemplified Otis Redding's hard-driving, rough-sounding soul style during his intense and creatively explosive "Imperial period" in 1967. It contributed to the strain on his voice that later required surgery. "that hard driving kind of rough sounding soul that we hear in respect, satisfaction, try a little tenderness."

## curiosities
**Career Height and Experimentation (1967)** : In 1967, Otis Redding was at the height of his powers, in an "Imperial period" of incredible creative explosion and experimentation. He had gained confidence from a successful European tour with the Stax Revue and an "incredible performance" at the Monterey Pop Festival, leading to songs like "(Sittin' On) The Dock of the Bay" where he experimented with a folksier aesthetic. "I think of 67 for him as almost what you often refer to on the show as the Imperial period, that he's on the verge or on the brink of that, that moment of incredible creative explosion and experimentation where an artist can kind of do anything and his fans will go with him."
**Vocal Cord Surgery (1967)** : The hard-driving, rough-sounding soul performances of songs like "Respect," "(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction," and "Try A Little Tenderness" took a toll on Redding's voice, necessitating vocal cord surgery. He had to rest his voice and body for five weeks, after which he went into the studio to record "(Sittin' On) The Dock of the Bay." "try a little tenderness, has taken its toll on his voice. And so, he needs to get surgery on his vocal cords."
**Themes of Labor and Resistance in Music** : The song "(Sittin' On) The Dock of the Bay" can be interpreted as social commentary about day laborers, reflecting the persona of someone who has left home for the promise of work that might not be available. It also embodies the idea of resting and thinking as a form of resistance for artists in an industry built on black labor. "I do see it as a song of social commentary. That's in some ways about day laborers."
**Advocacy for Black Artists' Rights** : Otis Redding commented on the conditions of black workers both on and off stage. During a 1966 performance at the Whiskey-A-Go-Go in Hollywood, he highlighted the hard work musicians put in. He also proposed forming an all-black entertainers' union with James Brown to prevent exploitation by white promoters and managers, aspiring to own his own label and be his own boss. "He also apparently proposed the idea of an all black entertainer's union to James Brown."
**Pioneer of Introspective Soul** : Redding's work, particularly "(Sittin' On) The Dock of the Bay," showcases a quieter, more introspective soul aesthetic, contrasting with the bombastic performances often associated with soul. This approach, focused on turning inward and self-reflection, positions him as a forerunner to later artists like Al Green, Isaac Hayes, and Minnie Riperton. "we can see Otis is working in this kind of mode of introspective soul."

**Posthumous Hit Definition** : A posthumous hit is defined as a song or album recorded by an artist that achieves chart success after their death. There have been dozens, perhaps hundreds, of such hits, ranging from Eddie Cochran in 1960 to contemporary artists, often serving as a collective eulogy where the public focuses grief on a particular work. "A posthumous hit is specifically defined as a song or an album recorded by an artist that makes the charts after his or her death."
