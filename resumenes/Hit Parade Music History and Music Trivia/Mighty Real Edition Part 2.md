# artist - David Bowie

## songs
**Fame (1975) - First Hot 100 No. 1 by an Out Performer (With an Asterisk)** : Bowie's 1975 American No. 1 hit came three years after his public 1972 self-outing in Melody Maker, which in theory makes it the first Hot 100 chart-topper by an out performer. That claim is complicated because Bowie kept revising his sexual identity over the years. Fame and Let's Dance are his only two US chart-toppers, and with a small asterisk they qualify as the first No. 1s by an artist who had publicly come out. "In theory, that would make Bowie's 1975 American number one hit, theme the first hot 100 chart-topper by an out performer."
**Let's Dance (1983) - Return to No. 1** : Bowie returned to No. 1 on the Hot 100 with Let's Dance just weeks after his Rolling Stone interview with Kurt Loder, in which he called his public declaration of bisexuality "the biggest mistake I ever made." It is his second and last US chart-topper. "Just weeks before he would return to number one on the hot 100 with, let's dance."
**Queen Bitch - Queer-Accented Song** : One of Bowie's many queer-accented songs. Along with others, it challenged the public about how gender was represented. "Bowie's many queer accented songs from Queen Bitch to Boys Keep Swinging have challenged the public about how gender was represented."
**Boys Keep Swinging - Queer-Accented Song** : Another of Bowie's queer-accented songs that gay historians cite as challenging public ideas about how gender was represented. "Boys, boys keep swinging, boys don't like to work yet."

## curiosities
**Melody Maker Self-Outing - January 22, 1972** : In the January 22, 1972 issue of England's Melody Maker magazine, Bowie said he was gay. At the time he was newly married and had just fathered his first child. The self-outing was a big deal, and Bowie was adopted as a gay icon in both Britain and America. Gay historians still regard it as a signal moment for LGBTQ acceptance in pop music. "I'm gay and always have been. Even when I was David Jones."
**Cameron Crowe Interview (1976) - Bisexuality as Career Tool** : In a 1976 interview with the journalist Cameron Crowe, Bowie declared, somewhat cynically, that he was bisexual but had used that fact to his advantage. In effect he made his queerness sound like a matter of image and career. "it's true I am bisexual, but I can't deny that I've used that fact very well."
**Kurt Loder Rolling Stone Interview (1983) - "Closet Heterosexual"** : Seven years after the Crowe interview, Bowie told Rolling Stone's Kurt Loder that his public declaration of bisexuality was a mistake and that he had always been "a closet heterosexual." The interview ran just weeks before Let's Dance hit No. 1. "the biggest mistake I ever made."
**2002 Statement - Bisexual but No Banners** : As late as 2002, Bowie said he did consider himself bisexual but had no wish to act as a public standard-bearer. "had no inclination to hold any banners."
**Barry Walters' Billboard Appreciation (2016)** : After Bowie's death in 2016, critic and journalist Barry Walters wrote an appreciation for Billboard magazine about the 1972 revelation. Walters is one of many queer historians who credit Bowie with making history. "he used his outsider stance not simply to be breathtaking, he also built bridges."

# curiosity

**Not Out at No. 1 - Lesley Gore and Billy Preston** : Both Lesley Gore and Billy Preston scored No. 1 hits, but neither was out at the time. In the rock era's first decades, the artists who were out, from Jobriath to Sylvester, did not top the Hot 100 or the album chart. "both Leslie Gore and Billy Preston did score number one hits but neither was out at the time."
**Heterosexualization of Bisexual Artists** : A Janis Joplin historian observed that bisexual artists were often "heterosexualized." The same idea applies to Billie Joe Armstrong and Lady Gaga, whose identities the industry flattened into straight-presenting images. "bisexual artists were to quote one Janice Joplin historian often heterosexualized."
**Back-to-Back Out Performers at No. 1 (January–February 1992)** : Elton John and George Michael's live Don't Let the Sun Go Down on Me was followed at No. 1 by Right Said Fred's I'm Too Sexy, putting two fully out performers (Elton and Richard Fairbrass) at No. 1 consecutively. "Coincidentally, two such singles went to number one in early 1992 back to back."
**2012 Billboard 200 Study - No Out Artist at No. 1** : A 2012 historical study of the Billboard 200, prompted by Adam Lambert's new album, found that arguably no artist from the rock era through 2010 had a No. 1 album while out. The ten closeted album chart-toppers it identified were Elton John, Janis Ian, Freddie Mercury, George Michael, Jonathan Knight, Michael Stipe, Ricky Martin, Lance Bass, Luther Vandross and Clay Aiken. Topping the album chart proved even harder for out queer artists than topping the songs chart. "The only thing they all have in common is none was fully out when they scored a number one album."
**Back-to-Back Queer No. 1s (October 2022)** : Steve Lacey's Bad Habit and Sam Smith and Kim Petras's Unholy held No. 1 consecutively. They were two very different songs, both proudly queer, and showed how far the charts and pop listeners had come. "two wildly different songs that were both proudly queer, showed how far the charts, pop listeners, and indeed America itself had traveled."
**Zillennial Openness and Gender-Fluid Straight Stars** : The zillennial generation openly shares gender presentation, sexual orientation and pronouns. Straight artists such as Harry Styles and Bad Bunny present themselves as more sexually fluid and wear gender-nonconforming costumes onstage. For queer chart-toppers, coming out is now not only possible but mostly expected. "it is now not only possible for chart-topping musicians to come out, it is mostly expected."
**Queer Chart History Arc - 1950s to 2020s** : Queer chart history runs from Johnnie Ray and Little Richard in the 1950s to Kim Petras and Chappell Roan in the 2020s. "shows the rainbow arc of history bending ever upward."
