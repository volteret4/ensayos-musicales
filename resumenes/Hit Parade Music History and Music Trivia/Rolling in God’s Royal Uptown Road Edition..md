# curiosity

**Pop Chart History - Decades Theory** : A "grand unified theory" of pop music suggests that decades can be broadly divided into two halves, with each half defined by a distinct dominant sound or trend. This system, despite its inherent oversimplification, is useful for understanding major pop trends and has been applicable to most decades of the last half-century. "I believe decades of music can be divided very broadly into halves. The first half of a decade is mostly one thing, the second half another."
**Pivot Record Theory** : A key element in pop chart history is the concept of a "pivot record"—one or two chart-topping hits occurring in the middle of a decade that signal an imminent shift in major pop trends. These records serve as cultural cues, indicating a turning tide in musical styles. Examples include The Beatles' US breakthrough in 1964 and Gwen Stefani's "Hollaback Girl" in 2005. "And then there's one more key element. In the middle of each decade, there's what I call a pivot record, a chart topping hit or two that signals that a trend shift is about to occur."
**Billboard Hot 100 - YouTube Data Inclusion (2013)** : In February 2013, Billboard significantly altered its Hot 100 methodology by incorporating YouTube data for the first time. This new rule counted any video playing at least 30 seconds of an original recording towards a song's chart position, a change that immediately impacted the charts and "kicked off a year in which cultural bias on the charts was laid bare." "just weeks into 2013, Billboard finally brought YouTube data to the Hot 100, and kicked off a year in which cultural bias on the charts was laid bare."
**2010s - The Technological Tens** : The 2010s are defined as "the technological tens," a period that commenced with the widespread adoption of digital downloads and the rise of "Queens of Pop," and concluded with the dominance of streaming jukeboxes and the emergence of "Kings of the Cloud." This decade's popular songs were profoundly shaped by evolving digital mediums and music consumption patterns. "Today on Hit Parade, we will consider the decade that was, the technological tens, a period that opened with the digital download and the Queens of Pop, and closed with the streaming jukebox and the Kings of the Cloud."
**2010s - Non-Stop Dance Party Start** : The transition from the late 2000s ("aughts") to the 2010s was akin to the shift from the 1970s to the 1980s, characterized by pop music providing a "non-stop dance party" to distract from the lingering effects of the Great Recession and a wobbly economy. "The 2010 Dance Party USA. The end of the aughts and the turn of the tens was a little bit like the turn from the 1970s to the 1980s."
**Female Pop Star Dominance (2011)** : The year 2011 was a period of strong female presence in pop, with women holding the number one spot on the Hot 100 for an impressive 45 consecutive weeks, mostly in lead roles. This included both rising stars like Lady Gaga and Adele, and 90s veterans such as Britney Spears and Christina Aguilera, while even male-fronted hits often featured prominent female contributions. "2011 was a good time to be a female pop star. For most of the year, a woman was number one on the Hot 100."
**The New Monoculture (2012)** : The 2010s witnessed a "digital reboot of the so-called monoculture," where social media transformed quirky songs into universal hits. This era was defined by a combination of catchiness and virality, creating a "knock-on effect" where social sharing influenced traditional metrics like sales and airplay, even before video views were directly factored into the Hot 100. "It was a digital reboot of the so-called monoculture, critics word for the pre-digital era of the late 20th century, when TVs had fewer channels, consumers had fewer entertainment choices, and a Big Hit song was the lingua franca of a wide swath of America."
**Billboard Hot 100 - Video Views Exclusion (2012)** : As of 2012, video views did not factor into the Hot 100 chart calculations. This meant that songs like Psy's "Gangnam Style," despite accumulating over a billion views on YouTube, could not leverage that immense popularity to reach number one on the Billboard chart. "Interestingly, as of 2012, video views did not factor into the hot 100."
**Chart Racial Makeup (2013)** : For the first time in the Hot 100's history, the entire year of 2013 passed without any number one hits led by African Americans. This occurred despite charts incorporating more cross-genre data, and many of the biggest hits being indebted to R&B and hip hop but fronted by white performers, often in forms of "blue-eyed soul" with black artists in supporting roles. "For the first time in the Hot 100's history, a whole year, all of 2013, went by with no number one hits led by African Americans."
**Peak Woman Power on Charts (2014)** : The fall of 2014 represented a "dizzying peak" of "woman power" on the charts, with women occupying the entire top five of the Hot 100 for seven consecutive weeks. This marked the longest period of female control in the chart's history, featuring hits from artists like Meghan Trainor, Iggy Azalea, Rita Ora, and Taylor Swift. "Women occupied the entire top five for seven consecutive weeks, the longest period of female control in the chart's history."
**Hot 100 Shift to Male Dominance (2015)** : Following a period of peak female power on the charts in 2014, the Hot 100 pivoted significantly in 2015, shifting towards male dominance. Mark Ronson and Bruno Mars's "Uptown Funk" was the first number one by men in approximately five months, inaugurating a four-year period during which the Hot 100 was largely dominated by male artists. "For the next four years, the Hot 100 was dominated by men."
**Streaming Overtakes Downloads (2015)** : The year 2015 marked a crucial pivot for the Hot 100, as it definitively moved away from relying on dollar downloads and embraced streaming as the primary metric. With Spotify overtaking Apple's iTunes and Apple launching its own streaming service, Apple Music, this shift fundamentally reshaped how chart success was measured and influenced the types of artists and genres that topped the charts. "Because 2015 is when the Hot 100 shifted away from the dollar download and toward the stream."

**2013 No Black Artist Number One** : In 2013, there were no black artists leading a number one hit on the charts, highlighting a significant lack of diversity at the top of popular music at that time. "just four years after 2013, when, remember, no black artist lead a number one hit."
**White Pop Acts Supporting Rappers (2017)** : By 2017, a remarkable turnaround had occurred from 2013's chart landscape, with white pop acts like Justin Bieber beginning to support rappers on their hooks and tracks, indicating a cross-genre collaboration trend. "Now, white pop acts were supporting the rappers, Justin Bieber, yes, him again, saying yet another hook on I'm the One."
**Women's Chart Strategy in Streaming/Rap Era** : For women, particularly pop artists, to effectively compete in the streaming and rap-dominated chart world of the late 2010s, they needed to adopt a strategy of becoming "more lyrically acute and more personal." "For women, particularly pop artists, to effectively compete in this streaming and rap dominated chart world, they would have to get more lyrically acute and more personal."
**2019 Chart Pendulum Swing** : As 2019 began, the charts were undergoing another "half-decade pendulum swing." Initially, some artists like Ariana Grande and Halsey fused "centrist pop with hip hop cadence." However, within weeks, the Hot 100 was being led by singles with "no overt connections to rap." "It seemed as 2019 got underway that the charts were undergoing another half-decade pendulum swing."
**Gen Z Disregard for Genre** : A new generation of Gen Z fans "don't seem to care what genre category the song belongs in," as exemplified by their adoration for the genre-bending "Old Town Road." This signals a potential shift in how music is categorized and consumed by younger audiences. "Old Town Road was utterly adored by a new generation of Gen Z fans who don't seem to care what genre category the song belongs in."
**Wide-Ranging Hot 100 (Post "Old Town Road")** : Following the "youth quake" of "Old Town Road" in the summer of 2019, the Hot 100 became "admirably wide ranging," topped by diverse songs from artists like Billie Eilish, Lizzo, Selena Gomez, and Lewis Capaldi, disproving "reports of pure pop's demise." "Since the youth quake of Old Town Road this summer, the hot 100 has been admirably wide ranging."
**"Winner of the Tens" Debate** : The question of "who won the tens" (2010s) considered several artists: Adele (piles of sales, two LPs), Taylor Swift (more sales, Hot 100 resistance late decade), Drake (epic late 10s, struggled early 10s), and Bruno Mars (savvy adjuster to hip hop). Ultimately, Rihanna was declared the "Pop Dominator of the Decade." "What pop act best navigated these heavy swings in the zeitgeist?"
