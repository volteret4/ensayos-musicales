## albums
**Bob Dylan (1962) - Debut Album** : Released on Columbia Records in March 1962, this self-titled debut album did not make the charts and sold approximately 5,000 copies in its first year, barely breaking even. It featured two original songs. "It didn't make the charts at all. It sold about 5,000 copies in its first year, and barely broke even."
**The Free Wheelin' Bob Dylan (1963) - Influential Second LP** : Dylan's second album, released in 1963, featured songs like "Blowin' in the Wind" that immediately garnered acclaim from the New York folk scene. Though initially not charting nationally, its catalog proved to be a major boost for Dylan through covers by other artists. "it wasn't until his second LP, 1963's The Free Wheel in Bob Dylan, that he wrote songs that would inspire scores of other performers to emulate him."
**Bringing It All Back Home (1965) - Electric Breakthrough** : Released in early 1965, this album marked Dylan's "going electric." It generated his first ever pop Top 40 hit, "Subterranean Homesick Blues," and eventually became his first Top 10 LP, reaching number 6 on the album chart. "Dylan had also gone electric on his early 65 album bringing it all back home."
**Highway 61 Revisited (1965) - All-Electric LP** : Released just weeks after "Bringing It All Back Home," this was a virtually all-electric LP. It generated "Like a Rolling Stone," widely acclaimed as his greatest single and his biggest pop hit, and climbed to number 3 on the album chart by November 1965, setting a new high water mark for Dylan. "Highway 61 Revisited, a virtually all electric LP that generated the song both widely acclaimed as his greatest single and incidentally, also his biggest pop hit, Like A Rolling Stone."
**Blonde on Blonde (1966) - Double LP Classic** : This 1966 double LP continued Dylan's electric sound. It produced two significant singles: "Rainy Day Women, Numbers 12 and 35," which became his second number two hit, and the more romantic "I Want You," a top twenty hit. "In 1966's Double LP, Blonde on Blonde, generated his second number two hit, A Joke of a Single with a brass band arrangement and a deliberately silly refrain."
**John Wesley Harding (1967) - Quieter and Rustic** : Released in late 1967 after Dylan's motorcycle accident, this album marked a perverse shift to a quieter, more rustic sound amidst a heavier, more psychedelic popular music landscape. Fueled by pent-up demand, it rose to number 2 on the Billboard album chart in early 1968, becoming Dylan's highest-charting album of the 60s, despite no singles being released by him. "When Dylan finally emerged with a new LP in late 1967, called John Wesley Harding, popular music had gotten heavier and more psychedelic, while Dylan s music perversely had become quieter and more rustic."
**Nashville Skyline (1969) - Crooning Country** : This 1969 album saw Dylan attempt something new by crooning melodically, rather than his signature blues-speak-singing. Its country-leaning sound gave Dylan his final top 10 pop hit as a lead artist with "Lay Lady Lay." "As it's title suggests, Nashville Skyline took the country-leaning sound of John Wesley Harding and went deeper into its twangy roots."
**Self-Portrait (1970) - Critically Savaged** : Released in 1970, this album was largely savaged by critics during an unfocused period for Dylan in the early 1970s. "Critics savaged LPs like 1970s self-portrait."
**New Morning (1970) - Better Received** : Released in late 1970, this album was one of Dylan's better-received LPs from a period often characterized by critical struggles. It included tracks like "The Man in Me" and "If Not For You," which later became a standard covered by other artists. "Late 1970s new morning album, one of Dillon's better-received LPs included classics like The Man in Me."
**Pat Garrett & Billy the Kid (1973) - Soundtrack and Film Debut** : This 1973 album served as the soundtrack to a Western film, also marking Dylan's first major feature film role, which critics were not kind to. Despite the film's poor reception, the album generated the hit single "Knockin' on Heaven's Door." "Critics savaged LPs like... 1973's Pat Garrett and Billy the Kid, a soundtrack to a western film that included Dillon's first major feature film role."
**Planet Waves (1974) - First Chart-Topping LP** : Released in early 1974 after Dylan signed with Asylum Records and recorded with The Band, this album became his first ever chart-topping LP. Backed by an aggressive promotional campaign and a hyped tour, it topped the album chart in just two weeks, affirming Dylan's apex as an album-era artist, despite producing no Top 40 hits. "That LP released in early 74, backed by an aggressive, asylum promotional campaign and hyped by the tour, would become Dillon's first ever chart-topping LP."
**Before the Flood (1974) - Live Platinum Album** : A double LP derived from Dylan's highly successful 1974 tour with The Band, this album achieved platinum status. It also produced a minor pop hit with a live take on "Most Likely You Go Your Way and I'll Go Mine," showcasing a more aggressive, 70s contemporary sound. "Dillon issued a double LP from the tour, called Before the Flood, that went platinum and even produced a minor pop hit."
**Blood on the Tracks (1975) - Acclaimed Personal LP** : Released in January 1975 after Dylan returned to Columbia Records, this is cited by some "Dillonologists" as his best album. Though Dylan denied it was autobiographical, its songs explored the dissolution of a relationship during his estrangement from his wife, Sarah Lowndes. It sold quickly, reaching number one on the album chart in just four weeks and generating the Top 40 hit "Tangled Up in Blue." "Released in January 1975, the LP sold nearly as quickly as Planet Waves had, reaching number one on the album chart in just four weeks."
**Desire (1976) - Imperial Moment Chart Topper** : Released in January 1976, this album was recorded between stops on the Rolling Thunder Review tour. All Music's Stephen Thomas Erlewine described it as "unwieldy and messy," yet it arrived during Dylan's "imperial moment" when his releases were guaranteed to top the charts, spending five weeks at number one, the longest of any Dylan LP. "Still, Desire arrived at Bob Dillon's most imperial moment when virtually anything he released was guaranteed to top the charts, especially after blood on the tracks."

## songs
**Like a Rolling Stone (1965)** : This 1965 electric folk rock single is widely acclaimed by critics and remains Dylan's biggest hit on the American singles charts as a performing artist. It reached number 2 on the Hot 100 for two weeks in September 1965, held from the top spot by The Beatles' "Help." Dylan chronicles his disgust with himself, calling the first draft "ten pages of vomit," but it restored his faith in songwriting. "Not only is, like a Rolling Stone, acclaimed by generations of music critics, it also remains Dylan's biggest hit on the American singles charts, as an artist, the person singing the record, not just the songwriter."
**Forever Young (1974)** : The signature song from Dylan's first chart-topping LP, "Planet Waves," this lullaby-like benediction was written for his eldest son, Jesse, expressing a father's hopes for his child's happiness. Dylan held it in reserve for years and almost removed it from the album for being too sentimental, but ultimately included it twice on the album in different tempos. "Forever Young was a kind of lullaby crossed with a benediction that Bob Dillon wrote for his eldest son, Jesse, a touching song expressing a father's hopes for his child's happiness and fulfillment in life."
**Song for Woody (1962)** : One of the two original songs on Dylan's self-titled debut album, this track was written for and in the style of folk legend Woody Guthrie. An apocryphal scene in the film "A Complete Unknown" depicts Timothy Chalamet's Dylan performing it for Guthrie himself. "Song for Woody, one of the two original songs on the album, was written for and in the style of Folk Legend Woody Guthrie."
**Blowin' in the Wind (1963)** : From Dylan's second LP, "The Free Wheelin' Bob Dylan," this song was acclaimed by the New York folk scene from the moment he started playing it in Greenwich Village nightclubs. It gained national prominence through Peter, Paul and Mary's cover, which reached number 2 on the Hot 100 and was performed at the March on Washington in 1963. "Blowing in the wind was acclaimed by the New York folk scene from the moment Dylan started playing it in Greenwich Village Nightclubs."
**Don't Think Twice, It's All Right (1963)** : Another song written by Dylan that became a hit for other artists. By the fall of 1963, Peter, Paul and Mary's cover of this song reached number 9 on the Hot 100. The Four Seasons, under the pseudonym The Wonder Who, also covered it, reaching number 12. "By the fall of 1963, their cover of Don't Think Twice, It's All Right, reached number nine on the Hot 100."
**All I Really Want to Do (1964)** : This Dylan composition was covered by a teenage Cher, who took her version to number 15 on the charts. "others scoring top 20 hits with songs by Dylan included a teenage share who took all I really want to do to number 15."
**Mr. Tambourine Man (1965)** : Covered by The Byrds in June 1965, their folk rock version topped the Hot 100, marking Dylan's only number one hit as a songwriter. The term "folk rock" was first used to describe The Byrds in relation to this song. "Most famously, The Birds, who in June 1965 gave Dylan his only number one hit as a songwriter topping the Hot 100 with their folk rock cover of Mr. Tamberin Man."
**Subterranean Homesick Blues (1965)** : This rollicking track from "Bringing It All Back Home" scored Dylan's first ever pop Top 40 hit in May 1965, spending one week at number 39 on the Hot 100. It is famously associated with one of the first promotional short films (later called music videos), featuring Dylan flipping hand-scrolled cue cards in a London alleyway, seen in the 1967 documentary "Don't Look Back." "In May of 65, Dylan scored his first ever pop top 40 hit when the album's rollicking subterranean homesick blues spent just one week at number 39 on The Hot 100."
**Positively Fourth Street (1965)** : This acerbic standalone single was released shortly after "Highway 61 Revisited" and became another top ten hit for Dylan, reaching number 7 on the charts. "The same week, Dylan scored another top ten hit with the Asurbic Standalone single Positively Fourth Street, which hit number seven."
**Rainy Day Women, Numbers 12 and 35 (1966)** : A deliberately silly, joke of a single from "Blonde on Blonde," this song featured a brass band arrangement and an infamous chorus. Despite its playful nature, it became Dylan's second number two hit. "In 1966's Double LP, Blonde on Blonde, generated his second number two hit, A Joke of a Single with a brass band arrangement and a deliberately silly refrain."
**I Want You (1966)** : This track from the "Blonde on Blonde" album offered a more romantic and lusty contrast to its companion single. It achieved success as a top twenty hit. "But Blonde on Blonde also generated the more romantic, lusty top twenty hit I Want You."
**All Along the Watchtower (1967)** : A menacing parable from "John Wesley Harding," Dylan declined to release this track as a single. However, legendary guitarist Jimi Hendrix's electrifying cover of the song took it into the Top 20 in the fall of 1968, becoming Hendrix's only top 40 pop hit. "although one track, a menacing parable called All Along the Watch Tower... Would become yet another top forty hit for someone other than Bob Dylan."
**Lay Lady Lay (1969)** : A gentle love-ballad from "Nashville Skyline," this song became Dylan's final top 10 pop hit as a lead artist, reaching number 7 just before the start of 1970. "The gentle love-ballad, Lé Lé Dé Lé, which reached number seven just before the start of 1970."
**The Man in Me (1970)** : Featured on Dylan's "New Morning" album, this classic track found new life nearly three decades later when it was included on the soundtrack to the film "The Big Lebowski." "Late 1970s new morning album, one of Dillon's better-received LPs included classics like The Man in Me, which would wind up on the soundtrack to The Big Lebowski, nearly three decades later."
**If Not For You (1970)** : Though not issued as a single in America from the "New Morning" album, this song became a standard. It was covered multiple times, notably by Dylan's friend George Harrison on his "All Things Must Pass" album, and became Olivia Newton-John's first hit single in 1971. "And that same albums, if not for you, though it wasn't issued as a single in America, became something of a standard."
**Knockin' on Heaven's Door (1973)** : From the "Pat Garrett and Billy the Kid" soundtrack, Dylan actually issued this song as a single, and it came close to the Top 10, peaking at number 12 in October 1973. It has been covered countless times, including a reggae version by Eric Clapton in 1975 and a 1990 cover by Guns n' Roses. "As for the underperforming Pat Garrett and Billy the Kid soundtrack, it generated Knockin' On Heaven's Door, which Dillon actually did issue as a single and came close to the top 10, peaking at number 12 in October of 73."
**Shelter From the Storm (1975)** : This song is mentioned as an example of Dylan's 70s practice of not releasing many of his best-known album tracks as singles in America, reflecting his transformation into an "album rocker." "Dillon didn't even release many of his best-known 70s songs as singles in America from If Not For You to Shelter From the Storm."
**Most Likely You Go Your Way and I'll Go Mine (1974)** : Originally a classic from the 1966 album "Blonde on Blonde," a live version with The Band from the 1974 tour was included on "Before the Flood." This more aggressive and 70s contemporary take became a minor pop hit. "Most likely Ugo Your Way and I'll Go Mine, a 60s blonde on blonde classic that Dillon and the band made more aggressive and 70s contemporary."
**A Night Like This (1974)** : The first single released from the "Planet Waves" album, it only reached number 44 on the charts. The album itself produced no Top 40 hits despite topping the album chart. "Though the Planet Waves album produced no top 40 hits, the first single on a night like this only reached number 44."
**Tangled Up in Blue (1975)** : The first single from "Blood on the Tracks," it debuted at number 39 and peaked at a modest number 31 on the Hot 100, largely because fans opted to buy the album instead of the single. Dylan stated it took him "10 years to live and two years to write." Rolling Stone ranked it third among Dylan's best songs, calling it "his most personal examination of hurt and nostalgia." "In concert, Dillon introduced the sonnet-like tangled up in blue by saying, quote, this song took me 10 years to live and two years to write, unquote."
**Hurricane (1976)** : A catchy protest song from the "Desire" album, this track was a favorite on both pop radio and AOR and featured in the 1993 film "Dazed and Confused." Written by Dylan in collaboration with Jacques Levy, it tells the theatrical story of black middleweight boxer Ruben "Hurricane" Carter, who was wrongly accused of murder, and Dylan's song helped bring attention to his case, leading to a retrial and overturned conviction. "Written by Dillon in collaboration with stage director Jacques Levy, Hurricane was a theatrical story song about real life black middleweight boxer Ruben Hurricane Carter, who was wrongly accused of a triple murder and serving a life sentence."
**Maggie's Farm (1965)** : This electric blues jam was infamously performed by Dylan at the 1965 Newport Folk Festival, where he "plugged in and extra loud," antagonizing the audience and leading to boos, a legendary moment depicted in the film "A Complete Unknown." Dylan later performed it with Mumford & Sons and The Avett Brothers at the 2011 Grammys. "The same song Dylan infamously performed at the 1965 Newport Folk Festival, plugged in and extra loud, and taginizing the Newport audience."
**It's Alright, Ma (I'm Only Bleeding) (1965)** : This song is referenced with a quote showcasing Dylan's "word play junkie" and "media trickster" persona, characteristic of his early career. "This is Carl, it's alright, Ma, I'm only bleeding ho ho ho."

## curiosities
**Consistent Chart Success (Mid-1970s & 21st Century)** : While his legendary 1965 single "Like a Rolling Stone" was his biggest hit as an artist, Dylan's most consistent success on the Billboard charts began a decade later in the mid-1970s, and he continued topping the charts into the 21st century. "As far as record sales go, Dylan's most consistent success on the charts began a decade later, in the mid-1970s, and that wasn't even the last time he topped the charts."
**Pop Star Identity** : Despite being praised as a poet and a Nobel Prize-tier literary figure, Dylan's identity as a "pop star" is often overlooked. He was a pop phenomenon and a teen idol in the 1960s, known for his celebrity magnetism and seat-of-the-pants wit, much like a young Elvis or The Beatles. "But maybe you haven't thought much lately about Bob Dylan pop star."
**"Never-Ending Tour"** : More than 60 years after his debut, Bob Dylan continues to perform on his "never-ending tour" with no signs of slowing down, a testament to his enduring career. "more than 60 years later, Dylan is still on a so-called never-ending tour, with no signs of slowing down."
**Born Robert Allen Zimmerman** : Bob Dylan was born Robert Allen Zimmerman in Duluth, Minnesota, and raised in Hibbing, Minnesota, before making his way to New York City at age 19 in the winter of 1961. "Before we jump into the 60s, when the 19 year old born Robert Allen Zimmerman in Duluth and raised in Hibbing, Minnesota, made his way to New York City, guitar in hand, we need to spend a few moments in the 50s."
**Early Imitation of Woody Guthrie** : Dylan openly imitated and aspired to the skill of folk legend Woody Guthrie in his early career, even writing the song "Song for Woody" in his style, reflecting a significant influence on his nascent songwriting. "Dylan did indeed openly imitate and even aspire to Guthrie's skill with writing folk standards."
**Manager Albert Grossman** : Albert Grossman, a "manager Svengali," played a crucial role in Dylan's early career, as he also managed Peter, Paul and Mary, who significantly boosted Dylan's song catalog. "Peter Yaro, Paul Stooky and Mary Travers were New York folk soloists who joined forces under the influence of manager Svengali Albert Grossman, who by the way also managed Dylan."
**Songwriter for Other Artists (1963-1966)** : For three years following 1963, Bob Dylan served as a hit-generating jukebox as a songwriter, with artists like Peter, Paul and Mary, Cher, Stevie Wonder, The Wonder Who, and The Byrds scoring top 20 hits with his compositions before he had a major hit single of his own. "For the next three years, Bob Dylan became a hit generating jukebox as a songwriter, not a frontline artist."
**Music Video Pioneer ("Subterranean Homesick Blues")** : The promotional short film for "Subterranean Homesick Blues," featuring Dylan flipping hand-scrolled cue cards in a London alleyway, is considered one of the first ever music videos. The clip later opened D.A. Pennebaker's 1967 documentary "Don't Look Back." "This song, by the way, is the one used in one of the first ever promotional short films which were later called music videos."
**Motorcycle Accident (July 1966)** : In July 1966, Bob Dylan was sidelined for months due to a motorcycle accident, an unusual break for a prolific artist during the 1960s when major acts typically released two LPs a year. This hiatus preceded his shift to a quieter, rustic sound. "In July of 1966, a motorcycle accident sidelined Bob Dylan for months."
**Vocal Style Evolution** : Dylan's voice, initially described as quirky and nasal, has grown raspier and gruffer in the 21st century, becoming "more of an acquired taste." Despite criticisms, he continues to sell hundreds of thousands of new recordings, suggesting that his unique vocal instrument, combined with his mystique and charisma, remains appealing. "If anything, the carping about Dylan's vocal instrument has only grown in the 21st century, as his voice has grown raspier, gruffer. And more of an acquired taste."
**Transformation to Album Rocker (1970s)** : In the 1970s, Dylan commercially transformed into an "album rocker," a staple of FM radio's Album-Oriented Rock (AOR) format. Like canonical AOR acts, he often chose not to issue many of his best-known songs as singles in America. "commercially he transformed into what the industry would call an album rocker, a staple of FM radio's album-oriented rock or AOR format."
**Road Warrior (1970s)** : Dylan became a "road warrior" in the 1970s, embarking on tours that played to the largest crowds of his career, signifying a major shift in his performance strategy. "Like those bands, Dillon also became a road warrior in the 70s, touring to the largest crowds of his career."
**1974 Tour with The Band** : In 1974, Dylan announced his first tour in seven years, supported by the Canadian-American rock group The Band. All 40 dates sold out, making it one of the most lucrative tours of the 1970s, and it coincided with the release of his first number one album, "Planet Waves." "In 1974, when he announced his first tour in seven years, supported by his friends in the Canadian American rock group The Band, all 40 dates sold out, making it one of the most lucrative tours of the 70s."
**Return to Columbia Records (1975)** : After a brief stint with Asylum Records for "Planet Waves," Dylan was lured back to Columbia Records by 1975, just in time to release his highly acclaimed album "Blood on the Tracks." "As successful as Planet Waves was, Dillon did not stay long on asylum records. He was lured back to Columbia by 1975."
**"Renaldo and Clara" (1978)** : A four-hour conceptual film about Dylan's "Rolling Thunder Review" tour, "Renaldo and Clara" received poor reviews upon its release in 1978, contributing to the petering out of Dylan's imperial phase on the charts. "Dillon's four-hour conceptual film about the Rolling Thunder tour called Reinaldo and Clara received poor reviews in 1978."
**Artistic Swerve to Christianity (Late 1970s)** : As the 1980s approached, Dylan made an artistic swerve that surprised even his most ardent fans, attempting to "get right with God" by literally being born again, leading him into a period of chart wilderness during the MTV era. "Before he tried to make peace with the synthesizer, Bob Dillon tried to get right with God."
