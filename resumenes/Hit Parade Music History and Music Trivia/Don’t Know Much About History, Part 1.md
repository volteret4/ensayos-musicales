## songs
**Jesus Gave Me Water (1951)** : This single featured Sam Cook as the lead vocalist with the Soul Stirrers. Cook biographer Peter Guralnick described it as "almost dancing and playful," and it became the group's biggest selling and most requested song to date. "He is lead vocal on the Sturs 1951 single, Jesus gave me water, which Cook biographer Peter Gralnick called, almost dancing and playful, gave the group their biggest selling and most requested song to date."
**Be With Me Jesus** : An original song written by Sam Cook for the Soul Stirrers, structured as a counterpoint duet between Cook and fellow Sturer Paul Foster. The song was noted for sounding like two generations forming a "soul alliance." "His original song, Be With Me Jesus, was structured as a counterpoint duet between Cook and fellow sturer Paul Foster."
**Lovable (1957)** : This was Sam Cook's first secular single, released under the pseudonym "Dale Cook" in January 1957 on Specialty Records. It was a secular adaptation of the gospel song "Wonderful," created in a solo studio session with producer Bumps Blackwell. Despite selling modestly and receiving a lukewarm review from Billboard, it served as an important experiment for Cook to transition from gospel to pop and helped Specialty president Art Rupe see his potential as a pop star. "The specialty issued the single in January 1957 under the artist's name Dale Cook."
**Summertime (1957) - Uptempo Version** : Sam Cook and Bumps Blackwell recorded two takes of George Gershwin's standard from Porgy and Bess, with the uptempo, super smooth, "Rat Pack" style version featuring white backing vocalists intended as a single. This version caused a major dispute with Specialty Records president Art Rupe, who wanted a grittier "Little Richard" sound from Cook, ultimately leading to Cook's release from his contract. Keen Records later agreed to release this version. "Cook's up tempo, super smooth, almost rat pack style take on summertime, complete with white backing vocalists, was not at all what Art Roup wanted from his next Little Richard."
**You Send Me (1957)** : Sam Cook's debut single for Keen Records, which he wrote by himself. Although originally intended as the B-side to "Summertime," radio DJs universally flipped it to the A-side. It quickly became a massive hit, debuting on Billboard's bestsellers chart at number 6 on October 27, 1957, reaching number 1 by December 2, and topping Billboard's R&B charts by early December. All Music described it as a "pioneering soul record" for blending R&B, gospel, and pop elements. This song made Sam Cook a self-powered crossover star before Motown or Ray Charles' major pop hits. "When Keen issued Sam Cook's debut single, Radio DJs all flipped it over and treated you send me as the A side."
**I Love You for Sentimental Reasons (1958) - Nat King Cole Cover** : Sam Cooke's cover of Nat King Cole's classic, released in early 1958, became a top 20 hit on both the pop and R&B charts. "Sam T. Cook's version of Nat King Cole's I Love You for Sentimental Reasons was a top 20 hit on both the pop and R&B charts in early 1958."
**I'll Come Running Back to You (1958)** : This romantic and soulful song became an instant number one R&B follow-up to "You Send Me" for Sam Cooke in early 1958. "And on the R&B side, Sam scored an instant number one follow-up to you send me. The similarly romantic and soulful I'll come running back to you."
**Mary, Mary Lou (1958)** : A ring-a-ding-dinging song performed by Sam Cooke on the TV variety hour "The Arthur Murray Party" in 1958, showcasing his rapid ascent as a TV personality. "By 1958, he was serving as a march of dimes spokesman and singing his ring-a-ding-ding-ing Mary Mary Lou on the TV variety hour, The Arthur Murray Party."
**You Were Made for Me (1958-1959)** : One of three consecutive singles released by Sam Cooke during 1958-1959 that reached the R&B top 10 but failed to break into the pop top 20, indicating a temporary relegation to the black audience after his Copa Cabana setback. "Three consecutive singles went top 10 R&B, but missed the pop top 20."
**Everybody Loves to Cha-Cha-Cha (1958-1959)** : A well-known party starter from Sam Cooke's late 1950s period, this single reached number two on the R&B chart but only number 31 on the Hot 100, reflecting his struggle to maintain pop crossover success at the time. "It reached number two on the R&B chart, but a lowly number 31 on the hot 100."
**Only Sixteen (1959)** : A classic single from Sam Cooke released in the summer of 1959. This period marked a time when Cooke was considering leaving Keen Records due to the label's perceived inability to promote a rising artist of his stature and his need for more income to support his growing family. "By the time of his summer 1959 single, only 16, another classic from this late 50s period."
**Teenage Sonata (1960)** : Sam Cooke's first single released under RCA Records in early 1960. It did not achieve significant success, peaking at number 50 on the Hot 100. "In the meantime, in early 1960, RCA, which at the time was the biggest label in music, thanks to having Elvis Presley on their roster, issued their first single with Sam Cook, called Teenage Sonata, It Did Not Set the World on Fire, peaking on the hot 100 at number 50."
**What a Wonderful World (1960)** : This was the last single Sam Cooke recorded for Keen Records, released in June 1960, just after his RCA debut "Teenage Sonata." It became a much bigger hit, reaching number 12 on the Hot 100 and number 2 on the R&B chart. As of March 2021, it is Sam Cooke's most played song on Spotify and remains one of his most beloved and remembered hits. "In June of 1960, what a wonderful world reached number 12 on the hot 100 and number 2 on the R&B chart."
**Good News (1964)** : On the week ending February 29, 1964, this song was number 13 on Billboard's Hot 100 and number 2 on the Cashbox R&B chart. It was the title track of his last studio album and an adaptation of a gospel song, reflecting his unique career path. This song was on the charts during the factual "One Night in Miami" gathering. "The week ending February 29th, 1964, when Sam Cook's aimed that good news was number 13 on Billboard's Hot 100, and number two on the cash box R&B chart."

## curiosities
**Birth and Early Life** : Sam Cook was born in Clarksdale, Mississippi, in 1931, the fifth of eight children to Reverend Charles Cook and Annie May Cook. In 1933, at age two, his family moved north and settled on Chicago's south side, where his father established himself in the religious community. "Sam entered the world in Clarksdale, Mississippi in 1931, the fifth of eight children of the Reverend Charles Cook and Annie May Cook."
**Early Musical Influences** : Growing up in Chicago, Sam Cook was immersed in church music, singing with his siblings and forming his own Jubilee quartet in high school. He was also a fan of black secular music artists like The Ink Spots and Nat King Cole, who was a dozen years older and had attended Cook's high school. Both acts later influenced Cook's vocal style. "But he was also a fan of black secular music by such pre-rock artists as the ink spots."
**Joining The Soul Stirrers (1950)** : In 1950, at the age of 19, Sam Cook (then spelled COOK) was recruited by the Soul Stirrers, a renowned Jubilee-style gospel troupe that had been active for nearly a quarter-century and had pioneered a hard, close harmony gospel style influencing Doo-wop and Soul. He replaced former leader R.H. Harris, quickly becoming the group's de facto leader, most prodigious songwriter, and their first teen idol. "When the Sturs leader, R.H. Harris, decided to leave the group in 1950, they recruited 19 year old Sam Cook, whose surname, by the way, was still spelled COOK, like his father's."
**First Teen Idol of Gospel Music** : With a potent natural tenor voice and good looks, Sam Cook became a sensation in the gospel world, drawing young women to church services just to hear him sing. He was effectively the genre's first teen idol. "Young women would come to church just to hear him sing. Cook was essentially the first teen idol of gospel music."
**Decision to Crossover to Pop (1956)** : By 1956, Sam Cook, a prominent gospel star, saw a "ceiling" in gospel music and new opportunities for black performers with the explosion of rock and roll. Encouraged by producer Robert "Bumps" Blackwell, who had observed Cook's star quality at the 1955 Shrine Auditorium Concert, Cook pursued a secular career, inspired by artists like Harry Belafonte and Ray Charles, who had successfully transitioned spiritual themes to secular hits. "As good as it was being a star in gospel music, he could see it had a ceiling."
**Anticipated Backlash from Gospel Community (1957)** : Sam Cook was apprehensive about the church community's judgment regarding his switch to secular music, with many privately trying to dissuade him in 1957. He was particularly anxious about his father, Reverend Charles Cook's reaction. His father, however, provided pragmatic support, telling Sam that singing was his job and that if he could make more money singing pop, then he should. "He knew the church community would be withering in their judgment."
**Name Change to Sam Cooke (1957)** : As part of his transformation into a pop star, Sam Cook added an 'e' to the end of his surname in 1957. "The other thing the Dale Cook experiment achieved was it gave specialty records president Art Roup, the idea that maybe Sam Cook, who by the way added an E to the end of his name in 1957 as part of his transformation, could cut it as a pop star."
**Dispute with Specialty Records (1957)** : Specialty Records president Art Rupe pushed for Sam Cooke to adopt a "fierce, gritty rock and roll sound" akin to Little Richard, instructing producer Bumps Blackwell to that effect. However, Cooke was not interested in that raw sound and preferred a smoother, more "Rat Pack" style. This disagreement, particularly over the uptempo version of "Summertime" featuring white backing vocalists, led to a "rupture" and a "blowout fight" that resulted in Rupe releasing Cooke from his contract. "Cook's up tempo, super smooth, almost rat pack style take on summertime, complete with white backing vocalists, was not at all what Art Roup wanted from his next Little Richard."
**Ed Sullivan Show Controversy (1957)** : After "You Send Me" hit number one, Sam Cooke appeared twice on "The Ed Sullivan Show." During his first appearance in early November 1957, the show ran long, and CBS cut Cooke off after just two lines of his song. Public outrage led to a flood of calls to CBS, prompting Sullivan to invite Cooke back on December 1, where he performed two songs and issued a live apology on air. "Sam got through not even two lines of the song before CBS cut him off."
**Rapid Rise as Pop Fixture and TV Personality (1958)** : By 1958, Sam Cooke quickly became a prominent pop fixture and TV personality. He served as a March of Dimes spokesman and performed on variety shows like "The Arthur Murray Party." "Sam Cook had turned into a pop fixture and TV personality remarkably quickly."
**Infamous Copa Cabana Performance (March 1958)** : In March 1958, Sam Cooke performed at New York's prestigious Copa Cabana Nightclub, a long-time dream of his. However, the performance was an infamous failure, reportedly set up for disaster by preceding stand-up comedian Myron Cohen and faced with a largely white audience unresponsive to his style. Cooke later admitted on the Mike Douglas show in 1964, "I wasn't ready." This event is a major plot point in the film "One Night in Miami." "Infamously, he did not do well, as is depicted again in the 2020 film One Night in Miami."
**Stalled Momentum and Relegation (1958-1959)** : The Copa Cabana failure appeared to check Sam Cooke's momentum. For the rest of 1958 and 1959, his hits did not match the success of "You Send Me," and he seemed relegated primarily to the black audience, similar to his gospel days, with three consecutive singles hitting R&B top 10 but missing the pop top 20. "Back in 1958, the Copa seemed to check Sam Cook's momentum."
**Marriage and Family (1958)** : In 1958, Sam Cooke married his longtime sweetheart Barbara Campbell, and they were soon expecting their second child. This increased family responsibility was a factor in his desire to leave Keen Records for a larger label that could offer more financial stability. "What's more, in 1958, Sam had married his longtime sweetheart Barbara Campbell, and they were soon expecting their second child."
**Desire for Ownership and Mogul Aspirations (1959)** : Motivated by wanting more ownership of his material and feeling Keen Records was overmatched, Sam Cooke signed a major deal with RCA Records in late 1959. As part of this move, he bought back his copyrights from Keen, established his own publishing company, Cags Music, and began planning to launch his own record label. "So when he finally scored a major label deal with RCA records in the closing weeks of 1959, he not only bought back his copyrights from keen records, he also set up his own publishing company, Cags Music, and began making plans to start a label of his own."
**Civil Rights Activism** : Sam Cooke was a key figure in the American Civil Rights movement. He played to integrated audiences and actively fostered black talent, demonstrating a deep social commitment. The film "One Night in Miami" explores this aspect of his life. "All of this success and influence made Cook a key figure in the American Civil Rights movement, as he played to integrated audiences and fostered black talent."
**Soul Pioneer, Record Mogul, Social Activist** : Sam Cooke is remembered as a soul pioneer, a record mogul who launched his own label, a social activist for civil rights, and one of the greatest voices in music history, despite his tragically brief life. "He achieved so much in his two brief life, soul pioneer, record mogul, social activist, and one of the greatest voices ever to step in front of a microphone."
**Recording Socially Conscious Hits Pre-1964** : Contrary to some narratives, Sam Cooke was recording socially conscious music well before the pivotal year of 1964. "And no matter what the movie says, Cook was recording socially conscious hits well before 1964."

# concert - The Great Shrine Auditorium Concert

## curiosities
**Landmark Gospel Event (July 1955)** : Held in Los Angeles in July 1955, this concert is still considered over 65 years later to be a landmark show in gospel history. Thousands gathered to see stars like James Cleveland and The Caravans, The Pilgrim Travelers (soon to feature Lou Rawls), and The Soul Stirrers, for whom 24-year-old Sam Cook was the main attraction. "Thousands gathered in LA in July of 55 to see such stars as the caravans James Cleveland, a budding legend in gospel choir arranging."
**Pivotal for Sam Cook** : The concert proved "rather pivotal for Sam" Cook as it was where producer Robert "Bumps" Blackwell first observed his undeniable star quality, leading Blackwell to pursue Cook for a pop career. "The Great Shrine Concert turned out to be rather pivotal for Sam."
