## albums
**Ain't That Good News (1964) - Last Studio Album of His Lifetime** : The title track of this album was Sam Cooke's last studio album released in his lifetime. Its title song was an adaptation of a gospel song, which made it emblematic of his career as a gospel singer turned secular star. "the title track of the last studio album of his lifetime."
**Greatest Hits Album - Home of the Languid "Summertime"** : Several years after it was recorded, Cooke's slow, soulful ballad take on Gershwin's "Summertime" was included on his greatest hits album. This was not the up-tempo version that was originally issued as a single. "would wind up on his greatest hits album several years later."

## songs
**Peace in the Valley - With the Soul Stirrers** : A gospel recording Sam Cooke made with the Soul Stirrers. The Soul Stirrers were a Jubilee-style gospel group that had formed about five years before Sam was born. "That's him singing Peace in the Valley with the Soul Sturs."
**Jesus Gave Me Water (1951) - Soul Stirrers' Biggest Seller** : Cooke sang lead on this 1951 single. It became the group's biggest-selling and most requested song up to that point. Biographer Peter Guralnick described it as "almost dancing and playful." "gave the group their biggest selling and most requested song to date."
**Be With Me Jesus - Original Counterpoint Duet** : Cooke wrote this original song as a counterpoint duet between himself and fellow Soul Stirrer Paul Foster. By this time Cooke was in his early 20s and had become both the group's de facto leader and its most prolific songwriter. "It sounded like two generations forming a soul alliance."
**Wonderful (1956) - Gospel Source of "Lovable"** : This classic praise song was written about ten years earlier by Chicago gospel pioneers Virginia Davis and Theodore Frye, and the "wonderful one" in the lyric is God. The Soul Stirrers recorded it with Sam in early 1956. "The wonderful one in the lyric was God."
**Lovable (1957) - Released Under the Name "Dale Cook"** : In a solo studio session with Bumps Blackwell toward the end of 1956, Sam turned "Wonderful" into a secular song called "Lovable." Cooke was nervous about putting out a non-gospel pop single under his own name, so Specialty issued it in January 1957 credited to "Dale Cook." The disguise fooled no one, because he already had the most recognizable voice in gospel. Billboard gave it a lukewarm review and it sold only modestly and was not a hit. Even so, it helped Sam get past his fear of leaving gospel, and it gave Specialty president Art Rupe the idea that Cooke could succeed as a pop star. "But lovable wasn't fooling anyone."
**Summertime (1957) - Two Takes and a Label Rupture** : Cooke and Bumps Blackwell recorded two takes of George Gershwin's standard from Porgy and Bess. It was a smart crossover choice, because the song was one of the most recognizable in the mainstream American canon. Art Rupe wanted Cooke to sound like Specialty's Little Richard, but the version Cooke and Blackwell meant to release as the single was up-tempo, very smooth, close to Rat Pack style, and used white backing vocalists. This led to a major fight between Cooke, Blackwell and Rupe, and Rupe released Cooke from his Specialty contract. Cooke and Blackwell then took the recordings to Keen Records, which agreed to issue the up-tempo "Summertime" as a single. It ended up as the B-side of "You Send Me." "was not at all what Art Roup wanted from his next Little Richard."
**You Send Me (1957) - Self-Written Debut Single That Went to No. 1** : Cooke wrote this song by himself, and both he and Keen expected it to be the B-side of "Summertime." Radio DJs flipped the record and played "You Send Me" as the A-side, and listeners requested it in large numbers. In 1957 Billboard charted store bestsellers and jockey airplay separately, since the Hot 100 did not start until 1958. The song debuted on the bestsellers chart dated October 27, 1957 at No. 6 and was Billboard's top seller by December 2. On the jockeys chart it took two weeks to reach the top 10 and four more weeks to reach No. 1. By early December it was also No. 1 on Billboard's R&B chart. A couple of weeks before Christmas 1957, his very first track was the top-selling and most-played song in America. Among the ten artists in the first Rock and Roll Hall of Fame class, Cooke was the only one to start his career with a No. 1 hit he wrote alone, topping both the pop and R&B charts. An AllMusic writer called it "a pioneering soul record in its time, blending elements of R&B, gospel and pop." "You send me, however simple it might sound now, was in a way the most accomplished debut single of Rock's first decade."
**(I Love You) For Sentimental Reasons (1958) - Nat King Cole Cover** : Cooke's version of the Nat King Cole song reached the top 20 on both the pop and R&B charts in early 1958. "Sam T. Cook's version of Nat King Cole's I Love You for Sentimental Reasons was a top 20 hit on both the pop and R&B charts in early 1958."
**I'll Come Running Back to You (1958) - Instant R&B No. 1 Follow-Up** : This romantic, soulful single went straight to No. 1 on the R&B chart, following "You Send Me." "Sam scored an instant number one follow-up to you send me."
**Mary, Mary Lou (1958) - Arthur Murray Party Performance** : In 1958 Cooke sang this upbeat number on the TV variety show The Arthur Murray Party, at a time when he had quickly become a pop fixture and TV personality. "singing his ring-a-ding-ding-ing Mary Mary Lou on the TV variety hour, The Arthur Murray Party."
**You Were Made for Me / Win Your Love for Me (1958–59) - Top 10 R&B, Below Pop Top 20** : These were part of a run of three consecutive singles after the Copacabana failure that reached the R&B top 10 but missed the pop top 20. This suggested Cooke had been pushed back toward a mainly Black audience, as in his gospel years. "Three consecutive singles went top 10 R&B, but missed the pop top 20."
**Everybody Loves to Cha Cha Cha (1959) - Party Starter** : One of Cooke's best-known singles from this period. It reached No. 2 on the R&B chart but only No. 31 on the Hot 100. "It reached number two on the R&B chart, but a lowly number 31 on the hot 100."
**Only Sixteen (1959) - Late-50s Classic** : Cooke released this single in summer 1959. Around that time he began to feel that Keen Records was too small to promote an artist of his standing. "By the time of his summer 1959 single, only 16, another classic from this late 50s period."
**Teenage Sonata (1960) - First RCA Single** : RCA released this as its first Sam Cooke single in early 1960. It peaked at No. 50 on the Hot 100. "It Did Not Set the World on Fire, peaking on the hot 100 at number 50."
**(What A) Wonderful World (1960) - Final Keen Single** : This was the last single Cooke recorded for Keen, released just after the RCA single "Teenage Sonata," and it was a much bigger hit. In June 1960 it reached No. 12 on the Hot 100 and No. 2 on the R&B chart. As of March 2021 it was Cooke's most-played song on Spotify, and it remains one of his best-loved and best-remembered hits. "As of March 2021, it is Sam Cook's all-time most played song on Spotify."
**Ain't That Good News (1964) - On the Charts During One Night in Miami** : In the week ending February 29, 1964, the song was No. 13 on Billboard's Hot 100 and No. 2 on the Cash Box R&B chart. It was the Cooke single on the charts the week he actually spent the night in Miami with three other Black icons. It was adapted from a gospel song. "It's not one of Cook's best remembered hits, but it was the one on the charts that fateful week in 1964."
**A Change Is Gonna Come (1964) - Legacy Anthem** : Cooke had already recorded this anthem in real life, even though One Night in Miami shows him being pushed to go deeper with his music. It became his legacy shortly after his tragic death. "Cook had already produced an anthem that would become his legacy, not long after his tragic death."

## curiosities
**Birth and Family - Clarksdale, Mississippi, 1931** : Sam was born in Clarksdale, Mississippi in 1931, the fifth of eight children of Reverend Charles Cook and Annie May Cook. In 1933, when Sam was two, Reverend Cook moved the family north to Chicago's South Side, where he became established in the religious community. "the fifth of eight children of the Reverend Charles Cook and Annie May Cook."
**Childhood Influences - Ink Spots and Nat King Cole** : Sam grew up surrounded by church music, sang with his siblings, and had formed his own Jubilee quartet by high school. He was also a fan of pre-rock Black secular music, especially the Ink Spots and Nat King Cole. Cole was twelve years older than Sam and had attended the same Chicago high school. Both acts later shaped Sam's vocal style. "Both of these acts would later influence Sam's own vocal style."
**Joining the Soul Stirrers at 19 (1950)** : When Soul Stirrers leader R.H. Harris left in 1950, the group recruited 19-year-old Sam, whose surname was still spelled "Cook" like his father's. Harris had been the closest the group had to a star, but they had never had a star like Cooke. "the Sturs had never had a star like Sam Cook."
**The First Teen Idol of Gospel** : Cooke had a strong natural tenor voice and good looks, and young women would come to church just to hear him sing. "Cook was essentially the first teen idol of gospel music."
**Courted by Bumps Blackwell** : Specialty producer Robert "Bumps" Blackwell saw Cooke at the 1955 Great Shrine concert and spent the next year and a half persuading him to leave gospel for pop. Sam did not need much persuading, because he could see that gospel stardom had a ceiling. The explosion of rock and roll in 1955–56 had also opened new opportunities for Black performers. "Sam didn't need much convincing."
**Inspired by Harry Belafonte's Crossover** : According to biographer Peter Guralnick, Cooke had noticed the huge crossover success of Harry Belafonte, whose 1956 LP Calypso was bought by millions of white and Black listeners. "Cook himself had taken notice of the phenomenal crossover success of Harry Belafonte."
**Church Backlash and His Father's Blessing** : Cooke knew the church community would judge him harshly for going secular, and throughout 1957 many people privately urged him not to. He was especially worried about his father's reaction. Reverend Charles Cook told him, according to Guralnick, "The Lord gave you a voice to sing to make people happy. And if you can make more money singing pop music..." "Well, don't nobody get saved over singing."
**Adding the "E" (1957)** : As part of his move to pop, Sam added an "E" to the end of his surname in 1957. "who by the way added an E to the end of his name in 1957 as part of his transformation."
**Ed Sullivan Show - Cut Off, Then Brought Back (1957)** : In early November 1957, Ed Sullivan booked Cooke as the final guest of the night, but the show ran long and CBS cut him off before he finished two lines of "You Send Me." Viewers were so angry that they flooded CBS with calls. Sullivan brought Cooke back on December 1, let him sing two songs, and apologized on air. By the time the song hit No. 1, Cooke had appeared on the show twice. "And I never received so much mail in my life, Sam."
**March of Dimes Spokesman (1958)** : By 1958 Cooke was a spokesman for the March of Dimes, a sign of how quickly mainstream audiences accepted him. "By 1958, he was serving as a march of dimes spokesman."
**Bombing at the Copacabana (March 1958)** : By March 1958, Cooke had been invited to play New York's Copacabana nightclub, which had long been a dream of his. The show went badly; comedian Myron Cohen, who opened for him, reportedly set him up to fail in front of the mostly white audience. Afterward his momentum seemed to stall, and none of his 1958–59 hits came close to "You Send Me." "Back in 1958, the Copa seemed to check Sam Cook's momentum."
**Still Stung by the Copa in 1964 - Mike Douglas Show** : As late as February 1964, Cooke was still upset about the Copa. Asked on The Mike Douglas Show to summarize his career highlights, he brought up the failure himself, calling the Copa "the prestige date of all time." When asked why he bombed, he answered, "Because I wasn't ready." "If you make it at the Copa, that's it."
**Marriage to Barbara Campbell (1958)** : In 1958 Sam married his longtime sweetheart Barbara Campbell, and they were soon expecting their second child. He was not earning enough at Keen to support a family, which added to his reasons for leaving the label. "Sam wasn't making enough on keen records to raise a family."
**Owning His Masters - Kags Music and RCA (1959)** : When Cooke signed a major-label deal with RCA in the last weeks of 1959, he bought back his copyrights from Keen. He also set up his own publishing company, Kags Music, and began planning his own label. At the time RCA was the biggest label in music, thanks to Elvis Presley. "he not only bought back his copyrights from keen records, he also set up his own publishing company."
**Self-Powered Crossover Star Before Motown** : Cooke was a crossover star on his own terms two years before Motown had its first hit and before Ray Charles had his first major pop hit. He played to integrated audiences and supported Black talent, which made him a key figure in the Civil Rights movement. "Cook was a self-powered crossover star."
**Portrayal in One Night in Miami (2020)** : In the film, Leslie Odom Jr. plays Cooke as a foil to Malcolm X: a successful Black performer seeking white approval at the expense of his gospel roots. Malcolm X (Kingsley Ben-Adir) criticizes everything from Cooke's activism to his music. Slate's Jack Hamilton wrote that the film "so drastically distorts the facts of Cook's life and work that it actually sells short his artistry." The movie also treats the 1958 Copa failure as a fresh wound, although it happened six years before the 1964 night in Miami. "The church songs that nurtured you, you twisted them and you perverted them to feed a white crowd."

## curiosities
**Gospel's Biggest Act** : The Soul Stirrers formed about five years before Sam Cooke was born, and they were nearly 25 years old by the time he joined. They survived the Depression and World War II by touring nationally. By the 1940s they had helped pioneer a hard, close-harmony gospel style that later influenced doo-wop and soul. "in the gospel world Sam was raised in, there was no musical act bigger than the Soul Sturs."
**Leadership Change (1950)** : Leader R.H. Harris left in 1950 and was replaced by 19-year-old Sam Cooke. Cooke soon became the group's de facto leader and main songwriter. "When the Sturs leader, R.H. Harris, decided to leave the group in 1950, they recruited 19 year old Sam Cook."

**I've Got a Woman (1954) - Gospel Melody Turned Secular** : In summer 1954, while on tour, 23-year-old Ray Charles heard the Southern Tones' "It Must Be Jesus" on the radio. # label - Specialty Records

## curiosities
**Gospel Label in Los Angeles** : Specialty Records was founded in Los Angeles by entrepreneur and Black music enthusiast Art Rupe, and the Soul Stirrers were signed to it. Producer Bumps Blackwell began moving the label beyond gospel with Little Richard in 1955. "founded in Los Angeles by budding music in Pricario and Black Music Efficient Auto, Art Group."
**Release of Sam Cooke** : After the fight over the up-tempo "Summertime," Art Rupe released Sam Cooke from his Specialty contract. "Art Roup released Sam Cook from his contract with specialty."

# label - Keen Records

## curiosities
**Home of "You Send Me"** : Keen was a smaller label that agreed to release Cooke's up-tempo "Summertime." It ended up with the No. 1 hit "You Send Me" on the B-side. By 1959 the label seemed too small to promote an artist of Cooke's stature, but his last Keen single, "Wonderful World," outsold his first RCA release. "The small label seemed overmatched to promote a rising artist of his stature."

# concert - Great Shrine Auditorium Concert 1955

## curiosities
**Landmark Show in Gospel History** : Thousands attended this show in Los Angeles in July 1955. The Soul Stirrers co-headlined with the Caravans, James Cleveland (then a rising gospel choir arranger), and the Pilgrim Travelers, who would later add Lou Rawls as a vocalist. Sam Cooke, age 24, was the main attraction. Producer Bumps Blackwell was in the audience and was taken with Cooke's star quality. "what is still over 65 years later considered the landmark show in gospel history."

# concert - Copacabana 1958

## curiosities
**Sam Cooke's Failed Debut** : In March 1958 Cooke played New York's Copacabana, a long-held dream of his, and the show was a failure. Comedian Myron Cohen, who preceded him, reportedly set him up to fail with the mostly white audience. One Night in Miami recreates the performance, including Cohen. "For some reason, it happens to be the prestige date of all time."

# genre - Gospel

## curiosities
**Gospel Roots of R&B** : As rhythm and blues became established on the charts in the first half of the 1950s, its roots in gospel became obvious. R&B stars were creating new forms by openly turning spiritual songs into secular ones, and some records were direct remakes. "R&B stars were pioneering bold new forms by shamelessly turning spiritual music secular."

# genre - Soul

## curiosities
**Sam Cooke as Inventor** : After his self-written debut went to No. 1 on both the pop and R&B charts, Sam Cooke went on to essentially invent soul music. He understood instinctively how soulful gospel could become soul. "And then he went on to basically invent Soul music."

# curiosity

**Rock and Roll Hall of Fame - First Class (1986)** : The Rock and Roll Hall of Fame inducted its first class of ten performers in 1986: Chuck Berry, Jerry Lee Lewis, Elvis Presley, Buddy Holly, Ray Charles, the Everly Brothers, Little Richard, Fats Domino, James Brown and Sam Cooke. Sam Cooke was the only one to launch his career with a No. 1 hit he wrote by himself. "the rock and roll Hall of Fame inducted its first class of legendary performers, 10 in all."
**The Real Night in Miami - February 25, 1964** : On February 25, 1964, Cassius Clay, soon to become Muhammad Ali, beat Sonny Liston to become heavyweight champion of the world. He then spent a quiet evening at a Miami hotel with Sam Cooke, Malcolm X and football star Jim Brown. What the four men discussed, argued about, or listened to is unknown to anyone except Jim Brown, who was still alive at the time. "what actually happened in that hotel room is pure speculation."
**One Night in Miami - Oscar Nominations** : Regina King directed the film, which Kemp Powers adapted from his own stage play. It received three Oscar nominations: Best Supporting Actor and Best Original Song for Leslie Odom Jr., and Best Adapted Screenplay for Powers. It was not nominated for Best Picture or Best Director. Ironically, Powers's script treats Sam Cooke more harshly than any other character. "the character Powers Story, his hardest on is Sam Cook."
**Billboard's Pre-Hot 100 Charts** : In 1957 Billboard charted store bestsellers separately from songs most played by "jockeys," as radio DJs were called. The Hot 100 did not launch until 1958. "The hot 100 would not launch until 1958."
