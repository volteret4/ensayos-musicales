## albums
**Talking Heads: 77 (1977)** : The band's debut LP, released in 1977 after they signed to Sire Records. It marked their first recording as a quartet with the addition of multi-instrumentalist Jerry Harrison, formerly of The Modern Lovers. "actually titled their debut LP Talking Heads 77. It was their first recording as a quartet after adding multi-instrumentalist Jerry Harrison, formerly of Boston band The Modern Lovers."
**More Songs About Buildings and Food (1978)** : The band's sophomore album, released in 1978 and produced by Brian Eno. It balanced David Byrne's "trademark quirk with increasingly accessible rhythms" on tracks like "Thank You For Sending Me an Angel" and "The Girls Want to Be With The Girls." It was their first album to reach the Top 40, peaking at number 29. "In 1978, Talking Heads returned with a sophomore album produced by iconoclastic producer Brian Eno. More songs about buildings and food balanced the trademark David Bern Quirk with increasingly accessible rhythms."
**Fear of Music (1979)** : A Brian Eno-produced album released in 1979, which represented Talking Heads' attempt to become more danceable. It featured "Life During Wartime" and "I Zimbra." "with their Brian Eno produced album Fear of Music."

## songs
**Psycho Killer (1975, early version)** : An early version of this song was performed by Talking Heads as a trio at CBGB in 1975, predating its formal studio recording and release. "Here's Talking heads performing an early version of Psycho Killer at CBGB in 1975."
**Love Goes To Building on Fire (1976)** : Talking Heads' 1976 debut single, characterized by the stripped-down minimalism of punk both structurally and lyrically, but infused with a "winsome-arty melodicism." This song expanded the definition of punk by adding its characteristic quirk. "Their 1976 debut single Love Goes To Building on Fire had the stripped down minimalism of Punk structurally and lyrically, but with a win-some-arty melodicism."
**Uh-Oh, Love Comes to Town (1977)** : The first single from *Talking Heads: 77*. Described as "slap-happy strutting," it did not chart. "The first single from Talking Heads 77 was the slap-happy strutting, uh-oh, Love Comes to Town, which did not chart."
**Psycho Killer (1977)** : Formally recorded in the studio, having been played as a trio at CBGB since 1975. Released in December 1977, it debuted on the Hot 100 in February 1978 and peaked at number 92 in its five-week run, making Talking Heads the second CBGB band to crack the pop chart. It featured "incongruous French lyrics and nonsense, fa-fa-fa-fa-fa-refrain." "The release in December 1977, Psycho Killer, still with its incongruous French lyrics and nonsense, fa-fa-fa-fa-fa-refrain, debuted on the Hot 100 in February 1978."
**Thank You For Sending Me an Angel (1978)** : A track from *More Songs About Buildings and Food* that featured accessible rhythms. "On tracks like Thank You For Sending Me an Angel."
**The Girls Want to Be With The Girls (1978)** : A track from *More Songs About Buildings and Food* that featured accessible rhythms. "and The Girls Want to Be With The Girls."
**Take Me to the River (1978)** : Their first Top 40 hit and first cover song, suggested by producer Brian Eno. Originally a 1974 Al Green deep cut with a strutting tempo, Talking Heads slowed it to a "lurch," creating a "soul punk" sound. It broke into the Top 40 the week before Christmas 1978, eventually peaking at number 26. "Talking Heads, Take Me to the River, broke into the top 40 the week before Christmas, 1978."
**Life During Wartime (1979)** : This song debuted on Billboard's Hot 100 in the fall of 1979, blending funky rhythms with jittery, apocalyptic lyrics about 1970s New York City. Despite its lyrical themes about things being "too hard" for fun or disco, the song itself was described as "totally fun and quite danceable." It name-checked New York nightspots like the Mud Club and CBGB, the venue that helped launch the band. It peaked at number 80 on the pop chart. "The song claimed that things were too hard in the apocalyptic New York City of the 70s to have any fun."
**I Zimbra (1979)** : A deep cut on *Fear of Music*, described as "Afrobeat flavored" and "even closer to club music." It brought Talking Heads to Billboard's Disco chart, where it reached number 28. "Another deep cut on Fear of Music, the Afrobeat flavored Izymbra, was even closer to club music and it brought talking heads to Billboard's Disco chart where it reached number 28."

## curiosities
**Rhode Island School of Design Origins** : Before forming Talking Heads in New York to focus on music, drummer Chris Frantz, bassist Tina Weymouth, and frontman David Byrne met at the Rhode Island School of Design. "At the time, they were a trio who'd met at the Rhode Island School of Design, and decided to move to New York to focus on music."
**Art Students to Musicians** : David Byrne, Chris Frantz, and Tina Weymouth were all students at the Rhode Island School of Design in 1975, working in visual and conceptual arts. Disenchanted, they decided to form a rock band to better express their artistic ideas. "Back in 1975, David Byrne, Chris, and Martina Weymouth were all students at the prestigious Rhode Island School of Design. According to David, we were all artists working in the visual and conceptual arts, but we were disenchanted. So, David, Chris and Martina decided that they just might be able to express their artistic ideas better as musicians."
**Debut Album and Name Change** : The band recorded their first album and changed their name within two years of forming, after adding a keyboard player. "Within two years, they added a keyboard player recorded their first album and changed their name."

## curiosities
**Genre Flexibility and Pop Success** : Blondie was a "flexible sex tet" led by Debbie Harry and Chris Stein, notable for scoring four number one hits in four different genres within just two years. Their historic run included chart-toppers in Electro-Rock, Rap, and Reggae, exemplifying how punk could morph into pop success. "And no band better exemplified that approach than a flexible sex tet led by singer Debbie Harry and guitarist Chris Stein, who scored four number one hits in four different genres in just two years."
**Debbie Harry's Early Persona** : Deborah Harry, Blondie's frontwoman, was a former waitress and Playboy bunny from New Jersey before achieving fame. "fronted by a former waitress and Playboy bunny from New Jersey named Deborah Harry."
**European and Australian Success** : For their first half-decade, Blondie's eclecticism hindered their US success, but they became "reliable hitmakers" in Europe and Australia, partly due to Chris Stein and Debbie Harry's skill with covers. They achieved "gold and platinum" in Germany, England, France, Holland, and Belgium. "In Europe and Australia, they had become reliable hitmakers, helped by Chris Stein and Debbie Harry's good ear for covers."
**Opposition to Disco (or lack thereof)** : Unlike some punk or guitar rock bands, Blondie was not opposed to disco and had even covered songs by Donna Summer and Gloria Gaynor live, making them receptive to producer Mike Chapman's suggestion to transform "Once I Had a Love" into a disco-infused track. "Unlike some punks or guitar rock bands of the time, Blondie were not opposed to disco. They'd even covered songs by Donna Summer and Gloria Gainer live."
**First CBGB Band Platinum Album** : The *Parallel Lines* album soared into the top 10, peaking at number 6, and "went platinum," making it the first platinum album for any CBGB band. "The Parallel Lines album soared into the top 10, a first for any CBGB band, and peaked at number 6 and went platinum."
**Number One Hit and Pop Stardom** : "Heart of Glass" became the number one song in America in its 11th week on the Hot 100, elevating Blondie to massive pop stardom. This success, though sneered at by "punk true believers" as selling out, validated the band's eclectic approach. "Moving up from number three last week to number one, the biggest selling song in the USA, Blondie, and Heart of Glass."
**"Sold Out" Criticism for "Heart of Glass"** : After "Heart of Glass" hit number one, some "punk true believers" criticized Blondie, "sneered that Blondie had sold out," viewing their pop success as a betrayal of punk ideals. "besides the punk true believers who sneered that Blondie had sold out but never mind them."

Produced by John Cale, the album highlighted Smith's poetic freedom and her band's musical eclecticism. ## songs
**Hey Joe (1974)** : Patti Smith's 1974 debut single, a cover of the rock standard. This release demonstrated her unique blend of poetry and spoken-word improv with spontaneous musical backing. "On her 1974 debut single, A Cover of the Rock Standard Hey Joe."
**Piss Factory (1974)** : An original song that served as the B-side to Patti Smith's 1974 debut single, "Hey Joe." Its lyrics were delivered in a stream-of-consciousness style, embodying her unique "punkness" through words as much as music. "backed by this original song, Piss Factory, lyrics tumbled out of Smith with what seemed like stream of consciousness."
**Redondo Beach (1975)** : A "punk reggae song" from Patti Smith's *Horses* album, which tells a story of a tragic drowning on a lesbian beach yet has an "oddly perky bounce." It exemplifies the album's musical eclecticism. "Redondo Beach, which is seriously a punk reggae song, tells a story of a tragic drowning on a lesbian beach, yet has an oddly perky bounce."
**Gloria (1975)** : The lead-off track on *Horses*, a transformation of an early hit by the Irish band Them (fronted by Van Morrison). Patti Smith reimagined it into a "galloping epic," demonstrating punk's ethos of recontextualizing old R&B and rock-and-roll chestnuts into punk anthems. "For the lead-off track on horses, Patty Smith took Gloria, an early hit by the Irish band Them, fronted by a young Van Morrison. And she transformed it into a galloping epic."
**Free Money (1975)** : A deep cut from Patti Smith's *Horses* LP that gained wider attention in 1977 when covered by Sammy Hagar. "A deep cut from her horse's LP called Free Money. ... Gained wider attention in 1977 when it was covered by no kidding, Sammy Hagar."
**Because the Night (1978)** : A collaboration with Bruce Springsteen, this song became Patti Smith's first pop hit and the first Top 40 hit for any CBGB act. Originally a fragment by Springsteen, it was completed by Smith with most of the lyrics, blending her punk poetry with his romance. Debuted by Smith and Springsteen at CBGB's Theatre Annex in December 1977, it cracked the Hot 100 in April 1978, peaked at number 13 in June 1978. "Patty Smith wrote most of the lyrics and recorded it with her typically fiery vocals. It blended Patty's punk poetry with Bruce's home spun romance."
**Frederick (1979)** : A "Springsteen-esque" single released in 1979, as a follow-up to her 1978 hit "Because the Night." It cracked the Hot 100 but peaked at number 90. "She tried to follow up her 1978 hit because of the night with the Springsteen-esque 1979 single, Frederick."

"rock front woman who'd gotten her start in performance art, made her bones as an artist working alongside her sometime lover Robert Maplethorpe, and had been gigging all over town as a beat poet backed up by a band."
**"Punx Poet Laureate"** : Patti Smith was brought into CBGB by Television and quickly built a reputation as the "Punx Poet Laureate," known for her unique blend of poetry, spoken-word improv, and spontaneous musical backing. "she was building a reputation as Punx Poet Laureate, Patricia and Smith, better known as Patty Smith."

## songs

## curiosities
**Catalyst for CBGB's Rock Shift** : Television, an angular improvisatory four-piece, played sprawling, jamming, almost jazzy music that convinced Hilly Crystal to transform CBGB into a rock venue. Their "no-frill style and DIY attitude" made them punk, despite their sound differing significantly from the Ramones or Talking Heads. "Television, the angular improvisatory four piece, that finally convinced Hilly Crystal to make CBGB a rock venue, played sprawling, jamming, almost jazzy music."
**CBGB Debut and Residency** : Around spring 1974, Hilly Crystal was persuaded to allow Television to play at CBGB. Their stripped-down yet intricate sound quickly became a "center of gravity" for the downtown New York scene, and they settled into a significant Sunday night residency. "Sometime around the spring of 74, Hilly Crystal was persuaded to allow a scruffy rock-for-some who called themselves Television...to take the CBGB stage."
**Rejected by Island Records** : In 1975, Television recorded a demo for Island Records with producer Brian Eno, but the label ultimately decided not to sign them due to their "sprawling jams" that resisted initial label interest. "The band even recorded a demo for island records, with producer Brian Eno in 1975, yet the label decided not to sign television."

## curiosities
**Defining Punk in the Zeitgeist** : The Ramones made the greatest impression at their 1974 CBGB debut, more so than Television or Patti Smith, and came to define punk in the public consciousness. They were known for their distinctive look of black leather jackets and their "wall of noise" sound, contrasting sharply with the hippie aesthetic. "Perhaps this explains why the Ramones, more than television or patty Smith, defined Punk in the zeitgeist."
**"Pure Punk" Archetype** : The Ramones were considered the embodiment of "pure Punk," requiring no adjectives or qualifiers. Joey Ramone sang with a sneer, Johnny Ramone aggressively played only downstrokes, Dee Dee Ramone played deceptively simple baselines at breakneck speed, and Tommy Ramone, originally their manager, became drummer to match their relentless tempo. "If there is such a thing as pure Punk, Punk that needs no adjective, compound word, or qualifier, the Ramones were it."
**Paul McCartney Pseudonym** : The band members all pretended to have the same last name, "Ramone," which was a former pseudonym of Paul McCartney. "who all pretended to have the same last name, a former pseudonym of Paul McCartney, Ramone."
**Joey Ramone, Fan of 50s and 60s R&B and Girl Group Pop** : Joey Ramone was an "unabashed fan" of R&B and Girl Group Pop from the 1950s and 1960s, which influenced the Ramones' mission to return rock to its primitive roots and their choice of cover songs and stylistic adaptations. "No one took this mission more seriously than Joey Ramone, who was an unabashed fan of 50s and 60s R&B and Girl Group POP."
**Tommy Ramone, Drummer and Songwriter** : Thomas Eardilly, or Tommy Ramone, switched from being the band's manager to its drummer because he was the only player who could keep up with the band's relentless tempo. He also adapted the sound of The Shangri-La's into the song "I Want to Be Your Boyfriend." "Thomas Eardilly, or Tommy Ramone, switched from being the band's manager to its drummer because he was the only player who could keep up with the band's relentless tempo."

## curiosities
**Progenitors of Punk** : The New York Dolls were widely considered the progenitors of punk before it had a name, known for their outrageous and cross-dressing style. # genre - Punk Rock

## curiosities
**Early Uncommerciality** : Initially, punk rock was defining itself in the mid-70s as music for a "blank generation" that eschewed the commerciality of earlier rock waves. **"Blank Generation" Ethos** : In the mid-1970s, punk was defined by a "blank generation" that rejected the commercialism of previous rock waves, but this commercial avoidance was short-lived as bands gained ambition and signed major label deals, leading their music to the Billboard charts. "it was meant to give voice to a so-called blank generation that issued the commerciality of prior waves of rock."
**Multifaceted Sound** : Punk was not a monolithic sound but contained multitudes, evolving and melding with other genres such as post-punk, art pop, new wave, metal, disco, funk, and hip hop. This flexibility allowed its ethos to take many forms and achieve pop chart success. "These bands showed that the punk ethos could take many forms, as it morphed into and melded with post-punk, art pop, new wave, metal, even disco, funk, and hip hop."
**DIY Ethos and Eclecticism** : The "do it yourself" (DIY) philosophy of punk extended to willfully eclectic musical styles, allowing bands to experiment across genres and still embody the punk spirit. "If CBGB was all about do it yourself, weren't all of these willfully eclectic hits a form of DIY?"
**Eclectic Nature at Birth** : From its inception, punk music was eclectic, encompassing diverse sounds like Television's jazzy improvisations, the Ramones' straightforward aggression, and Talking Heads' polyrhythmic approach. It was more an attitude of "no-frill style and DIY" than a single genre, which mirrored the inherent eclecticism of pop music. "So, from its birth, punk was eclectic, the way pop music has always been eclectic."
**Future of Pop** : Despite its initial uncommercial nature, CBGB's punk music, being catchy and arresting, was seen as the future of pop, with the rest of the world eventually catching up to its innovative sound. "CBGB music was simply the future of pop."
**Rejection of Classic Rock Complexity** : Early punk's ethos involved rejecting the "density and instrumental wizardry" of late 1960s and early 1970s classic rock. Instead, it sought to return rock to its "primitive roots" as hard-driving R&B and rock-and-roll, often through covering and recontextualizing older songs. "This was punk's ethos, rejecting the density and instrumental wizardry of late 60s and early 70s classic rock, and returning rock to its primitive roots as hard-driving R&B and rock-and-roll."
