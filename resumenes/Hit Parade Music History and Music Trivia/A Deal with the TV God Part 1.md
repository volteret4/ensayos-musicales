Its comeback was catalyzed by its placement in the TV show "Stranger Things," single-handedly making it a top five hit. The full title of the song is "Running Up That Hill (A Deal with God)." **Television as Music Catalyst** : Television has played a significant role in making songs into hits for a long time, not only by boosting pop stars or creating them, but also by resurrecting previously failed songs and making them into "second chance smashes." In the 21st century, music licensing to TV has become a substantial business, with a well-placed song on a hit show capable of launching an artist's career or turning an oldie into a new classic. "Television. And TV has been making songs hits for a long time. There were pop stars made bigger by TV. Pop stars literally made by TV. But pop songs expressly written for television."
**Music Licensing to TV (21st Century)** : In the 21st century, the practice of licensing music to television shows has evolved into a significant industry. A strategically placed song in a popular TV series can be instrumental in breaking an artist into the mainstream or re-establishing an older song as a contemporary classic. "And in the 21st century, music licensing to TV has become big business. That's the way some artists break wide open. A well-placed song on a hit show could make a career? Or turn an oldie into a new classic."
**Rock Era Commencement (1955)** : Music historians identify 1955 as the start of the so-called "rock era," a period that saw significant changes in popular music. "Music historians call 1955 the start of the so-called rock era."
**Television Penetration (1955)** : By 1955, television sets were present in two-thirds of American homes, indicating a growing reach for the medium as a platform for popular culture and music. "TVs were in just two-thirds of American homes by 55"
**Early TV's Impact on Music Sales (1950s)** : In the early days of television, successes like "The Ballad of Davy Crockett" and "Sixteen Tons" demonstrated to the music industry that TV was an effective medium for selling pop songs and launching pop acts. "Both Davey Crocket and 16 tons proved to the music biz in the early days of TV that the medium was a great way to sell pop songs. It was also a good way to launch a pop act."
**TV Idol Archetype (1960s)** : The "TV idol" archetype, exemplified by Ricky Nelson, continued into the 1960s with teen actresses like Shelley Fabares leveraging their TV stardom for musical success. "There was the TV idol, Alaw Ricky Nelson."
**Plot Explainer Theme Songs (1960s)** : During the 1960s, TV shows commonly featured "plot explainer" theme songs, such as "Gilligan's Island," "The Beverly Hillbillies," and "Green Acres," which aimed to summarize the show's premise in a minute or less. These explanatory themes generally did not become chart hits. "TV was going through its period of the plot explainer theme. Years before the Brady Bunch, themes like, say, Gilligan's Island were trying to summarize everything you needed to know about a show in about a new."
**Successful Theme Song Formula** : For a theme song to achieve radio success, it needed to possess a personality that could stand alone, independent of the show it was tied to. This meant having lyrics that weren't "too ploddy" and a strong musical identity. "The trick with a theme song, if you wanted it to succeed on the radio, was giving it a personality not reliant on the show."
**Divergent Industry Priorities (TV vs. Music)** : The TV and music industries often had different priorities, which could affect a theme song's chart performance. TV themes were designed for broadcast over credits, while music hits required qualities for standalone radio play. "Part of the issue with chart performance was that the TV and music industries had different priorities."
**Accidental TV Hits (1968)** : Television's broad reach allowed it to create hit songs accidentally, as demonstrated by Herb Alpert's "This Guy's in Love with You" in 1968. Viewers' emotional connection to the song's context on TV led to its clamored-for release and chart-topping success. "Indeed, TV's megaphone was so big, it could make a song a hit accidentally."
**Contextual Significance in TV Hits** : The success of a song derived from TV often hinges on its context within the show. Viewers' association of a song with beloved characters or moments on screen is a key factor in its popularity. "Alpert's hit reinforced a key element of TV musical success. Context mattered. It was a hit because viewers associated it with something they loved on their TV screens."
**70s Variety Show Platform** : The 1970s was characterized as the decade of the variety show, offering performers a significant platform to promote their music and turn songs into hits. "The 70s was the decade of the variety show, giving performers yet another platform to promote hits."
**Polished TV Theme Songs (1970s)** : In the 1970s, TV theme songs became more polished, evolving to be both popularized by television and capable of standing on their own as radio hits, including instrumentals and vocal themes. "What really took off in the 70s was the TV theme song, which finally got really polished, popularized by the tube, but able to stand on its own as a radio hit."

"It was the first TV theme to hit number one on the Hot 100 and, depending on how you regard the limited series story song from Davey Crockett, maybe the first such number one hit ever."
**Theme Songs vs. Show Ratings (70s & Early 80s)** : In the 1970s and early 1980s, a TV show did not necessarily need to be a ratings hit for its theme song to connect with audiences and become a chart success, as seen with "Making It" by David Naughton. "In the 70s and early 80s, a show didn't even have to be a ratings hit for its theme song to connect."

**Music Supervisor Role in TV** : The influence of music supervisors in Hollywood grew significantly from the dawn of the 1980s, enabling them to discover and integrate songs that could powerfully drive a plot. This role became crucial in the "General Hospital" model of recontextualized hits and was affirmed by the success of "Miami Vice." "Hollywood music supervisors gained the clout to search farther afield for songs that could motivate a plot."
**Miami Vice Impact on Music (1984-1985)** : NBC's "Miami Vice," conceived as "MTV cops," transformed TV music placement with its highly stylized and impressionistic music sequences. "The Miami Vice soundtrack spent a whopping 11 weeks at number one on the album chart. Still the longest any TV soundtrack has spent on top of that chart."
