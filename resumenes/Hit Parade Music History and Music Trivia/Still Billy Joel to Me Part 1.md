## albums
**Attila (1970)** : This was the one LP released by Billy Joel's proto-metal band Attila, which consisted of Joel on keyboards and his former Hassles bandmate John Small on drums. The album cover featured the duo posed in a meat locker wearing medieval armor, with hair comparable to Led Zeppelin's Robert Plant. The album was not a commercial success and the band broke up within a year, partly due to Joel's affair with Small's wife, Elizabeth Weber. "Attila's album was not a success."
**Cold Spring Harbor (1971) - Solo Debut** : Billy Joel's first solo album, issued on the Family Productions label under a contract he would later regret. The LP was initially mastered at the wrong speed, causing Joel's voice to sound more nasal and high-pitched on some tracks. Despite being a flop, it bubbled under the Billboard album chart at number 202, marking Joel's initial exploration of his future signature style as a piano balladeer and writer of alluring melodies. "The Cold Spring Harbor LP was initially mastered at the wrong speed, making Joel's voice on some tracks more nasal and high pitched."
**Piano Man (1973) - Major Label Debut** : This album, which gave Joel his iconic nickname and persona, was his major label debut after signing with Columbia Records in 1973 due to the strength of his live track "Captain Jack." The album reached the top 40, peaking at number 27, but sold only modestly at the time and took over two years to go gold. "The piano man album also reached the top 40 that spring peaking at number 27 on the album chart, it sold only modestly at the time."
**Streetlife Serenade (1974)** : Joel's 1974 album, which scraped the top 40 on the coattails of its predecessor, "Piano Man," but spent less than half as long on the charts. Joel was still trying to hone his sound, and the album's title track, "Streetlife Serenade," bore a resemblance to the style of Elton John, a chart-dominant piano man of the era. "1974's Street Life Cerenade scraped the top 40 on the coattails of its predecessor, but it spent less than half as long on the charts."
**Turnstiles (1976)** : Released a year and a half after "Streetlife Serenade," this album fared even worse on the charts, peaking at number 122 and leaving the album chart in just 12 weeks. Despite its poor chart performance, the well-reviewed album showcased Joel casting a wider net for musical inspiration and contained many songs that would become fan favorites, including "Angry Young Man" and "Summer Highland Falls," and the eventual standard "New York State of Mind." "A year and a half later Joel's album Turn Styles fared even worse on the charts, peaking at number 122."
**The Stranger (1977) - Breakthrough Album** : This album marked Billy Joel's commercial and creative breakthrough, released in November 1977. It was produced by Phil Ramone, who instilled confidence in Joel's material and supported his use of his touring band instead of studio musicians. The LP reached number two in February 1978, right behind the "Saturday Night Fever" soundtrack, and by the end of 1978, it had spun off four top 40 hits, with "Just the Way You Are" winning Record of the Year at the Grammys. "The Stranger was Billy Joel's commercial and creative breakthrough."
**52nd Street (1978)** : Issued one year to the month after "The Stranger" debuted, this album was named after Manhattan's famed mid-20th century jazz performance center. Benefiting from the momentum of "The Stranger," it shot to number one on the album chart in only its fourth week. The album broadened Joel's musical scope, incorporating harder rock elements influenced by punk and invoking contemporary California pop styles, culminating in an upset win for Album of the Year at the Grammy Awards in February 1980. "Fifty second street benefited from the coattails of the stranger and it shot to number one in only its fourth week on the album chart."
**Glass Houses (1980)** : Released in 1980, this album was a self-conscious attempt by Joel to shatter his "piano man" persona, reflected in its title and a cover photo of Joel holding a rock in front of a plate glass window. Despite critics like Jimmy Gooterman calling it "a package of bluster," the album's career reboot worked, making Billy Joel a quintessential 1980s multi-genre rock star. The album was number one on the Billboard album chart for six weeks, and its first single, "You May Be Right," reached the top 10. "Glass Houses was a self conscious attempt down to its title and the cover shot of Joel holding a rock in front of a plate glass window to shatter his prior piano man persona."
**The Nylon Curtain (1982)** : A more experimental and thematically unified album for Joel, largely an homage to John Lennon after his assassination in December 1980, featuring double-tracked vocals and evocative, often downbeat lyrics. Joel's mood during its creation was also affected by a motorcycle accident in April 1982 and the impending divorce from his first wife, Elizabeth Weber. The album's lyrics chronicled the American experience on a large scale, notably addressing traumatized Vietnam veterans in "Goodnight Saigon," while still maintaining pop hooks despite its experimental package. "The Lennon sound marked by double tracked vocals and evocative lyrics was all over Billy Joel's 1982 studio album the nylon curtain."

## songs
**She's Got a Way (1971)** : This song was the lead-off track from Billy Joel's 1971 solo debut LP, "Cold Spring Harbor," and was inspired by his future first wife, Elizabeth Weber, with whom he had an affair while she was married to his Attila bandmate, John Small. From its initial release, the song showcased the sound that would later define Joel as a superstar, even though the album itself struggled due to mastering issues and a problematic contract. "Elizabeth Weber would later become Joel's first wife, and the inspiration for a number of his early songs, including this one."
**Captain Jack (1972)** : Joel debuted this apparent "stoner anthem" during a live radio performance at Philadelphia's Sigma Sound studio for progressive rock station WMMR in April 1972, while on tour to promote "Cold Spring Harbor." Despite Joel later attesting it was an anti-drug song whose protagonist was a "pathetic loser," its lines like "Captain Jack will get you high tonight, just a little push, and you'll be smiling" resonated with AOR radio. The seven-minute track was repeatedly spun by WMMR for a year, directly leading to Joel's 1973 contract with Columbia Records, which signed him on the strength of this song. "Joel would later attest that the song was anti-drug. Its protagonist was, to him, quote, a pathetic loser."
**Traveling Prayer (1973)** : Featured on Joel's 1973 Columbia debut, this song demonstrated his eclecticism by being essentially a country song, dominated by a banjo played by Eric Weisberg. The track was so authentically country-sounding that it was recorded by actual country artists, with banjo player Earl Scruggs issuing his version less than a year after Joel's, and Dolly Parton later covering it a quarter-century later. "Traveling Prayer, for example, was essentially a country song, dominated not by Joel's piano, but by a banjo, played by Dooling Banjo's performer, Eric Weisberg."
**Piano Man (1973)** : This iconic, piano-based song chronicles the sad lives of barflies in a pub, requesting songs and "put[ting] bread in my jar." Despite its title, its primary instrumental hook is played on a harmonica, which Joel would often perform himself, attaching the harp to his neck in Bob Dylan's style, whom he cited as an influence. The song, described as a pop folk / Tin Pan Alley meets Desolation Row hybrid, debuted on the Hot 100 in February 1974 and peaked at number 25 by April, finally getting Billy Joel onto the charts. "But its primary instrumental hook is played not on piano, but on a harmonica."
**Streetlife Serenade (1974)** : The title track from Joel's 1974 album of the same name, which bore more than a passing resemblance to the style of Elton John, a prominent piano man of that era. This comparison highlights Joel's ongoing experimentation and efforts to hone his sound during his early career. "The albums title track, Street Life Cerenade, bore more than a passing resemblance to the chart dominant piano man of the era, Billy's future friend, Elton John."
**Say Goodbye to Hollywood (1976)** : The lead single from Joel's 1976 album "Turnstiles," this song was a knowing homage to the 1963 Ronettes hit "Be My Baby." Joel recreated both the singing style of Ronnie Spector and the distinctive drum beat from Phil Spector's production, showcasing his ability to channel and synthesize specific influences. "Joel was doing his best Ronnie Specter imitation, recreating both the singing style and even the drum beat of the immortal Phil Specter produced 1963 Ronets Hit Be My Baby."
**New York State of Mind (1976)** : An album cut from "Turnstiles" that became one of Joel's most played radio tracks, despite never being issued as a 45 RPM single and thus being ineligible for the Hot 100 at the time. Written after Joel spent three years living and recording on the West Coast, the song reflected his homesickness for his home state and invoked the feel of classic crooners like Tony Bennett, with Joel also stating he aimed to emulate the soulful playing style of Ray Charles. "New York state of mind was never issued as a 45 RPM single."
**Angry Young Man (1976)** : A fan-favorite song included on the "Turnstiles" album, highlighting Joel's wider musical inspiration despite the album's poor chart performance. "Despite containing many of the songs that would become fan favorites, including Angry Young Man and Summer Highland Falls."
**Summer Highland Falls (1976)** : Another fan-favorite song featured on the "Turnstiles" album. "Despite containing many of the songs that would become fan favorites, including Angry Young Man and Summer Highland Falls."
**Movin' Out (Anthony's Song) (1977)** : Initially, Joel attempted to lead off "The Stranger" with this song as the first single, but it was overlooked by radio programmers in favor of "Just the Way You Are." When re-issued as a single, it performed better, reaching number 17. The closing piano section was, by Joel's own admission, an interpolation of the closing piano from Eric Clapton's classic "Layla" by Derek and the Dominoes. "The closing piano on the album's first single, move it out Anthony's song. Was reportedly by Joel's own admission an interpolation of the closing piano from the Eric Clapton classic Leila by Derek and the Dominoes."
**Scenes from an Italian Restaurant (1977)** : An epic seven-minute track from "The Stranger" that changes tempos multiple times. This song was Joel's attempt to cross the eclecticism found on the second side of The Beatles' "Abbey Road" with the dramatic scope of mid-70s Bruce Springsteen epics like "Jungleland." "The epic scenes from an Italian restaurant which changes tempos multiple times over the course of its seven minute running time. Was Joel's attempt to cross the eclecticism of the second side of the Beatles' Abbey Road with the drama of mid-70s Bruce Springsteen Eppics like Jungle Land."
**She's Always a Woman (1977)** : A drumless ballad from "The Stranger" that was elevated by stately and more polished arrangements than any of Joel's prior albums. It became a number 17 hit, one of four top 40 singles from the album. "Even the ballads such as the drumless she's always a woman were kicked up a notch with stately arrangements more polished than on any prior Joel album."
**Just the Way You Are (1977)** : This jazzy, sax-inflected mellow track from "The Stranger" was almost left off the album because Joel and his bandmates found it "overly sappy." However, visiting singers Linda Ronstadt and Phoebe Snow convinced him to keep it. Released in November 1977, it became Joel's first top ten hit in February 1978, peaking at number three, and went on to win Record of the Year at the Grammys. "It took fellow singers Linda Ronstadt and Phoebe Snow who visited Joel in the studio to convince him the sentimental song was worth keeping."
**Only the Good Die Young (1977)** : A Catholicism satire and pro-lust anthem from "The Stranger" that started as a slower-paced reggae song. Drummer Liberty DeVito persuaded Joel to change it to a boogie-woogie shuffle beat. Despite its libidinous lyrics causing discomfort at some radio stations, it reached number 24, becoming one of four top 40 hits from the album. "The Catholicism satire and pro-lust anthem only the good die young was a slower-paced reggae song until drummer Liberty DeVito persuaded Joel to give it a boogie-wogie shuffle beat instead."
**Big Shot (1978)** : The opening track of "52nd Street," this song was a snotty downtown guy's evisceration of uptown Studio 54-era proto-yuppie culture, with references to fashion designer Halston and the Upper East Side restaurant Elaine's. The Beastie Boys later covered it live numerous times, playing it at a speed closer to what Joel originally intended. "It was a snotty downtown guys evisceration of uptown studio fifty four era proto yuppie culture with references to fashion designer Halston and the upper east side Seenster restaurant Elaine's."
**My Life (1978)** : The first single from "52nd Street," this California-style pop song was a meta-hit, talking about California in its lyrics, even though Joel had returned to the East Coast. It channeled the sound of contemporary West Coast artists like Jackson Browne, Linda Ronstadt, Rickie Lee Jones, and Randy Newman. The song peaked at number three on the Hot 100 in January 1979, matching "Just the Way You Are," and was later repurposed as the theme song for the Tom Hanks and Peter Scolari sitcom "Bosom Buddies." "The album's first single was about as meta as a hit gets a California style pop song at the peak of California pop that talks about California in the lyrics."
**Honesty (1978)** : The third single from "52nd Street," this more traditionally Joel-like piano ballad was nominated for Song of the Year at the Grammy Awards in February 1980. Its nomination highlighted the album's broad appeal and critical recognition alongside its commercial success. "The piano ballad called honesty was a nominee for song of the year."
**You May Be Right (1980)** : This guitar rock song was the first single from the "Glass Houses" album and helped the album reach number one even before its second single was released. The piano, typically central to Joel's sound, was pushed "way back in the mix," emphasizing Joel's intentional shift towards a more rock-forward sound and away from his "Piano Man" persona. "You could hear its singer and songwriter, Billy Joel, pounding away on the piano as usual. But not much about this song was usual for Joel."
**It's Still Rock and Roll to Me (1980)** : Released as the second single from "Glass Houses," this song became Billy Joel's first Hot 100 number one hit in July 1980. It's a self-referential track where Joel, at 30, engages in a "skitso" conversation with himself, contrasting an "old man rockabilly Joel" baffled by new wave trends with a "new wave Joel" sneeringly contemptuous of the past, with different vocal effects for each persona. This clever commentary on aging and trend-hopping helped Joel embrace new wave while retaining his rockabilly pulse, and the single topped the charts concurrently with the "Glass Houses" album. "Has there ever been a more self referential chart topping hit than it's still rock and roll to me."
**Don't Ask Me Why (1980)** : The third and least rock-driven single from "Glass Houses," this song showcased Joel's continued genre-hopping with gentle Latin percussion and a melody that fused the sounds of Paul McCartney and Paul Simon. It specifically alluded to the Latinesque ballads found in McCartney's early Beatles work and Simon's light ethnic-derived folk, reaching number 19 in September 1980. "Don't ask me why was yet another genre hopper for Joel with gentle Latin percussion and a melody that fused the sound of two Paul's McCartney and Simon indeed it alluded to the Latinesque ballad of Paul McCartney's early Beatles work."
**Goodnight Saigon (1982)** : A deliberately bleak track from "The Nylon Curtain" that chronicles the experiences of traumatized Vietnam veterans. This song exemplified the album's ambitious, large-scale chronicling of the American experience, moving beyond personal observations to thematic social commentary. "Most notably traumatized Vietnam veterans on the deliberately bleak Elijayak good night Saigon."
**Pressure (1982)** : The lead-off single from "The Nylon Curtain," released in November 1982, reaching number 20. Described as a "synthesizer symphony," this song found Joel delving deeper into trendy new wave, featuring synth hooks, a pounding beat, and paranoid lyrics about a society gone mad. It was Joel's overt bid for MTV success, accompanied by a high-concept music video directed by Russell Mulcahy, which utilized slow-motion special effects and relentless edits to match the song's jittery mood. "The pressure was in essence Joel's overt bid for MTV success."
**Allentown (1983)** : The second single and biggest hit from "The Nylon Curtain," reaching number 17 in February 1983. Originally titled "Levittown" (a Long Island suburb near Joel's upbringing), Joel switched the title to the Pennsylvania town, a former stronghold of the iron and steel industry, transforming it into an ode to blue-collar workers. The song fused influences from Bruce Springsteen's "jaundiced eye" and John Lennon's double-tracked vocals, with its music video styling Joel as a modern-day Woody Guthrie. "Allentown turned into an ode to blue collar workers after Joel switched the title to the Pennsylvania town. A former stronghold of the iron and steel industry."

"It is both element about aging by a man who'd only just turned 30 and a sly commentary on trend hopping."

**"The Nylon Curtain" - John Lennon Homage** : Billy Joel's 1982 album, "The Nylon Curtain," was largely an homage to John Lennon, influenced by his assassination in December 1980. The album incorporated elements of the "Lennon sound," characterized by double-tracked vocals and evocative lyrics. "Having invoked McCartney long time Beatles fan Billy Joel's next studio album would be largely an homage to John Lennon in tribute to the former Beatles after his assassination in December 1980."
**"The Nylon Curtain" Delayed by Motorcycle Accident** : The creation and release of "The Nylon Curtain" were delayed by a couple of months due to a motorcycle accident Billy Joel suffered in April 1982. "Joel's mood may have been affected not only by John Lennon's death but by a motorcycle accident that Joel suffered in April 1982 which delayed the album by a couple of months."
**"The Nylon Curtain" Context of Divorce** : During the creation of "The Nylon Curtain" LP, Billy Joel's marriage to Elizabeth Weber was falling apart, and they would divorce a few months before the album's release, contributing to the album's often downbeat and impressionistic lyrics. "In addition during the LP's creation Joel's marriage was falling apart he and Elizabeth would divorce a few months before the albums release."
**"Pressure" as MTV Bid** : "Pressure" was Billy Joel's "overt bid for MTV success," strategically released after MTV had launched. The song's synth hooks, pounding beat, and paranoid lyrics made it sound like a music video even on the radio, and its high-concept clip by director Russell Mulcahy featured slow-motion effects and relentless edits for the visual medium. "The pressure was in essence Joel's overt bid for MTV success."
**"Allentown" Title Change** : The song "Allentown" was originally titled "Levittown," referring to a Long Island suburb near where Billy Joel grew up. Joel later changed the title to "Allentown," a Pennsylvania town known as a former stronghold of the iron and steel industry, transforming the song into an ode to blue-collar workers. "Originally titled Levittown about a long island suburb near where Joel grew up Allentown turned into an ode to blue collar workers after Joel switched the title to the Pennsylvania town."

"What finally got Billy Joel on the radio was when he re-oriented his piano playing toward an AOR power anthem."

**WMMR (Philadelphia Progressive Rock Station)** : WMMR was Philadelphia's leading progressive rock station that aired a live radio performance by Billy Joel and his band from Sigma Sound in April 1972. The station's DJs and listeners "loved" Joel's debut of "Captain Jack," and WMMR put the seven-minute live version into regular rotation for the next year, significantly boosting Joel's career and leading to his Columbia Records contract. "They did it for Philly's leading progressive rock station, WMMR."
**Sigma Sound (Philadelphia Studio)** : This famed Philadelphia studio was the location of a live radio performance by Billy Joel and his band in April 1972 for WMMR. It was during this performance that Joel debuted several unrecorded songs, including "Captain Jack," which became a key factor in his subsequent success. "Joel and his band did a live radio performance at the famed Philadelphia studio Sigma Sound."

**Fading of Disco (1980)** : In 1980, disco music began to fade commercially, prompting several hit acts from the 1970s to pivot towards "retro rock sounds" to achieve new hits. "Disco began to fade in 1980 several hit acts from the 70s turned toward retro rock sounds to score hits."

"MTV had launched kicking off the music video era in America."
