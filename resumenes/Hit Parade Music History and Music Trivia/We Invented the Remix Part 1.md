# artist - Gloria Gaynor

## albums
**Never Can Say Goodbye (1975)** : Producer Tom Moulton built Side A of this album as a seamless disco suite, comprising three songs ("Honey Bee," "Never Can Say Goodbye," and "Reach Out I'll Be There") designed to keep the dance floor rolling for 18 minutes. # curiosity

**Remix Definition Evolution** : The concept of a remix, invented half a century ago, has evolved significantly from its origins as a DJ's art form designed to keep dancers on the floor longer. It soon migrated to pop charts, where it could reinvent dormant album cuts into radio smashes, turn non-hit acts into chart-toppers, or become the definitive version of a song, encompassing re-thinks, reboots, reinventions, and re-recordings.
"Whatever you call it, Cold Heart Penow Remix represents virtually all of the things a remix has become half a century after the concept was invented."
**Billboard's Charting Rules for Remixes** : The classification of remixes significantly impacts how Billboard tallies them for its charts. Artists and record companies often engineer remixes specifically to ensure that multiple versions count toward a song's overall chart position.
"This matters because this classification affects how Billboard tallies remixes for its charts."
**Remix as Genre Crossover Tool** : A truly novel and creative remix has the power to cross a song over to a completely new audience and different genres, as demonstrated by the Imanbek remix of Saint Jhn's "Roses."
"Moreover, a truly novel remix can cross a song over to a whole new audience."
**Creativity and Remix Culture Roots** : Creativity has always been at the heart of the remix, which is more than just a technical technique; it's a deep-seated culture. Its origins predate common imagination and were not initially in the United States.
"Creativity was always at the root of the remix, which is more than a technique. It's a culture."
**Jamaican Sound System Origins of Remix** : The earliest roots of remix culture trace back to Jamaican sound systems pioneered in Kingston in the 1940s and 50s. These involved DJs using turntables and custom-built, bass-heavy speakers to entertain crowds, flipping and cutting between records based on audience reaction.
"The Jamaican sound system pioneered in Kingston consisted of at least one turntable or more. And custom built speakers amplified to emphasize heavy bass lines."
**Dub as an Art Form** : By the early 1970s, the live manipulation of records in Jamaica had evolved into the art form known as Dub, spearheaded by studio and sound system pioneers like King Tubby. These "Dub wizards" would create backing tracks for "toasters" (proto-rappers) like U Roy, actively mixing elements in and out on the fly.
"By the turn of the 70s, this had been elevated into an art form known as Dub."
**DJ Kool Herc and the Birth of Hip Hop** : Kingston-born DJ Kool Herc brought the techniques of the Jamaican sound system, including live manipulation and elongation of records through his "merry-go-round" method of playing instrumental breaks, to the Bronx, New York. These innovations fundamentally spawned American hip hop, making the history of rap music synonymous with the history of the remix.
"Kingston-born Clive Campbell, the legendary DJ Cool Herk, brought the techniques of the Jamaican sound system and the live manipulation and elongation of records to the Bronx, New York."
**Tom Moulton's Re-edit Innovation** : The recorded remix, specifically the re-edit, was pioneered in the early 1970s by Tom Moulton. His first such creation was a 45-minute dance mix for Fire Island Club The Sand Piper, meticulously compiled on reel-to-reel tape by splicing together rock and soul records and excising less danceable segments to create continuous dance floor energy. This monumental task took him 80 hours to complete.
"His first such creation was itself not a remix, strictly speaking, but a re-edit."
**Seamless Album Suites and Dance Floors** : While songs flowing directly into each other had been present on rock LPs since The Beatles or The Moody Blues, Tom Moulton innovated this concept for dance music with Gloria Gaynor's 1975 album "Never Can Say Goodbye," crafting Side A as an 18-minute seamless disco suite specifically to maintain continuous dance floor activity. This predated similar seamless albums by Donna Summer or KC and the Sunshine Band.
"Mind you, songs flowing directly into each other had been tried on rock LP's Dating to the Hay Day of the Beatles or The Moody Blues."
**The Remix as Reinvention Era** : The era of the remix as a true reinvention began with the work of celebrated producer DJ Walter Gibbons, particularly with his 12-inch remix of Double Exposure's "Ten Percent," which impressed other DJs with its seemingly "endless" quality.
"So began the era of the remix as ReInvention."
**Mainstream Acceptance of Remixes** : By the late 1970s, remixers began to gain wider recognition with the general public. Hits like Donna Summer's "I Feel Love" played a role in familiarizing audiences with the sounds of electronic dance production, paving the way for remixes to cross over.
"By the late 70s, Remixers were starting to cross over with the general public."
**Commercial Availability of Remixes in the 80s** : Remixes became more widely commercially available by 1983 and 1984, with certain mainstream pop hits generating significant top 40 radio airplay in their remixed forms.
"By then, remixes were becoming more widely commercially available."
**Remixes as Big Business in the Late 80s and 90s** : From the late 1980s through the 1990s, remixes, including those that incorporated re-recorded elements, became a substantial business. They frequently served as the mechanism by which iconic album cuts were transformed into radio or club smashes.
"From the late 80s through the 90s, remixes, including those with re-recorded elements, were big business, and often the way Leconic album cuts became radio or club smashes."
