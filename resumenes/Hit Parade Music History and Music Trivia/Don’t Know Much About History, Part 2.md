## albums
**Night Beat (1963) - Moody, Bluesy Concept Album** : Described as a "lightly conceptual concept album," Night Beat came out in the summer of 1963, shortly after the drowning of Cooke's 18-month-old son Vincent. Cooke's grief came out in the music, and an unmistakable melancholy runs through songs like "Nobody Knows the Trouble I've Seen" and "Lost and Lookin'." His version of Willie Dixon's "Little Red Rooster" was recorded for the album. "When the moody and bluesy night beat album came out later that summer, an unmistakable melancholy infused songs like Nobody Knows the Trouble I've See."
**Ain't That Good News (1964) - Final Studio Album** : Cooke began recording it at the start of 1964, and it turned out to be his final studio album. Once again he worked the history of African American song into his pop. It was released on March 1, 1964. "A Change Is Gonna Come" was placed on side B and was not meant to be a single. The album produced further hits in 1964, including "Good Times" and Cooke's cover of "The Tennessee Waltz." "He began the year recording what would turn out to be his final studio album, Ain't That Good News."
**Live at the Harlem Square Club 1963 (1985) - Posthumous Live Album** : Recorded in January 1963 in Miami before a mostly Black crowd at the Harlem Square Club. It was a gritty show for an enraptured audience, and at one point Cooke turned his favorite Nat King Cole standard from light jazz into a boisterous sing-along jam. Fans who were not there did not hear the recording for more than two decades, until it was released posthumously on LP in 1985. It is now considered Cooke's best live recording and often appears on all-time greatest album lists, including Rolling Stone's. The show showed that while courting a white pop audience, Cooke still commanded a Black room and could code-switch. "Sam Cook was still a master in a black room, able to code switch, definitely."
**Sam Cooke at the Copa (1964) - Last LP Issued in His Lifetime** : In July 1964, more than six years after he "bombed" at the Copacabana nightclub, Cooke returned to the Copa. His set was received rapturously and released as a live album later that year. It closed a chapter of his career as a final triumph and was the last Sam Cooke LP issued while he was alive. "The at the Copa album was the last Sam Cook LP issued in his lifetime."
**Shake (1965) - First Posthumous Album** : The title track came out as a single one month after Cooke's death, and its title became the name of his first posthumous album. "Shake, was the title track of what would become Cook's first post-traumus album."

## songs
**Wonderful World (1960)** : This single brought Cooke closer to the pop Top 10 than he had been in years, which pushed his new label RCA to step up its efforts. Along with "Chain Gang," it set the template for Cooke's 1960s work: soulful pop songs with bright, infectious melodies that offered an alternative to the new Motown sound. "Like wonderful world, chain gang set a template for Cook in the 60s."
**Chain Gang (1960)** : Released three months after "Wonderful World," it reached No. 2 on both the R&B and pop charts. It is a jaunty, joyous tune about a despairing subject: prison labor and, by implication, the mass incarceration of African Americans. Cooke's friend and fellow touring singer Lou Rawls said Cooke wrote it after they drove past a real chain gang working in the hot sun by the road. Cooke built the beat from the metallic clank of prisoners' shackles and from the "Ha" chant a real chain gang might use to pass the time. The song brought a social conscience to his music in 1960, well before the peak of the civil rights movement, expressing sorrow and regret while still making you tap your foot. It stayed his highest-charting pop hit until "Shake" in 1965. "Chain Game was a jaunting joyous tune, disguising a truly despairing topic, prison labor."
**Cupid (1961)** : Written by Cooke himself, it became one of his most acclaimed songs. AllMusic's Bill Janovitz called it "a perfect pop song that combined Latin, R&B, jazz, and mainstream pop elements," and Rolling Stone ranked it among its 500 greatest songs of all time. It was a solid crossover hit. Its No. 17 peak on the Hot 100 was slightly higher than its No. 20 peak on the R&B chart, which was unusual for Cooke at that point. The Spinners' 1980 cover reached No. 4, higher than the original. "Cupid became one of his most acclaimed songs."
**Stand By Me Father (c. 1959-1960)** : Cooke and J.W. Alexander wrote and arranged it for The Soul Stirrers on SAR Records, and it was one of the group's most inventive SAR recordings. It interpolated Charles Albert Tindley's turn-of-the-century gospel standard "Stand By Me," changing a gospel hymn into something closer to a pop ballad with a very memorable refrain. Ben E. King credited both Tindley's hymn and especially Cooke's interpolation as the source of his 1961 hit "Stand By Me." "In truth, the bones of Stand By Me Father were not new."
**Twistin' the Night Away (1962)** : A classic party record that went No. 1 R&B and No. 7 pop, showing Cooke's versatility. Rod Stewart later covered it. "He could score with party records, like his classic number one R&B, number seven pop hit, Twist in the Night Away."
**Having a Party (1962)** : A party record that reached No. 4 R&B and No. 17 pop. "The number four R&B, number seventeen pop hit, having a party."
**Bring It On Home to Me (1962)** : A slow-dance soul ballad that reached No. 2 R&B and No. 13 pop in the summer of 1962. Like "Chain Gang," it was a polished pop/R&B song with more depth than it first showed. Cooke drew on a line from Odetta's arrangement of the post-Civil War spiritual "Oh Freedom" ("before I'll be enslaved, I'll be buried in my grave") and recast it as a love song. He did this a full year before Joan Baez sang "Oh Freedom" at the March on Washington, which shows his awareness of social justice movements well before 1964. Rod Stewart later covered it. "Cook re-contextualizes that line in the guise of a love song."
**Another Saturday Night (1963)** : A danceable yet ruminative party song about having no one to party with. It went Top 10 pop and No. 1 R&B in the spring of 1963. In 1974 Cat Stevens took his cover to No. 6 on the Hot 100, a few places higher than Cooke's original. "A party song about not having anyone to party with."
**Frankie and Johnny (1963)** : Cooke's follow-up to "Another Saturday Night" was his take on the decades-old murder ballad, which had been recorded by everyone from Louis Armstrong to Johnny Cash. In the 1960s it was a Top 40 hit for both Brook Benton and Elvis Presley, but Cooke's version was the biggest. It reached No. 14 pop and No. 4 R&B in late summer 1963. "But the biggest hit version of all was Cook's."
**Little Red Rooster (1963)** : Cooke's version of the Willie Dixon blues standard was recorded for Night Beat as a strutting soul record with jazzy percussion. In fall 1963 it reached No. 11 pop and No. 2 R&B, the highest-charting version of the song. "Sam Cook's take on Rooster was a strutting soul record with Jazzy Percussion."
**Nobody Knows the Trouble I've Seen (1963)** : A Night Beat track filled with the melancholy that followed the drowning of Cooke's son Vincent. "Lost and Lookin'" ("I'm lost and looking for my baby") carries the same mood. "His sadness came out in the music."
**Ain't That Good News (1964)** : The album's title track is down-home, Southern-flavored R&B based on a 19th-century Black spiritual of the same name. It was on the charts the week in February 1964 when Cooke went to Miami to watch Cassius Clay beat Sonny Liston. That week it sat at No. 13 on the Hot 100 on its way to an No. 11 peak, while The Beatles held No. 1. "The song was at number 13 on Billboard's Hot 100 that week on its way to a number 11 peak."
**A Change Is Gonna Come (1964)** : Cooke wrote it to his own guitar accompaniment in December 1963 while preparing material for Ain't That Good News. He had been thinking he needed to write his own "Blowin' in the Wind" since the March on Washington. He played it for J.W. Alexander a couple of days after Christmas, said he didn't know where it came from and that it might have come to him in a dream, and told him, "I think my daddy will be proud." Biographer Peter Guralnick calls it "both more personal and more political," with a gospel-like melody that comes from no specific spiritual, and notes it has no single antecedent: it doesn't sound like Odetta or mimic Dylan, though it owes something to Dylan's spirit. Guralnick quotes the deceptively simple lyrics ("it's been too hard living, but I'm afraid to die"; "I go to the movie and I go downtown, somebody keep telling me, don't hang around"). Arranger René Hall's orchestration made it sound less like a folk song and more like Nat King Cole's ornately arranged "Nature Boy." It was recorded in January 1964 and placed on side B of the album, not as a single. Cooke performed it live only once, with an orchestra on NBC's The Tonight Show with Johnny Carson, about two weeks before the Clay-Liston fight. It was reportedly a titanic performance, but no tape survives. It came out as the B-side of "Shake" one month after his death and peaked at No. 31 pop in early March 1965, just barely Top 40, while reaching the R&B Top 10. It was one of the first civil rights songs to be so self-reflective, written weeks before Nina Simone's "Mississippi Goddam." Covers include Otis Redding, Aretha Franklin (1967), and Jennifer Hudson at the 2020 Democratic National Convention. "It was perhaps the most succinct vision in song of how racism is lived."
**Good Times (1964)** : A hit from Ain't That Good News. Aretha Franklin covered it on her 1967 breakthrough album. "The ink that Good News album spun off further hits, like Good Times."
**The Tennessee Waltz (1964)** : Cooke's cover was another hit from Ain't That Good News. "And Cook's cover of The Tennessee Waltz."
**Shake (1965)** : RCA released it as a single one month after Cooke's death. It is an exuberant dance record in the line of "Twistin' the Night Away," "Having a Party," and "Another Saturday Night." It reached No. 7 on the Hot 100 in late February 1965, his highest-charting pop hit since "Chain Gang" reached No. 2 in 1960. Its B-side was "A Change Is Gonna Come." Rod Stewart later covered it. "Shake was an exuberant dance record in the tradition of past Cook hits."

## curiosities
**Founding SAR Records - Sam, Alex and Roy** : In 1959, while still on Keen Records, Cooke heard that his former gospel group The Soul Stirrers had been dropped by Specialty Records. He offered to write and produce for them, including new vocalist Johnnie Taylor, and proposed releasing the recordings himself. With J.W. Alexander, his business and songwriting partner who had advised him on launching his publishing company, he formed a label just to record the group. SAR stood for Sam, Alex and Roy: Cooke, Alexander, and S.R. (Silas Roy) Crain, Cooke's former mentor and ongoing advisor from The Soul Stirrers. Before Motown had scored any real hits, Cooke envisioned SAR as a haven for Black performers and saw the work as a form of advocacy. "Cook envisioned the S.R. label as a haven for black performers."
**RCA's Second Best-Selling Act** : By 1962 Cooke was the second best-selling act on RCA Records, behind only Elvis Presley. Once established at RCA, he stayed with the label for the rest of his career. "By 1962, he was the second best-selling act on RCA Records after Elvis Presley."
**Refusing Segregated Shows** : While touring relentlessly and building his reputation as one of the most dynamic stage performers of his generation, Cooke refused to play separate shows for white and Black audiences, even in the Deep South. "He was living out his principles by refusing to play separate shows for white and black audiences, even in the deep south."
**1958 Car Crash - Driver Killed** : In November 1958, on the way from St. Louis to Greenville, Cooke's convertible hit an idling truck. Cooke, Lou Rawls, and guitarist Cliff White were hospitalized, and Cooke's driver Ed Cunningham was killed. "Cook's driver, Ed Cunningham, was killed."
**Death of First Wife Dolores (1959)** : A car crash in 1959 killed Cooke's first wife, Dolores. They were already divorced and he had remarried, to Barbara, but Cooke paid for Dolores's funeral. "Cook paid for Dolores' funeral expenses."
**Death of Son Vincent (June 1963)** : In June 1963, at Cooke's Los Angeles home, his 18-month-old son Vincent wandered away from his mother and the housekeeper, fell into the swimming pool, and drowned. Cooke was inconsolable and withdrawn, and friends like Lou Rawls and J.W. Alexander could not get him to open up. He kept touring and recording. "But 1963 brought the greatest tragedy of all."
**Clay vs. Liston in Miami (February 1964)** : Cooke went to Miami to watch his friend Cassius Clay beat Sonny Liston for the World Heavyweight Championship, and spent the night in a hotel room with Clay, Jim Brown, and Malcolm X. "He went to Miami to watch his friend, Cassius Clay, defeat Sunny Liston to become World Heavyweight Champion."
**Inspired and Bothered by Blowin' in the Wind** : Contrary to the film One Night in Miami, Cooke already knew Dylan's song and had written and recorded a song inspired by it by February 1964. Biographer Peter Guralnick writes that Cooke "was so carried away with the message, and the fact that a white boy had written it, that he was almost ashamed not to have written something like that himself." "Sam Cook loved blowing in the wind."
**Fired Up by the March on Washington** : Guralnick writes that Cooke was energized by Mahalia Jackson appearing alongside Dr. King at the March on Washington, including when she sang "How I Got Over." That same year Cooke and his SAR team recorded The Soul Stirrers singing "Free at Last," the spiritual King referenced in his "I Have a Dream" speech. "Sam Cook had been thinking he needed to write his own blowing in the wind as far back as the march on Washington."
**Lost Tonight Show Performance** : About two weeks before the Clay-Liston fight, Cooke performed "A Change Is Gonna Come" on NBC's The Tonight Show with Johnny Carson with an orchestra. It was his only live performance of the song, reportedly a titanic one, and no tape exists. "Sadly, no tape of the Tonight Show performance exists."
**Death at the Hacienda Motel (December 11, 1964)** : Cooke was killed at the Hacienda Motel in Los Angeles in an incident still shrouded in mystery. The motel's manager, Bertha Franklin, said she shot him in self-defense when a drunken Cooke moved to attack her. His family and supporters have questioned that account ever since Los Angeles authorities investigated and closed the case. His death is often grouped with the 1990s murders of Tupac Shakur and Biggie Smalls as among the most disputed deaths of a Black musical luminary, and as with them, hundreds of thousands of fans lined the streets in the days afterward. "In a senseless incident that remains shrouded in mystery to this day."
**Influence on James Brown** : The florid orchestrations and gut-bucket vocals of "A Change Is Gonna Come" can be heard in James Brown's 1966 hit "It's a Man's Man's Man's World," and its social conscience echoes through Brown's "Say It Loud – I'm Black and I'm Proud." "It cast a long shadow over the next half-decade of Black Popular Music."
**One Night in Miami - Malcolm X Record Scene Inaccuracy** : In the 2020 film, Kingsley Ben-Adir's Malcolm X plays Cooke's records to mock their shallowness, implying Cooke recorded nothing but pablum through 1964. Slate's Jack Hamilton points out this is wrong on two counts. Malcolm plays songs six to seven years old, such as "You Send Me," and the scene ignores Cooke's socially conscious hits from those years, such as "Chain Gang" and "Bring It On Home to Me." "The thing is Sam's music was deep."
**One Night in Miami - Blowin' in the Wind Scene Inaccuracy** : The film suggests Cooke needed Malcolm X to shake him into political material. "A Change Is Gonna Come" was recorded in January 1964 and was about to be released on the night depicted. Malcolm and the others may not have known it existed, since the album came out March 1 and the song was on side B. "It is, as my slate colleague Jack Hamilton points out, a distortion of the record."
**SAR Owned the Rights to It's All Over Now** : In the film, Leslie Odom Jr.'s Cooke says he gave The Rolling Stones permission to cover Bobby Womack's song over Womack's objection, and that because his company owned the rights, the royalties went into "our pockets." The core is true, but the film's chart claims are largely invented (see The Valentinos and The Rolling Stones). "My company owns the rights to the song."
