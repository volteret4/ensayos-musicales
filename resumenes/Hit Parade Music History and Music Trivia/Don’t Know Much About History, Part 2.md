## albums
**Night Beat (1963) - Reflecting Personal Tragedy** : Released in the summer of 1963, this moody and bluesy album was infused with an unmistakable melancholy, reflecting Sam Cook's inconsolable sadness after the tragic drowning of his 18-month-old son, Vincent, in June of that year. Songs like "Nobody Knows the Trouble I've Seen," "Lost Everything," and "Lost and Looking" express his grief. "When the moody and bluesy night beat album came out later that summer, an unmistakable melancholy infused songs like Nobody Knows the Trouble I've See."
**Ain't That Good News (1964) - Final Studio Album** : Cook's final studio album, recorded at the beginning of 1964, showcases his clever ways of infusing pop with African American song history. The title track is based on a venerable 19th-century black spiritual. The album also famously contained "A Change Is Gonna Come" on its B-side, though it was not initially released as a single. "He began the year recording what would turn out to be his final studio album, Ain't That Good News."
**Live at the Harlem Square Club, 1963 (1985) - Legendary Posthumous Release** : Recorded in January 1963 for a predominantly black crowd in Miami, this album captures a gritty performance for an enraptured audience, demonstrating Cook's mastery in a "black room" and his ability to "code switch." The recording was posthumously issued on LP in 1985 and is now considered Cook's best live recording, frequently ranking on all-time greatest album lists like Rolling Stone's. "Now considered Cook's best live recording, live at the Harlem Square Club 1963, frequently ranks on all-time greatest album lists, like Rolling Stones."
**Live At The Copa (1964) - Career Redemptive Performance** : This live album captures Sam Cook's rapturously received set at the Copacabana Nightclub in July 1964, marking a significant triumph six years after his initial "bomb" at the venue. It closed a career chapter for Cook and was the last Sam Cook LP issued during his lifetime. "The at the Copa album was the last Sam Cook LP issued in his lifetime."
**Shake (1965) - First Posthumous Album** : Sam Cook's first posthumous album, titled after its exuberant dance record A-side, was released a month after his death. The album continued the tradition of past Cook hits like "Twist in the Night Away" and "Having a Party". "Shake, was the title track of what would become Cook's first post-traumus album."

## songs
**Wonderful World (Year Unspecified) - Template for 60s Sound** : This song brought Sam Cook closer to the Pop charts' top 10 than he had been in years, marking a significant step in his artistry. It set a template for his 1960s sound: soulful pop songs with bright, infectious melodies, providing an alternative to the emerging Motown sound. "After a wonderful world brought Sam Cook closer to the top 10 on the Pop charts than he had been in years, his new label, RCA, knew they had to step up."
**Chain Gang (1960) - Socially Conscious Pop Hit** : Released three months after "Wonderful World," this single reached No. 2 on both the R&B and Pop charts. Cook wrote this "ingenious, deceptively deep pop song" after witnessing an actual chain gang, building its beat from the clank of shackles and prisoners' chants. It brought a social conscience to his music, addressing prison labor and mass incarceration of African Americans, well ahead of the civil rights movement's peak. "Chain Game was a jaunting joyous tune, disguising a truly despairing topic, prison labor."
**Cupid (1961) - Acclaimed Crossover Hit** : One of Sam Cook's most acclaimed songs, which he penned himself. All Music's Bill Janovitz later called it "a perfect pop song" that blended Latin, R&B, jazz, and mainstream pop elements. Rolling Stone ranked it among its 500 greatest songs of all time, and it was a solid crossover hit, peaking at No. 17 on the Hot 100 (unusually higher than its No. 20 R&B chart peak for Cook at that time). "All music's Bill Janovitz would later call it, quote, a perfect pop song that combined Latin, R&B, jazz, and mainstream pop elements."
**Twist in the Night Away (Year Unspecified) - Classic Party Record** : A classic party record that became a number one R&B hit and reached number seven on the Pop charts, showcasing Cook's versatility. "On the one hand, he could score with party records, like his classic number one R&B, number seven pop hit, Twist in the Night Away."
**Having a Party (Year Unspecified) - Popular Party Record** : Another successful party record for Cook, which hit number four on the R&B charts and number seventeen on the Pop charts, further demonstrating his range. "as well as the number four R&B, number seventeen pop hit, having a party."
**Bring It On Home to Me (1962) - Profound Soul Ballad** : A deeply felt soul ballad and slow dance classic, this song reached number two on the R&B charts and number thirteen on the Pop charts. Like "Chain Gang," it was a polished pop/R&B song with more profound depth than initially apparent. Cook re-contextualized a line from Odetta's "Oh Freedom" in this love song, illustrating his awareness of social justice movements and folk music a full year before Joan Baez sang "Oh Freedom" at the March on Washington. "As with Changer, two years earlier, Bring It On Home to Me was a polished pop slash R&B song that was more profound than it first appeared."
**Another Saturday Night (1963) - Ruminative Dance Smash** : Released in the spring of 1963, this song was a top 10 Pop and number one R&B smash. It's described as a danceable yet ruminative party song about not having anyone to party with. "In the spring, he scored another top 10 pop, number one R&B smash, with his danceable yet ruminative song, Another Saturday Night."
**Frankie and Johnny (1963) - Highest Charting Murder Ballad** : Cook's follow-up hit to "Another Saturday Night," this was a spin on the decades-old story song "Frankie and Johnny," an oddly sprightly murder ballad. Cook's version was the biggest hit of all, reaching number 14 Pop and number four R&B in the late summer of 1963, even surpassing versions by Louis Armstrong, Johnny Cash, Brooke Benton, and Elvis Presley. "But the biggest hit version of all was Cook's."
**Little Red Rooster (1963) - Highest Charting Blues Standard** : A strutting soul record with jazzy percussion, this track was Sam Cook's follow-up to "Frankie and Johnny" and appeared on his "Night Beat" album. Cook's take on the Willie Dixon blues standard reached number 11 Pop and number two R&B in the fall of 1963, making it the highest charting version of that classic tune. "Sam Cook's take on Rooster was a strutting soul record with Jazzy Percussion."
**Nobody Knows the Trouble I've Seen (1963) - Song of Grief** : Featured on the moody and bluesy "Night Beat" album, this song, along with "Lost Everything" and "Lost and Looking," reflected the profound melancholy that infused Cook's music in the wake of his son Vincent's tragic drowning in June 1963. "an unmistakable melancholy infused songs like Nobody Knows the Trouble I've See."
**Ain't That Good News (1964) - Spiritual-Based R&B Hit** : The title track of Cook's final studio album, this song is a down-home, southern-flavored R&B track based on a venerable 19th-century black spiritual. It was on the charts in February 1964, reaching number 13 on Billboard's Hot 100 on its way to an eventual number 11 peak, coinciding with Cassius Clay's victory over Sonny Liston. "Was a kind of down-home, southern-flavored R&B based on a venerable black spiritual that dated to the 19th century, Ain't That Good News?"
**A Change Is Gonna Come (1964) - Posthumous Civil Rights Anthem** : Recorded in January 1964 and released on the B-side of the "Ain't That Good News" album, this song was monumentally different from anything Sam Cook had done before, being both more personal and political. Written by Cook himself in December 1963, inspired by his reflections on "Blowin' in the Wind" and the March on Washington, it vividly brought to mind a gospel melody but didn't come from any particular spiritual. Orchestrated by Renee Hall to sound like Nat King Cole classics, it was one of the first self-reflective civil rights songs and came early in the lineage of Black Pride and Black Power Anthems. Though only performed once live (on The Tonight Show with Johnny Carson), and not intended as a single, it posthumously became a modest pop hit (No. 31 Hot 100) and a Top 10 R&B hit three months after his death in March 1965, casting a long shadow over subsequent Black Popular Music. "Of a change is going to come, Peter Giralnik writes, quote, it was a song both more personal and more political, a song that vividly brought to mind, a gospel melody, but that didn't come from any spiritual number in particular."
**Good Times (Year Unspecified) - Post-Album Single** : This song was one of the hits that spun off the "Ain't That Good News" album after its release. Aretha Franklin covered it on her 1967 breakthrough album "I Never Loved a Man the Way I Love You." "The ink that Good News album spun off further hits, like Good Times."
**The Tennessee Waltz (Year Unspecified) - Album Cover Track** : Sam Cook's cover of this song was another hit that spun off the "Ain't That Good News" album after its release. "and Cook's cover of The Tennessee Waltz."
**Shake (1965) - Posthumous Dance Hit** : Released posthumously one month after his death, this exuberant dance record was the A-side of a single and the title track of Cook's first posthumous album. It reached number 7 on the Hot 100 in late February 1965, becoming his highest charting pop hit since "Chain Gang" in 1960. "Shake, was the title track of what would become Cook's first post-traumus album."

## curiosities
**Early Career Transition** : Sam Cook began his career as a revered gospel legend before successfully pivoting to secular music, a move that laid the groundwork for his extensive influence across soul, pop, and R&B. "I talked about how Soul Legend Sam Cook began his career as gospel legend Sam Cook. And how he pivoted to secular music."
**One Night in Miami Factual Inaccuracies** : The 2020 film "One Night in Miami" reimagines aspects of Cook's career, compressing multiple years of accomplishments into early 1964. It inaccurately portrays Cook's music through 1964 as shallow and apolitical, ignoring deep, socially conscious hits like "Chain Gang" and "Bring It On Home to Me" which predate the film's timeline, and misrepresents the timing and inspiration behind "A Change Is Gonna Come." "I began a fact check of the film, which compresses multiple years' worth of his accomplishments into early 1964."
**Pioneering Entrepreneurship with SAR Records** : Even before leaving Keen Records, Sam Cook began establishing his own label, SAR Records, in 1959 with J.W. Alexander and Silas Roy Crane. Cook envisioned SAR as a haven for black performers, making former bandmates like The Soul Stirrers "new again" by writing and producing for them, and saw this work as a form of advocacy well before Motown had significant hits. "Cook envisioned the S.R. label as a haven for black performers."
**Artistic Versatility** : By 1962, Sam Cook was the second best-selling act on RCA Records after Elvis Presley, consistently flaunting his versatility. He could score with upbeat party records like "Twist in the Night Away" and "Having a Party," while simultaneously recording profoundly felt soul ballads such as "Bring It On Home to Me." "Cook was flaunting his versatility."
**Refusal of Segregated Shows** : Sam Cook actively lived out his principles by refusing to play separate shows for white and black audiences, even when touring in the Deep South, demonstrating his commitment to social justice beyond his music. "And he was living out his principles by refusing to play separate shows for white and black audiences, even in the deep south."
**Tragic Personal Losses** : Cook endured immense personal tragedies, including a 1958 car crash that killed his driver Ed Cunningham and hospitalized himself, Lou Rawls, and Cliff White. In 1959, his first wife Dolores was killed in another car crash (though divorced, Sam paid for her funeral). The greatest tragedy struck in June 1963 when his 18-month-old son Vincent drowned in the family swimming pool, leaving Cook inconsolable and incommunicative, with his sadness channeled into his music. "But 1963 brought the greatest tragedy of all. In June, at Sam's home in Los Angeles, his 18-month-old son Vincent wandered away from his mother and their housekeeper, fell into their swimming pool and drowned."
**Influence of Civil Rights and Folk Music** : Cook was deeply aware of social justice movements and folk music well before 1964, taking inspiration from figures like Odetta, the "Queen of American Folk Music." He incorporated lines from spirituals like Odetta's arrangement of "Oh Freedom" into his songs, demonstrating an early social conscience that fueled his later work, including "A Change Is Gonna Come." "This illusion offers ample evidence that Cook was aware of both social justice movements and the focusing well before 1964."
**Inspiration from "Blowin' in the Wind"** : Sam Cook was deeply moved and "carried away with the message" of Bob Dylan's "Blowin' in the Wind," to the point of being "almost ashamed not to have written something like that himself," particularly given it was written by a white artist. This profound impact spurred Cook to write his own social anthem, "A Change Is Gonna Come." "The fact is Sam Cook had been thinking he needed to write his own blowing in the wind as far back as the march on Washington."
**Posthumous Legacy** : Sam Cook's death on December 11, 1964, at the Hacienda Motel in Los Angeles, remains shrouded in mystery, with many questioning the official self-defense narrative. Like Tupac Shakur and Biggie Smalls, his passing cemented his legend, leading to hundreds of thousands of fans lining the streets for his funeral and the posthumous release of his masterpiece "A Change Is Gonna Come," which profoundly influenced subsequent Black Popular Music and became a lasting anthem. "In the popular imagination, Cook's death sits alongside the 1990s murders of Tupac Shakur and Biggie Smalls among the most disputed deaths of a black musical luminary."

# genre - Gospel

## curiosities
**Evolution to Pop Ballad** : The gospel standard "Stand By Me," written by Charles Albert Tindley around the turn of the century, underwent an evolution in the hands of Sam Cook and J.W. Alexander. Their interpolation, "Stand By Me Father" for The Soul Stirrers, changed the original gospel hymn into something closer to a pop ballad with a memorable refrain, directly influencing Ben E. King's smash hit "Stand By Me." "In cooks and Alexander's hands, their interpolation, stand by me father, changed from a gospel hymn to something closer to a pop ballad, with a very memorable refrain."

# label - SAR Records

## curiosities
**Vision for Black Performers** : Formed by Sam Cook, J.W. Alexander, and Silas Roy Crane (S.A.R.), this label was envisioned as a haven for black performers from its inception. Cook actively used it as a platform for advocacy, supporting artists like his former group The Soul Stirrers by writing and producing for them, establishing this vision before Motown truly scored any major hits. "From the start, before competing label Motown had really scored any hits yet, Cook envisioned the S.R. label as a haven for black performers."

# concert - March on Washington

## curiosities
**Catalyst for Social Anthems** : The March on Washington in 1963, highlighted by Mahalia Jackson's presence and performance of "How I Got Over" and Joan Baez's rendition of Odetta's "Oh Freedom," served as a significant catalyst for Sam Cook. Cook was "fired up" by the event and began contemplating the need to write his own powerful social anthem, leading to the creation of "A Change Is Gonna Come." "The fact is Sam Cook had been thinking he needed to write his own blowing in the wind as far back as the march on Washington."

# curiosity

**One Night in Miami (2020 Film) Inaccuracies** : The film "One Night in Miami" is noted for its significant dramatic license regarding musical timelines and inspirations. It inaccurately compresses multiple years of Sam Cook's accomplishments into early 1964, misrepresents the depth and political awareness of his music through Malcolm X's fictionalized criticisms, and incorrectly places the timing of key songs like "It's All Over Now" and "Blowin' in the Wind" relative to the February 1964 setting. "I began a fact check of the film, which compresses multiple years' worth of his accomplishments into early 1964."
**Posthumous Impact of Black Musical Luminaries** : The deaths of black musical luminaries like Sam Cook, Tupac Shakur, and Biggie Smalls are tragically linked by their controversial circumstances and the profound posthumous impact they had. In all cases, their disputed deaths led to massive public outpouring of grief and the release of influential material that shaped future generations of music. "In the popular imagination, Cook's death sits alongside the 1990s murders of Tupac Shakur and Biggie Smalls among the most disputed deaths of a black musical luminary."
**The British Invasion - Initial US Chart Performance** : While the Beatles famously kicked off the British Invasion in February 1964, and The Rolling Stones quickly achieved a UK No. 1 with "It's All Over Now," their US chart dominance was not immediate. The Rolling Stones' "It's All Over Now" only reached No. 26 in the US, and it took another single, "Time Is On My Side," for them to secure their first Top 10 American hit, indicating a gradual ascent rather than instantaneous chart domination in the US. "In the US, the Stones had not yet become chart dominators, and it's all over now only reached number 26 in late September of 64."
