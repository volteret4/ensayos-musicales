## albums
**Slow Train Coming (1979) - First Christian-Themed Album** : This album marked Bob Dylan's initial foray into Christian-themed music following his conversion. It climbed to number three on the LP chart, becoming his first top three LP since "Desire" three years prior, indicating an initial invigoration of his music on the charts despite personal surprise among fans and collaborators. The album cover features an image of a man carrying a cross. "His 1979 LP Slow Train Coming, his first Christian-themed album, complete with a cover image of a man carrying a cross, climbed all the way to number three."
**Saved (1980) - Second Christian Album** : Following "Slow Train Coming," this album was Dylan's second religious-themed release in the early 1980s. It stalled commercially at number 20 on the LP chart, received poor reviews from critics, and failed to generate any hit singles, marking a decline in the commercial success of his Christian phase. "The former stalled at number 20."
**Shot of Love (1981) - Third Christian Album** : This was Bob Dylan's third and final religious album released in the early 1980s. Commercially, it performed poorly, peaking at number 33 on the LP chart, which was Dylan's lowest-charting LP since 1964. Like its predecessor, "Shot of Love" garnered poor reviews and produced no hits. "The latter at number 33. Dylan's lowest charting LP since 1964, and both albums received poor reviews and generated no hits."
**Infidels (1983) - Post-Christian Album** : Released in the midst of Dylan's decade of reinvention, this album did reasonably well commercially. It reached number 20 on the album chart and achieved gold certification, marking his first LP to do so since "Slow Train Coming." Despite its success as an LP, its lead single "Jokerman" did not commercially impact pop or AOR radio charts. "Infidel's did reasonably well as an LP, reaching number 20 on the album chart and going gold."
**Empire Burlesque (1985) - Synth-Pop Attempt** : This 1985 album featured a synth-pop influence, punctuated by keyboards and guitar playing from members of Dire Straits, whose leader Mark Knopfler had collaborated with Dylan on several previous albums. The album, however, was a commercial stiff, peaking at number 33 on the charts. "But the album was a stiff, peaking at number 33."
**Oh Mercy (1989) - Acclaimed 80s LP** : Released just weeks before the end of the 1980s, "Oh Mercy" is considered Bob Dylan's most acclaimed LP of the decade. The album found Dylan contemplating politics and social mores after the era of Reaganomics, leaning into his middle-aged, "frog-year" voice. Though it only reached number 30 on the album chart, critics hailed it as Dylan's best LP since "Blood on the Tracks." "Critics hailed it as Dylan's best LP since Blood on the Tracks."
**Good as I Been To You (1992) - Traditional Folk Standards** : This album, released in the first half of the 1990s, was one of Bob Dylan's only new albums during a period when he recorded little new material. It consisted entirely of collections of traditional folk standards, showcasing his role as an interpreter of older works. "In the first half of the 90s, Dylan's only new albums were collections of traditional folk standards."
**World Gone Wrong (1993) - Grammy Winner** : Another collection of traditional folk standards, this 1993 album was particularly well-received by critics. It appeared on numerous critics' lists and earned Bob Dylan a Grammy Award for Best Traditional Folk Album. Released at the peak of the grunge era, the album presented Dylan as a world-weary acoustic elder statesman. "It made numerous critics lists and won the Grammy for Best Traditional Folk Album."
**Time Out of Mind (1997) - Platinum Comeback Album** : This album, produced by Daniel Lanois, was recorded with the intention of capturing the sound of old bluesmen like Little Walter and Charlie Patton, and the directness of Buddy Holly's 1950s recordings. Featuring dark, ruminative music infused with thoughts of mortality, it surpassed Dylan's previous collaboration with Lanois, "Oh Mercy," in moodiness, atmosphere, acclaim, and sales. Fuelled by pent-up demand for new Dylan music after a seven-year drought of self-penned material, it debuted at number 10 on the Billboard 200, his first album to debut in the top 10, rode the chart for over six months, and went platinum, becoming his first million-seller in nearly two decades. "Time out of Mind would not only surpass Dylan's previous collaboration with Daniel Lannois, oh mercy, in terms of moodiness and atmosphere, it also garnered even greater acclaim and much stronger sales, fueled by pent-up demand for new Dylan music after seven years of no-new self-pend material."
**Love and Theft (2001) - Post-9/11 Release** : Released on September 11, 2001, this self-produced album served as a follow-up to "Time Out of Mind." Despite its grim release date, the album was anything but grim, noted for being perhaps Dylan's most rollicking and loose album ever, and deeply steeped in American history. The title was inspired by Eric Lott's book "Love and Theft, Black Faced Minstrelsy, and the American Working Class," and its songs explored various forms of pre-rock musical Americana, from hillbilly music to vaudeville and black spirituals. It was instantly embraced by the public, debuting at a lofty number 5 on the Billboard 200, making it Dylan's first top 5 album since 1979's "Slow Train Coming." "When the album chart data came back a couple of weeks after 9-11, Love and Thaft had debuted on the Billboard 200 at a lofty number 5, instantly making it Dylan's first top 5 album since 1979's Slow Train Coming."
**Modern Times (2006) - First #1 Album of the Aughts** : Five years after "Love and Theft," Dylan released this similarly loose-limbed collection of old-timey blues, folk rock, and rockabilly. The album title was ironic, as its "modern" aspects were primarily its availability as an iTunes download and the inclusion of contemporary references in its lyrics, such as Alicia Keys in the opening track "Thunder on the Mountain." "Modern Times" debuted at number 1 on the Billboard 200, fueled in part by a special promotion from Apple featuring an iPod silhouette ad for its single "Someday Baby." "When the album chart data came back a couple of weeks after 9-11, Love and Thaft had debuted on the Billboard 200 at a lofty number 5, instantly making it Dylan's first top 5 album since 1979's Slow Train Coming."
**Together Through Life (2009) - Second Consecutive #1** : Bob Dylan's third album of the 2000s, "Together Through Life," also debuted at number 1 on the Billboard 200. Sonically, it blended the jaunty feel of "Love and Theft" with the morose atmosphere of "Time Out of Mind," featuring the addition of accordion from Los Lobos member David Hidalgo and songwriting contributions from Robert Hunter, a longtime Grateful Dead lyricist and one of the few co-writers Dylan had ever composed with. Its opening sales were down nearly 70,000 copies from "Modern Times," likely due to the absence of an iPod ad, but it solidified Dylan's streak of consecutive chart-topping studio albums since "Blood on the Tracks" (1975) and "Desire" (1976). "When together through life debuted at number 1 on the Billboard 200, it gave Bob Dylan his first pair of consecutive chart-topping studio albums since Blood on the Tracks and Desire in 1975 and 1976."
**Christmas in the Heart (2009) - Surprise Holiday Album** : Released in late 2009, this Christmas CD caught the media and public by surprise, generating considerable amusement due to Dylan's croaking voice taking on traditional holiday chestnuts like "Here Comes Santa Claus" and "Silver Bells." While some critics, like Michelangelo Matos, found it hilariously "fruitcake," others praised Dylan's guilelessness. It sold well for a holiday album, topping Billboard's holiday albums chart and charting at a sturdy number 23 on the All-Genre Billboard 200, signaling Dylan's shift towards being a song interpreter. "The media had a bit of a laugh. Okay, a lot of a laugh. When Bob dropped Christmas in the heart, hearing the now croaking voiced Dylan take on such chestnuts as, here comes Santa Claus, the Christmas song, and silver bells. Prompted at least one article with the headline, isn't it ironic?"
**Shadows in the Night (2015) - Sinatra Tribute** : This album marked a significant shift in Dylan's career, as he took on music he did not compose, specifically honoring Frank Sinatra. All of Dylan's selections were songs that "Old Blue Eyes" had either recorded or made famous. Dylan clarified on his website that he wasn't "covering" these songs but "uncovering them, lifting them out of the grave," delivering sincere and touching interpretations with arrangements that shamelessly placed his vocals front and center. The album peaked at number 7 on the Billboard 200, continuing his streak of Top 10 studio albums. "What me and my band are basically doing is uncovering them, lifting them out of the grave and bringing them into the light of day."
**Fallen Angels (2016) - Second Sinatra Tribute** : Following the success of "Shadows in the Night," this was Bob Dylan's second album of standards, also largely comprised of songs associated with Frank Sinatra. Like its predecessor, "Fallen Angels" performed well on the charts, peaking at number 7 on the Billboard 200, further solidifying Dylan's ongoing popularity as an interpreter and extending his streak of Top 10 studio albums. "So much so that, in 2016, Dylan issued a second album of standards called Fallen Angels, that again was composed largely of songs associated with Sinatra."
**Rough and Rowdy Ways (2020) - Acclaimed Album** : Released in 2020, this acclaimed album achieved commercial success, peaking at number 2 on the album charts. It continued Dylan's late-career run of critically and commercially successful original material, further cementing his reputation as a continuously evolving artist. "In the last five years, the first half of the 2020s, Bob Dylan has released two more albums, 2020s acclaimed Rough and Routy Ways, a number two hit."
**Shadow Kingdom (2023) - Personal Retrospection** : This album, released in 2023, peaked at number 7 on Billboard's album sales chart and is regarded as perhaps Dylan's most personal reminiscence. It is composed largely of new recordings of songs from the early days of his career, reinterpreting tracks like "Most Likely You Go Your Way (And I'll Go Mine)," "It's All Over Now, Baby Blue," and a particularly affecting take on his 1974 classic "Forever Young." "It is composed largely of new recordings of songs from the early days of Dylan's career, from most likely Hugo, Your Way, and Algo Mine to It's All Over Now, Baby Blue."

## curiosities
**Spiritual Conversion in the Late 1970s** : In November 1978, while on tour in San Diego, Bob Dylan experienced a profound spiritual shift after picking up a small silver cross thrown on stage by an audience member. Feeling spiritually empty due to poor reviews for his latest tour, film "Renaldo and Clara," and recent albums, he felt the overwhelming presence of Jesus Christ a couple of nights later in Tucson, Arizona. A girlfriend, who had recently joined the evangelical Vineyard Christian Fellowship in Tarzana, California, introduced him to the church. Raised Jewish, Dylan underwent a three-month discipleship course and soon announced he had become a born-again Christian, which greatly surprised his fanbase and studio collaborators, to whom he began proselytizing. "To say the least, Dylan's conversion came as quite a surprise to his fan base, including some of his studio collaborators, who reported that Dylan began proselytizing to them."
**1980s Career Search** : The 1980s were a decade of intense searching for Bob Dylan, as he grappled with how to navigate the evolving music landscape dominated by MTV and the New Wave era. His brief religious conversion served as a metaphor for this broader period of reinvention, characterized by experimenting with different sounds and styles in an attempt to maintain commercial relevance. "You might say that Dylan's brief religious conversion was a metaphor for his entire 1980s. It was a decade of searching for Dylan, figuring out how he wanted to proceed in the MTV and New Wave era."
**Guest Vocal on "We Are the World" (1985)** : Bob Dylan made a brief guest vocal appearance on USA for Africa's charity mega-single "We Are the World" in 1985, which remains the only number one song in Hot 100 history to feature a Dylan lead vocal, however brief. Footage from the recording session, recently resurrected in the Netflix documentary "The Greatest Night in Pop," showed Dylan to be anxious about his performance and bewildered by his presence on the Quincy Jones-produced charity record. "But as shown in the footage of The We Are the World recording, which was recently resurrected in Netflix's hit documentary The Greatest Night in Pop, Bob was anxious about his performance and positively bewildered by his presence on the Quincy Jones-produced Charity Record."
**Lyric Contribution to "Jammin' Me" (1987)** : Bob Dylan contributed lyrics to Tom Petty and The Heartbreakers' 1987 single "Jammin' Me," a song that reached number one on the album rock chart and number 18 on the Hot 100. The song poked fun at 1980s celebrity culture, and though Dylan did not sing on it, Petty later revealed that the lyrics specifically slamming actress Vanessa Redgrave and comedian Eddie Murphy were Bob Dylan's ideas. "Dylan contributed lyrics to Tom Petty and The Heartbreakers 1987 single Jammin' Me, a number one album rock, number 18 Hot 100 Hit. The song poked fun at 80s celebrity culture, and though Dylan did not sing on it, Petty later revealed that the lyrics slamming Vanessa Redgrave and Eddie Murphy were Bob's eyes. The idea."
**Collaboration with Daniel Lanois** : Daniel Lanois, a French-Canadian producer known for overseeing blockbuster 1980s albums for U2 and Peter Gabriel and regarded as the prime architect of atmospheric, smoldering rock production, became a pivotal figure for Dylan's sound. Lanois not only brought his ambient aura to Dylan's 1989 album "Oh Mercy" but essentially set a template for Dylan's entire next decade, which focused heavily on creating specific musical "vibes." "Lanois not only brought that ambient aura to Dylan's album, he basically set a template for Bob's next decade."
**"Never Ending Tour" (1990s-Present)** : In the 1990s, Bob Dylan spent a significant amount of his time touring as part of his so-called "Never Ending Tour," which began around that period and remains ongoing to this day. This commitment to live performance continued even during decades when he released less new studio material, establishing a consistent presence on the road. "He spent a lot of his time painting, touring, this was not long after he started his so-called Never Ending Tour, which by the way is still ongoing to this day."
**Fear of Songwriting Well Drying Up** : In a 1991 interview, Bob Dylan expressed a fear that his well of songs had dried up, noting that the prolific songwriting days of the past, when three or four songs would come to him at once, were long gone. He described new songs arriving rarely, "like a bulldog at the garden gate," but often rejected them due to self-doubt about their necessity. This personal struggle motivated his later return to recording new material with Daniel Lanois in 1996. "There was a time when the songs would come, three or four at the same time, but those days are long gone. Once in a while, the odd song will come to me like a bulldog at the garden gate and demand to be written. But most of them are rejected out of my mind right away."
**Near-Fatal Fungal Infection (1997)** : Shortly after completing the recording of his critically acclaimed album "Time Out of Mind," Bob Dylan contracted a near-fatal fungal infection. He recovered just before the album's release, but several critics retrospectively viewed the album's dark, ruminative music as Dylan prematurely writing his own epitaph, given the timing of his health crisis. "Indeed, shortly after completing the album, Dylan contracted a near fatal fungal infection. He did recover just before the CD was released, but several critics later regarded the album as Dylan prematurely writing his own epitaph."
**Grammy Awards Dominance (1998)** : At the 1998 Grammy Awards, Bob Dylan was arguably the star of the night, winning three prizes including the coveted Album of the Year for "Time Out of Mind," making it his first major Grammy win on his own (he had previously shared in the 1973 Album of the Year prize for George Harrison and Friends' "Concert for Bangladesh"). He also delivered the night's most talked-about performance, which included an infamous stage crash. "But, at the 1998 Grammys, Dylan was arguably the star of the night. Not only winning three prizes, including album of the year, but also delivering the night's most talked about performance."

**Shift to Song Interpreter (2010s)** : The release of "Christmas in the Heart" in late 2009 signaled a new artistic direction for Bob Dylan: less of a singer-songwriter and more of a song interpreter. Throughout the 2010s, he would generate several hit albums by taking on music he didn't compose, openly acknowledging this new phase and his intent to "uncover" rather than simply "cover" classic material. "Finally, with hindsight, maybe the most important aspect of Christmas in the heart was how it signaled Dylan's next phase, less singer-songwriter, more song interpreter."

The success of his albums interpreting standards, such as "Shadows in the Night" and "Fallen Angels," suggests that the dedication of "Dylanologists" extends beyond his self-penned material to encompass "whatever Dylan says, whatever he applies his voice to, in song or indeed in song choice," reinforcing his literary stature even when performing others' works. "It's why, in 2016, Dylan didn't just win a Nobel Prize, he won it in the field of literature."

"Nonetheless, it made headlines because it was Dylan's first number one on any Billboard song chart ever in chart history."
