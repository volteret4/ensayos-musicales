# artist - Peter and Gordon

## members
Peter Asher
Gordon

## songs
**A World Without Love (1964)** : This song became a number one hit in the summer of 1964, marking it as the first of three hits written by John Lennon or Paul McCartney that topped Billboard's Hot 100 without being Beatles recordings. Paul McCartney specifically wrote the bridge to finish the song, which he then "bequeathed" to his 19-year-old housemate, Peter Asher. The record notably filled a market gap when The Beatles themselves had no new "product in the pipeline." While John Lennon assessed it as "not fit to be a Beatles song" and it was considered one of the lesser Beatles-related number ones of 1964, it is described as a charming and solid jam that effectively allowed them to "hit the lottery." "Peter and Gordon's A World Without Love, pretty nice little song. I gave it a six out of ten."

# artist - Elton John

## songs
**Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds (1975)** : Elton John's cover of this Beatles song reached number one, even with John Lennon himself present and offering his blessing during its performance. However, critical reception for this cover is largely negative; it's described as "pure stick," a "big, hammy, Vegas performance" that "doesn't need to exist" and "adds nothing" to the original. It is often cited as his "least" effective number one hit, with "Island Girl" considered a superior chart-topper. The cover is seen by some as primarily a "flex," a demonstration of his ability to cover such material with the original artist's approval. "But his cover is kind of everything I don't like about Elton John all sort of rolled into one. It's just pure stick."
**Island Girl (1975)** : This song is recognized as one of Elton John's number one hits. Critically, it is regarded as a better and more substantive number one hit compared to his 1975 cover of "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds." "I think even Island Girl is a better number one hit by Elton John than this one is."

# artist - Stars on 45

## songs
**Medley (1981)** : This medley, featuring various Beatles songs, was the third number one hit to be written by Lennon/McCartney but not performed by The Beatles, topping the charts in 1981. It is noted for its "cheerful sacrilege" and its effective use of collective memory, acting as a "presage" to the sampling and DJ music that would follow, drawing comparisons to Grandmaster Flash's "On the Wheels of Steel" and Girl Talk. The medley combines "little bits and bobs of these other songs," primarily "beloved Beatles chestnuts," and while some transitions are "a little awkward," it "mostly works." The song begins with a "catchy" straight-up disco segment before transitioning into the Beatles pastiche. Despite initial perceptions of being "tacky" and "guileless," its forward-thinking approach to sampling became more appreciated over time. "there's a sort of cheerful sacrilege to that song that I find pretty enjoyable."

# curiosity

**1975 Pop Music Trend - 60s Covers** : The year 1975 witnessed a notable trend in pop music, with approximately five covers of 1960s songs reaching number one on the charts. This trend was exemplified by hits such as The Carpenters' "Please Mr. Postman" and Linda Ronstadt's "You're No Good." It coincided with a broader wave of nostalgia for 1950s and early 1960s rock and roll, marked by the re-emergence of pre-Beatles figures like Frankie Valli and Neil Sedaka. However, Elton John's 1975 cover of the 1967 song "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds" was an outlier, as most nostalgia typically followed a 20-year cycle. "There were a lot of covers of 60 songs that went to number one in 1975. There were like five of them."
**Top of the Pops TV Show** : This British television program was instrumental in making chart following "extremely fun and extremely easy" for a young music fan, who later became a prominent music critic. The show served as a significant cultural touchstone in England, holding a similar place to baseball in American culture for the critic. "but in England, they had top of the pops. Hello, hi, welcome to The Top of the Pop. Short, which made chart following extremely fun and extremely easy and kind of became baseball to me when I couldn't watch baseball anymore."
**Tom Ewing's "Popular" Column** : Tom Ewing has been writing a column titled "Popular" for over a decade in England, which systematically reviews every British number one single. This project significantly inspired Tom Bryan's "The Number Ones" column, which undertakes a similar endeavor for every US Billboard Hot 100 number one single. "It comes from a guy named Tom Ewing, who has been in England writing a similar column for years, more than a decade, I think... Tom writes popular, which is what he calls his column, and it's every British number one single reviewed."
**Sampling and DJ Music Precursors (Early 1980s)** : The "Stars on 45" medley, which achieved number one status in 1981, is highlighted as an early musical work that "presages a lot of the sort of the sampling and the DJ music that would come afterward." Its method of playfully combining "little bits and bobs of these other songs" with a "cheerful sacrilege" was surprisingly forward-thinking, drawing comparisons to contemporaneous works like Grandmaster Flash's "On the Wheels of Steel" and later artists like Girl Talk. "it kind of presages a lot of the sort of the sampling and the DJ music that would come afterward."
