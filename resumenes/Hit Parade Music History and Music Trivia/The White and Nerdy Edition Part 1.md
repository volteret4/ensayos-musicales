**Golden Age** : The 1950s and 60s are identified as the "golden era for goofy hits," a time when comical records frequently topped the Hot 100. "This was the golden era for goofy hits."
**Difficulty in Sustaining Careers** : Novelty records are inherently "flashes in the pan," designed to capture specific cultural moments, which makes it challenging for artists to build sustained careers from them, unlike some holiday-related novelty hits which can be perennials. "But most novelty hits by their very nature are flashes in the pan, meant to capture a specific cultural moment."
**Subjectivity of Definition** : The definition of a novelty record is subjective, as "novelty is in the eye, ear, and funny bone of the beholder." Joel Whitburn and Steve Ottfinoski, two scholars, hold differing views on which songs qualify. "What exactly is a novelty record? Who decides the recording artist, the music industry, the public?"
**Thin Line with Serious Hits** : The border between "serious hits" and novelties is often thin, with many artists throughout history walking this line. "I want to emphasize that the border between so-called serious hits and novelties is thin indeed."
**Early Popularity** : Dating back to the days of Tin Pan Alley, novelty songs were a regular fixture on the "hit parade," and many celebrated singers found them irresistible vehicles for radio play. "dating back to the days of Tin Pan Alley, novelty songs were such a fixture on the hit parade that some of the most celebrated singers found them irresistible vehicles for getting on the radio."
**Producer's Medium in Early Rock** : In the early rock era, novelty records were often considered a "producer's medium," where the artists were seen as incidental to the creative and recording process. "Generally, novelty records were a producer's medium and the artists were incidental."
**Dominance in 1958** : Novelty records were so omnipresent in 1958 that hits openly referenced each other, showcasing a dialogue between contemporary comical releases. "Novelty records were so omnipresent in 1958 that Welles hit even included a reference to another comical smash."
**Boom in Early 60s Chart-Toppers** : By the start of the 1960s, novelty records were regularly topping the Hot 100, with three such hits in 1960 alone, spanning diverse sonic styles. "By the start of the 60s, novelty records were topping the Hot 100 on the regular. 1960 alone had three chart-topping novelty hits, and sonically they were all over the map."
**Blurry Lines Post-British Invasion** : By the late 60s, following the British Invasion and the rise of hippie rock, the distinction between novelty records and rock hits became increasingly blurred and debatable. "By the late 60s after the British invasion and hippie rock began to take over the charts, the line between novelty records and rock hits became blurry."
**Disappearance from Radio in Late 60s** : Novelty records began to disappear from radio airwaves after rock music became more serious in the late 1960s, with airplay often being a limiting factor for funny or novelty records due to the repetition of Top 40 radio rotation. "novelty records were starting to disappear from the radio airwaves after rock turned more serious in the late 60s."
**Cultural Trends in 70s** : The 1970s was a generally "kitschy" decade where no cultural trend, from streaking to CB radios, escaped being satirized or capitalized on by novelty hits. "no cultural trend escaped novelty hit status."
**One-Offs in 70s** : By the 1970s, novelty singles were largely "one-offs," with few artists managing to build sustained careers from them, making artists like Ray Stevens an exception. "by the 70s, novelty singles were largely one-offs."
**Radio Challenges in 80s** : In the 1980s, despite novelty records still being produced, they faced challenges in gaining sustained airplay due to tightening radio formats. "Tightening radio formats meant that most stations would not play comical records in regular all-day rotation, especially after the launch of FM Radio's so-called Morning Zoo programs."

# curiosity
**Break-in Record** : This form of parody hit was invented by producer Dickie Goodman. It involved reinventing spoken-word news broadcasts, such as Orson Welles' "War of the Worlds," by punctuating them with snippets of then-current rock and roll hits, often without permission. "His 1956 hit with partner Bill Buchanan, the Flying Saucer, reinvented Orson Welles' War of the Worlds as a spoken word news broadcast, punctuated by snippets of then-current Rock and Roll hits."
**LP Records & Comedy** : In the early 1960s, at a time when the 33 1/3 RPM album was just starting to surpass the 45 RPM single, live comedy performed in front of an audience proved to be a durable seller of LP records. "At a time in the early 60s when the long playing 33 and a third RPM album was only beginning to overtake the 45 RPM single, live comedy in front of an audience proved a durable seller of LP records."
**Novelty Records in 1980s Radio** : In the 1980s, novelty records faced significant challenges in receiving heavy radio rotation due to tightening formats. While "Morning Zoo" programs on FM radio offered a venue, these shows might be the only time such songs would be played, making it difficult to build sustained careers for novelty artists. "Tightening radio formats meant that most stations would not play comical records in regular all-day rotation, especially after the launch of FM Radio's so-called Morning Zoo programs."
**Saturday Night Live's Musical Impact (1970s)** : The first five years of SNL are noted not only for reinventing TV sketch comedy but also for their underrated impact on the music business. The "Not Ready For Primetime Players" cast members were "music dabblers" whose efforts resulted in significant record sales. "But one underrated aspect of Saturday Night Live's launch was its impact on the music business."
