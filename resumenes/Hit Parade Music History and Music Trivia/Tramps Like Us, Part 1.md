## albums
**Greetings from Asbury Park, New Jersey (1973) - Columbia Records Debut** : This was Springsteen's debut album on Columbia Records, arriving in January 1973. It was a compromise between Columbia staff's preference for a solo acoustic Bruce and Springsteen's desire to rock out with his newly formed band. Half the songs were band-based rock, and half were stripped down and Dylan-esque, but Springsteen lavished the most attention on the band tracks, which received the most acclaim. Despite strong reviews, the album generated no radio hits and sold poorly upon its initial release, though it did establish a base for his live reputation. It made its very first appearance on the Billboard LP's chart on July 26, 1975, alongside his second album, priming the pump for new fans after the hype around *Born to Run*. "Greetings from Asbury Park, New Jersey album generated no radio hits, and sold poorly, despite strong reviews."
**The Wild, the Innocent, and the E Street Shuffle (1973) - Second Studio Album** : Released later in the same year as his debut, this album saw Springsteen and his band solidifying their sound, largely dispensing with the austere approach of his first album in favor of full band rock and roll. Reviews were even stronger than for *Greetings from Asbury Park*, but sales remained poor, largely confined to the New York/New Jersey area. Columbia issued no singles from the album, making them ineligible for the Hot 100. It made its very first appearance on the Billboard LP's chart on July 26, 1975, capitalizing on the buzz created by *Born to Run*. "Reviews for the wild, the innocent, and the East Street shuffle were even stronger than those for the first album, but it still wasn't selling much beyond the New York New Jersey area."
**Born to Run (1975) - Breakthrough Album** : Springsteen's third album, considered a "make or break" release as Columbia Records was unimpressed with his previous sales. Finished in July 1975, it was heavily anticipated after the title track was leaked to AOR stations in early 1974, generating euphoric response and growing his fanbase, which kept Columbia from dropping him. The album shot into the Top 10 on Billboard's LP chart in just two weeks in September 1975, then reached the Top 5 a fortnight later, peaking at number three in late October, just before Springsteen appeared on the covers of *Newsweek* and *Time*. Columbia actively promoted it by taking out ads, booking a tour, and restocking his first two albums. "Born to Run would be his epic, the title track of that third LP, and the single that would make good on the faith his band and his managers, the critics and DJs, had all placed in him."
**Darkness on the Edge of Town (1978) - Post-Lawsuit Transition** : This album was the long-awaited follow-up to *Born to Run*, recorded after Springsteen settled his lawsuits with Mike Appel. Co-produced by John Landau, it marked a significant transition in Springsteen's persona, moving him from an "East Coast centric boardwalk poet" to a "new Americana" figure. Released in June 1978, it shot into the Top 10 on the album chart due to pent-up demand from fans, peaking at number five in July. However, its singles did not achieve significant pop chart success. "darkness on the edge of town would begin the boss's transition from an East Coast centric boardwalk poet to a kind of new vo-americano."
**Born in the USA (1984) - Pop Stardom Album** : Springsteen's seventh studio album, released at the start of summer 1984, in one of the most competitive years for pop music. It controlled the Billboard album chart for all of July 1984. One year later, in July 1985, the LP was still in the Top 5 of the album chart, having already outsold the rest of the Top 5 combined. It generated five Top 10 singles, making it only the third album in chart history to achieve this feat, and by the end, it was not done producing hits. This album propelled Springsteen to Michael Jackson-level popularity, allowing him to tie one of Jackson's most storied Billboard chart records and cementing him as the only guitar rock act to accomplish such pop chart success. "Springsteen's LP controlled the Billboard album chart for all of July."

## songs
**Dancing in the Dark (1984)** : The lead single from *Born in the USA*, released in early summer 1984. It became a chart and MTV phenomenon, marking Springsteen's fastest pop hit ever, peaking at #2 on the Hot 100 in July 1984. "Dancing in the dark became a chart and MTV phenomenon, his fastest pop hit ever."
**Glory Days (1984)** : The fifth Top 10 single from *Born in the USA*. It broke into the Top 10 on the Hot 100 in July 1985, reaching number 9 on the week ending July 13, 1985. This achievement officially made *Born in the USA* only the third album in chart history to generate 5 Top 10 hits. "That's Glory Days, the fifth top 10 single from Born in the USA."
**It's Hard to Be a Saint in the City (early 70s)** : A song that demonstrated the evolution of Springsteen's songwriting, a style he had been developing since the late 1960s. This particular acoustic demo caught the attention of Mike Appel (his first manager/producer) and legendary talent scout John Hammond, leading to Springsteen's signing with Columbia Records in 1972. David Bowie recorded a cover of this song within a year of *Greetings from Asbury Park*'s release, recognizing it as a "glam rock anthem in disguise," though Bowie's version remained unreleased until the 1990s. "It's hard to be a saint in the city, showed how Bruce Springsteen's songwriting had evolved."
**Lost in the Flood (1973)** : A band-based rock track from *Greetings from Asbury Park, New Jersey*, which received significant attention and acclaim. "Songs like Lost in the Flood, For You, and the autobiographical Growing Up."
**For You (1973)** : A band-based rock track from *Greetings from Asbury Park, New Jersey*, which received significant attention and acclaim. "Songs like Lost in the Flood, For You, and the autobiographical Growing Up."
**Growing Up (1973)** : An autobiographical band-based rock track from *Greetings from Asbury Park, New Jersey*, which received significant attention and acclaim. David Bowie recorded a cover of this song within a year of the album's release, though it remained unreleased until the 1990s. "Songs like Lost in the Flood, For You, and the autobiographical Growing Up."
**Spirit in the Night (1973)** : A "Rave Up" song that Columbia Records President Clive Davis compelled Springsteen to write for *Greetings from Asbury Park, New Jersey* in 1972, to ensure the album had a potential single. While not a hit for Springsteen in 1973, it was covered by Manfred Mann's Earth Band in 1975, becoming a minor hit (peaking at #97 on the Hot 100) in April 1976. "The Rave Up Spirit in the Night, and the propulsive blinded by the light."
**Blinded by the Light (1973)** : A "propulsive" R&B-flavored boogie rock jam that Columbia Records President Clive Davis compelled Springsteen to write for *Greetings from Asbury Park, New Jersey* in 1972, to ensure the album had a potential single. It was a rambling, tongue-twisting track with veiled autobiographical tales about impressing girls, featuring the original lyric "cut loose like a deuce" (referring to a two-seat hot rod, inspired by The Beach Boys' "Little Deuce Coupe"). Although not a hit for Springsteen, Manfred Mann's Earth Band's 1976 cover, which tweaked the lyric to "revved up like a deuce" but was widely misheard as "wrapped up like a douche," became a #1 hit on the Hot 100 for the week ending February 19, 1977. This remains Springsteen's only #1 hit credit as a songwriter, and he later acknowledged the misheard lyric likely boosted the song's success. "The song was not actually a hit in 1973."
**Rosalita (Come Out Tonight) (1973)** : A seven-minute "stemwinder" from *The Wild, the Innocent, and the E Street Shuffle* about a guitarist trying to woo a girl whose parents disapprove of his profession. It became a fan favorite and a common, extended closer at Springsteen concerts, crescendoing with a big saxophone solo from Clarence Clemons. "Rosalita come out tonight, a seven-minute stemwinder about a guitarist trying to woo a girl whose parents don't appreciate what he does for a living."
**Fourth of July, Asbury Park (Sandy) (1973)** : A folksy mid-tempo ballad from *The Wild, the Innocent, and the E Street Shuffle*, characterized by a wistful air reminiscent of 1960s Van Morrison, with Bruce often whispering the vocals. About a year after its release, The Hollies covered it, and their easy listening version peaked at #85 on the Hot 100 in early 1975, making history as the first Bruce Springsteen writing credit on the Billboard pop chart. "Fourth of July, Asbury Park, better known as Sandy, was a folksy mid-tempo ballad with the wistful air of 60s Van Morrison."
**Born to Run (1975)** : The epic title track and single from Springsteen's third album, painstakingly labored over by the E Street Band for six months. Springsteen aimed to evoke Phil Spector's "Wall of Sound" and Christmas records, incorporating "walls of guitars," ringing glockenspiel, rumbling drum fills (like motorcycle engines), his lead guitar with a Dwayne Eddy twang, Ronettes-like harmony vocals, and Motown-evoking snares and saxes. The Dylan-esque lyrics, while nominally romantic about tramps, were grandly about America itself. Leaked to AOR stations in early 1974, it generated euphoric response and grew his fanbase, but despite being a 4.5-minute "masterpiece" and being featured on AM Top 40 stations by late 1975, it only peaked at #23 on the Hot 100 and fell out of the Top 40 two weeks later, partly due to the smooth pop landscape of the time. "Never had a pop song tried so self-consciously to be a masterpiece, and it was."
**Thunder Road (1975)** : The majestic opener for the *Born to Run* album. Little Steven Van Zandt, newly a full E Street Band member and an additional "concilieri" for Springsteen during the album's creation, provided backing vocals for this track. "The Majestic Thunder Road."
**10th Avenue Freeze-Out (1975)** : The only other single issued from the *Born to Run* album, it peaked at #83 on the Hot 100 in the winter of 1976. Springsteen himself admits he does not know the meaning of the title. This song marked the last time a Bruce Springsteen single would appear on the Billboard charts for two and a half years. "That song, 10th Avenue Freeze-Out, the only other single issue from Born to Run, peaked at number 83 in the winter of 1976."
**Because the Night (1978)** : A song fragment Springsteen recorded as a demo in 1977 but was having trouble completing. His engineer Jimmy Iovine, who was producing Patti Smith's album *Easter*, asked Springsteen for a song that would suit the punk poet but also be radio-friendly. Springsteen agreed to let Iovine have the track, which then only consisted of a title, some mumbled lyrics, and a melodramatic romantic melody. Patti Smith wrote most of the lyrics herself, recorded it with operatic intensity, and debuted it at CBGB in December 1977, accompanied by Springsteen on guitar and harmony vocals. Credited to both Springsteen and Smith, it became Smith's biggest pop hit, peaking at #13 on the Hot 100 in early summer 1978, and was one of the earliest Top 40 hits from the CBGB scene. "Though both Springsteen and Smith are credited songwriters on because the night, the finished song was not a direct collaboration."
**Prove It All Night (1978)** : A single from *Darkness on the Edge of Town*. This was the only single from the album to crack the Top 40, reaching number 33 in July 1978. "Proof it all night was the only one to crack the top 40 reaching number 33 in July."
**Badlands (1978)** : A single from *Darkness on the Edge of Town*. This follow-up to "Prove It All Night" did not achieve significant chart success, stalling at number 42. "The follow-up, Badlands, stalled at number 42."

## curiosities
**"The Boss" Nickname** : Springsteen earned the nickname "The Boss." "Despite his nickname, The Boss had never had a hit this big before."
**Legendary Concert Band Status** : Springsteen and the E Street Band earned a legend as one of the greatest concert bands of all time, tearing it up on the road, even when pop radio largely ignored his music. "While Springsteen and his E Street band were tearing it up on the road, earning their legend as one of the greatest concert bands of all time, Pop Radio was largely ignoring him."
**Initial Songwriter Success** : For his first decade as a singer-songwriter, Springsteen found more success through others recording his compositions, sometimes taking his songs to the top of the charts before he achieved major chart success as an artist. "Indeed, for his first decade as a singer-songwriter, Bruce scored more with the second part of that job title."
**Uncomfortable with Pop Stardom** : Once Springsteen broke big, he became uncomfortable with aspects of his newfound pop fame, including seeing his songs misinterpreted and misappropriated. "And in many ways that sometimes made him uncomfortable, he saw his songs misinterpreted and misappropriated."
**Chart Performance Anomaly** : Springsteen is an anomaly among singer-songwriters like Bob Dylan and Randy Newman. While all three scored #1 hits as songwriters but peaked at #2 as artists, Springsteen achieved sustained pop success, ringing up a dozen Top 10 hits and 18 Top 40 hits across three decades, unlike Dylan (4 Top 10 hits) or Newman (1 Top 40 hit). "The reason I share this delightful chart factoid about these three rock legends is to point out what an anomaly Springsteen is."
**Pop Icon Status by 1985** : By 1985, Springsteen had transformed into a Madonna and Michael Jackson-level pop star. Tabloids cared about who he was dating, his clothing set fashion trends, and he reflected and shifted commercial music sounds. This eminence was achieved in his 30s through an idiosyncratic path. "Bruce became a Madonna and Michael level pin up worthy pop star."
**"Dylan in the Making" Label** : In the early 1970s, music executives saw the "scrawny warfare at from freehold, gigging around Asbury Park" as a "Dylan in the making," which contributed to his signing by John Hammond to Columbia Records. "Why did they think the scrawny warfare at from freehold, gigging around Asbury Park, was a Dylan in the making?"
**Early Live Shows** : Before the E Street Band was officially named, Springsteen and his early band, including Gary Tallent, Danny Federici, David Sancious, and Clarence Clemons, played fervent shows resembling revival meetings. A then-scrawny Bruce, often in a knit cap, commanded the stage like a preacher in a pulpit. "They played fervent shows resembling revival meetings, with a then-scroney Bruce, often in a knit cap, commanding the stage like a preacher in a pulpit."
**Phil Spector Influence** : Springsteen was deeply influenced by legendary producer Phil Spector, worshipping records by The Crystals, The Ronettes, The Righteous Brothers, and Darlene Love which soundtracked his teenage years. In the 70s, he covered Spector songs like "Then He Kissed Me" and adapted Spector's Christmas arrangements of "Santa Claus Is Comin' to Town" into his live sets, striving to recreate the "Wall of Sound" with the E Street Band. "In Bruce's mind, he was reaching for and haunted by the sound of one legendary producer, Phil Specter."
**Leaking "Born to Run" Single** : Mike Appel, Springsteen's first manager/producer, leaked the recording of "Born to Run" to AOR radio DJs in early 1974, months before the album was complete. This generated euphoric response and grew Springsteen's fanbase, preventing Columbia Records from dropping him. "Mike Appell leaked Springsteen's recording of Born to Run to Radio DJs early in 1974, months before the Born to Run album was even close to complete."
**Managerial and Legal Disputes** : Tensions arose between Springsteen, his first manager/producer Mike Appel, and his new co-producer/advisor John Landau. Springsteen audited his books, discovered Appel controlled his publishing and the majority of his royalties, leading to a two-year period of suing and counter-suing to dissolve their relationship. During this time (second half of the 70s), Springsteen was unable to record as an artist, but he eventually won control of his songs, which proved crucial for profiting from his songwriting. "Springsteen and Appell spent two years suing and counter-suing to dissolve their relationship, making Bruce unable to record until he finally settled and won control of his songs."
**"Edgy for the Top 40" in 1975** : In 1975, despite his emerging fame, Bruce Springsteen was considered "kinda edgy for the Top 40" due to his impassioned street punk style (before punk), and poetic but cryptic lyrics. This contributed to "Born to Run" peaking only at #23 on the Hot 100 amidst a super-smooth, blissed-out pop chart. "in 75, Bruce Springsteen was kinda edgy for the top 40."
**Persona Shift on *Darkness on the Edge of Town*** : With the release of *Darkness on the Edge of Town*, Springsteen underwent a significant persona shift. As described by Slate's Steven Metcalf, "the endearing Jersey Warp Rat in Springsteen had been refined away. In its place was a majestic American simpleton with a generic Heartland twang, obsessed with cars, Mary, the man, and the bitterness between fathers and sons." "In its place was a majestic American simpleton with a generic Heartland twang, obsessed with cars, Mary, the man, and the bitterness between fathers and sons."

## albums

**The River (1980) - Double LP** : Released in 1980, this double LP featured "Hungry Heart" as its lead single, which became his first ever top 5 hit. The album reached number 1 on the Billboard album chart in just two weeks, staying in the top slot for a month, fueled by the single's success. It was critically acclaimed, like his previous albums, and ranked as one of the ten biggest sellers of 1981. Critics called it Springsteen's "first fully adult album," released just after his 31st birthday, with its deep cuts like the title track exploring the breakdown of the American Dream more explicitly. "Hungry Heart was the lead single from Springsteen's 1980 double LP The River."
**Dedication (1981)** : Bruce Springsteen co-produced this entire album for Gary U.S. Bonds in 1981, alongside Little Steven Van Zant. "Springsteen and Little Steven Van Zant co-produced Gary U.S. Bones' entire 1981 album, Dedication."
**Nebraska (1982)** : Following the success of *The River*, Springsteen created this album as a collection of stark demos, recorded at home by himself on a 4-track recorder. Influenced by synth rockers Suicide, particularly Alan Vega's screams on "Frankie Teardrop," Springsteen channeled their style in his own track "State Trooper." Attempts to re-record these songs with the E Street Band in a studio proved unsuccessful, as the "yearning, high-loansome tracks" sounded best on his original homemade tape, which became the album itself. Despite its uncommercial nature, *Nebraska* shot into the album chart top 10 in two weeks, peaking at number three in under a month, with its lead single "Atlantic City" cracking the AOR radio top 10. "On his next album, 1982's Nebraska, Bruce leaned fully bleak."

## curiosities
**Songwriting for Elvis Presley** : Springsteen wrote "Fire" deliberately trying to sound like Elvis Presley and sent a demo to Elvis in 1977, just before the King's death, though Elvis never recorded it. "Fire was a song Springsteen wrote deliberately trying to sound like Elvis Presley."
**Decision to Omit "Fire" from Album** : He chose to leave "Fire" off *Darkness on the Edge of Town* because he felt it was "in Congress with the rest of the LP" and worried that Columbia Records might push it as a single, which he didn't want. "But he left it off the album, thinking it in Congress with the rest of the LP and fearing Columbia records might gravitate toward it as a single."
**Initial Reluctance to Keep "Hungry Heart"** : Going into 1980, Springsteen almost gave away "Hungry Heart" to The Ramones, but his manager, John Landau, "put his foot down" and insisted Bruce keep the song and record it himself for his next album, *The River*, a decision Landau was proven right about. "And going into 1980, Bruce almost did it again."
**First Top 5 Hit** : "Hungry Heart" became his first ever top 5 hit, peaking at number 5 the week of Christmas 1980, which played a significant role in fueling the chart success of *The River*. "It was his third ever top 40 hit, his first ever top 10 hit, and his first top 5."
**John Lennon's Praise for "Hungry Heart"** : On the day of his tragic death, John Lennon, in his final interview, praised "Hungry Heart" as his favorite current song on the radio, seeing it as a "fine companion piece to his own 50s throwback hit." "In fact, John Lennon, on the day of his tragic death in his final interview, praised Hungry Heart as his favorite current song on the radio."
**Intentional Hit Giveaway to Gary U.S. Bonds** : For once, Springsteen "fully intentionally" gave away a hit to another artist, refashioning "This Little Girl" (a *Darkness on the Edge of Town* outtake) to suit classic R&B singer Gary U.S. Bonds. "And for once, Springsteen gave away a hit to another artist fully intentionally."
**"First Fully Adult Album" for Critics** : Critics dubbed *The River*, released just after his 31st birthday, his "first fully adult album," noting its more pronounced exploration of themes like the breakdown of the American Dream. "Critics called the River Springsteen's first fully adult album, released just after his 31st birthday."
**Duality of Celebration and Desolation** : Springsteen's career is characterized by a "dichotomy" between "celebration and desolation," with his "road-tested rave-ups side by side with his Dust Bowl Durgis," a contrast he consistently played both sides of without attempting to settle. "This duality of Springsteen, the celebration and the desolation, his road-tested rave-ups side by side with his Dust Bowl Durgis, it's a dichotomy that Bruce never tried to settle in his career."
**Nebraska as a Home Recording Experiment** : Coming off the success of *The River*, Springsteen recorded *Nebraska* at home, by himself, on a 4-track recorder, a collection of "Stark demos" that tested his ability to create a successful album from raw, uncommercial material. "So, he tested that theory by writing a collection of Stark demos that he recorded at home, by himself on a 4-track recorder."
**Influence of Suicide on "State Trooper"** : Springsteen was "powerfully moved" by the synth rockers Suicide's 1977 debut, particularly Alan Vega's "blood-curdling screams" on "Frankie Teardrop," an influence directly channeled into his own "State Trooper" through its rhythm and harrowing shrieks. "Cutting-edge synth rockers suicide, the musical art project of vocalist Alan Vega and keyboardist Martin Rev, powerfully moved Springsteen when he heard their self-titled 1977 debut, especially Vega's blood-curdling screams on the Vietnam PTSD story, Frankie Teardrop."
**Preference for Homemade Tape Sound for Nebraska** : When Springsteen attempted to re-record the raw songs for *Nebraska* in the studio with the E Street Band, he found that the "yearning, high-loansome tracks" worked best in their original form on his homemade tape, which then became the final album. "When Bruce took these raw songs into the studio and tried to re-record them with the E Street Band, he found that the yearning, high-loansome tracks worked best the way they sounded on his original homemade tape."
**Established as a Rock God, Not Pop Icon** : By 1982, Springsteen was "solidly established as a rock god" but was neither a "pop icon" nor was he attempting to become one. "Bruce Springsteen was now solidly established as a rock god, but he still wasn't a pop icon, nor was he trying to be."
**Framework for Future Success on Nebraska Demos** : Unbeknownst to Springsteen at the time, while creating *Nebraska*, his "least poppy album," he was simultaneously building the framework for his biggest one, as several more rollicking songs he demoed at home but didn't use for *Nebraska* would eventually be recorded by the E Street Band in larger arrangements, despite often having equally bleak lyrics. "Little did Bruce Springsteen realize that, at the same time he had made his least poppy album, he had also built the framework for his biggest one, even if the lyrics to these songs were often just as bleak."

**Springsteen's Fear of Single Choice** : Bruce Springsteen decided to leave his song "Fire" off *Darkness on the Edge of Town* partly because he feared Columbia Records might "gravitate toward it as a single," seeing it as incongruous with the rest of the LP. "fearing Columbia records might gravitate toward it as a single."

# curiosity

**John Landau's Influence on Springsteen's Career** : John Landau, Bruce Springsteen's manager, began advising him to stop giving away his "catchiest tracks," an intervention that prevented Springsteen from giving "Hungry Heart" to The Ramones and instead led him to record it himself, resulting in a smash hit. "John Landau started telling Bruce he needed to stop giving away his catchiest tracks."
**"The Bard of Asbury Park" Epithet** : Bruce Springsteen was referred to by the epithet "the bard of Asbury Park" when Joey Ramone requested a song from him in 1979. "When Joey Ramone met Bruce in 1979, he went ahead and asked the bard of Asbury Park if he could give them a song."
**Breakdown of the American Dream Theme** : This thematic element, first explored in *Darkness on the Edge of Town*, became "more pronounced" on deep cuts of *The River*, such as the title track. "And the Gimlett-Eye themes from its predecessor, Darkness on the Edge of Town, about the breakdown of the American Dream, only became more pronounced on deep cuts like the album's title track, The River."
**Artist Duality of Celebration and Desolation** : Bruce Springsteen's career is characterized by a "dichotomy" between "celebration and desolation," encompassing both "road-tested rave-ups" and "Dust Bowl Durgis," a balance he never tried to resolve, playing both sides throughout his work. "This duality of Springsteen, the celebration and the desolation, his road-tested rave-ups side by side with his Dust Bowl Durgis, it's a dichotomy that Bruce never tried to settle in his career."
**Megastardom as Inspiration** : It is noted that despite its potential drawbacks, "megastardom" can serve as an inspiration for "some great songs." "Megastardum isn't all it's cracked up to be, but it does inspire some great songs."
