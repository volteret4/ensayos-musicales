## genres

## albums
**Do You Wanna Get Away (1985)** : This 1985 album's title track topped the Dance Disco Chart and reached the R&B Top 20, demonstrating Shannon's continued success and the blend of R&B with freestyle elements. "Shannon herself came back in 1985 with a new album, Do You Wanna Get Away, whose title track topped the Dance Disco Chart and reached the R&B Top 20."
## songs
**Let the Music Play (1983)** : Released in the summer of 1983, this song was ubiquitous by 1984 and became freestyle's "galvanizing hit" and first Top 10 hit, peaking at number 8 on the Hot 100 in February 1984, surpassing Madonna's "Holiday." Composed by teenage Puerto Rican producer Chris Barbosa, it featured a "blazing rhythm track" with gated snare (inspired by Arthur Baker/John Roby/Afrika Bambaataa), breakbeat/Latin salsa syncopation, and Roland TR-808 synth sounds. Lyrics, ghost-written by Ann Godwin, focused on club culture and romantic dance-floor dynamics. "Shannon's Let The Music Play...pushed past Madonna's holiday to become freestyle's first top 10 hit."
**Give Me Tonight** : This follow-up hit to "Let the Music Play" aimed to recreate its magic, reaching number 46 on the Hot 100 but more successfully hitting number six on Billboard's R&B chart. Its R&B chart peak suggested freestyle was establishing itself as a "firmly black driven genre." "Shannon herself would try to recreate the magic of Let The Music Play on her own follow-up hit Give Me Tonight. It only reached number 46 on the hot 100, though it did reach number six on Billboard's R&B show."
**Do You Wanna Get Away (1985)** : The title track from Shannon's 1985 album, it successfully topped the Dance Disco Chart and entered the R&B Top 20, highlighting her continued presence in the dance and R&B scenes. "whose title track topped the Dance Disco Chart and reached the R&B Top 20."
## curiosities
**One-Hit Wonder Status** : Despite its significant impact as the "Big Bang moment" for freestyle, Shannon's "Let the Music Play" was her only song to reach the American Top 40, making her a pop one-hit wonder. "Shannon would never return to the American top 40."
**Early Career** : Brenda Shannon Green, who performed as Shannon, was raised in Washington, D.C., and worked as a bookkeeper in New York City. Prior to her hit, she had sung with jazz ensembles and R&B troupes, contributing an R&B foundation to her club-attitudinal vocals on "Let The Music Play." "Raised in Washington, D.C. and as an adult working as a bookkeeper in New York City, Shannon had previously sung with jazz ensembles and R&B troupe."

## songs
**I Wonder If I Take You Home (1985)** : This 1985 hit is a quintessential example of the freestyle movement. It bluntly addresses a teen girl's dilemma about sleeping with her boyfriend, being significantly more explicit in its theme than earlier songs like The Shirelles' "Will You Love Me Tomorrow?", despite lacking curses or overt sex talk. The song features a pulsating, quantized backing track, contrasting with earlier orchestral styles. "Lisa Lisa and Colt Jam's 1985 hit, I wonder if I take you home. ## curiosities
**Formation and Multicultural Identity** : Lisa Lisa and Cult Jam was formed by the Brooklyn rap crew Full Force, envisioning a "multi-cultural black and Latin group." They auditioned Lisa Velis and hired Alex Spanador Mosley (guitarist/bassist) and Mike Hughes (drummer/keyboardist) to form the trio, a structure that aided their marketability on pop radio and MTV. "Full Force were a Brooklyn rap crew, and they were the ones who formed Lisa Lisa and Colt Jam as a multi-cultural black and Latin group."
**1987 Crossover Success & Genre Dilemma** : In 1987, Lisa Lisa and Cult Jam achieved the biggest crossover success for any freestyle act, topping both the Crossover 30 chart and, for the first time, the Hot 100. This raised questions about whether they were moving away from pure freestyle to achieve such mainstream appeal. "the biggest small sea crossover of 1987 by any freestyle act came from Lisa Lisa and cult jam who not only topped the new crossover 30 chart but even in a first topped the hot 100."

# genre - Freestyle
## genres
Post disco
Electro
Latin club music
New Wave Rock
British synth pop
## curiosities
**Origin and Characteristics** : Freestyle emerged from clubs and radio stations in Miami and New York as a hybrid genre. It combined the dance rhythms of post-disco and electro with the romantic yearnings of 80s Latin club music. It was characterized by frenetic beats and impassioned lyrics, often romantic in nature, even with male vocalists. "Emerging from the clubs and radio stations in Miami and New York, freestyle was a hybrid genre that combined the dance rhythms of post disco and electro. With the florid romantic yearnings of 80s Latin club music."
**Mainstream Crossover and Impact** : Freestyle successfully generated hits that crossed over to Top 40 radio, launching the careers of artists who later achieved mainstream pop success, and even influencing established New Wave Rock and British synth-pop artists to seek out freestyle producers. Billboard magazine eventually acknowledged its popularity by giving it its own chart. "Freestyle caught lightning in a bottle, creating stars and generating hits that crossed over at top 40 radio."
**Chart Softening Dilemma** : While energetic in clubs and on radio, freestyle music often had to become "softer" to achieve number one positions on the Hot 100 mainstream charts, indicating a tension between its club sound and broad pop appeal. "But on the mainstream charts, the frustration for freestyle was that to go all the way to the top of the hot 100. The music had to get a lot softer."
**Longevity** : The energetic rhythms of freestyle music persisted beyond the 1980s, carrying into the 1990s and beyond in clubs and on radio, demonstrating its lasting influence on dance music. "But in clubs and on the radio, the energetic rhythms of freestyle carried into the 90s and beyond."
**Bridge to House Music** : Freestyle is identified as an 80s dance mini-genre that served as a bridge between the disco era and the emergence of house music, marking a significant transitional period in dance music history. "the 80s dance mini genre that bridged the disco era into the house era."
**Chart Defining Track - Shannon's "Let The Music Play"** : According to dance historians, Shannon's "Let the Music Play" is considered the "Big Bang moment" for freestyle on the charts, reaching number 8 on Billboard's Hot 100 in February 1984, surpassing Madonna's chart performance up to that point. "no track got more people to the floor than the song that dance historians say started it all on the charts. Freestyle's Big Bang moment."
**Production Complexity** : Despite appearing simple, freestyle tracks featured "deceptively complex" arrangements, often built using "off the shelf first wave 80s synthesizers." This production style provided a "street immediacy" that resonated particularly with hip hop and pop fans, especially Black and Brown audiences. "the seeming simplicity of the production was a feature not above. These freestyle tracks arrangements were deceptively complex, even when they were built on off the shelf first wave 80s synthesizers."
**Name Origin** : The genre name "freestyle" is most likely derived from the group name "Freestyle," associated with Miami producer Pretty Tony's track "Don't Stop the Rock," though the specific backstory is debated. "the group name is credited as the likely origin of the genre name freestyle."
**Mainstream Dilution Dilemma** : The mainstream success of acts like Lisa Lisa and Cult Jam highlighted a dilemma for freestyle: to be embraced outside of clubs and major cities, artists often faced the question of how much they would need to "water down their music," suggesting a tension between artistic integrity and commercial viability. "reveals freestyle's dilemma to be embraced outside of the clubs and the big cities how much would freestyle stars need to water down their music."
