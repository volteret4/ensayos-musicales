## albums
**Bon Jovi (1984)** : The self-titled debut album that debuted on the Billboard album chart in February 1984. Its lead-off single, "Runaway," took 18 months to become a hit and only spent one week in the national Top 40. "The self-titled Bonjovie album debuted on the Billboard album chart in February 1984."
**7800 Fahrenheit (1985)** : The band's second album, from which none of its singles connected at top 40 radio. The album's title, 7800 degrees Fahrenheit, is noted as the supposed melting point for rock. "The album, 78 hundred degrees Fahrenheit, by the way, that temperature is the supposed melting point for rock."
**Slippery When Wet (1986)** : A huge breakthrough album for Bon Jovi and for the "jugular-grabbing pop metal sound" fostered by co-writer Desmond Child. The final 10-song lineup for the album was determined after surveying New York area teens about which songs they liked best from a pool of dozens. The original cover art, depicting a buxom woman in a wet yellow t-shirt, was abandoned for a more anodyne photo of the album's title written with a finger on a wet black garbage bag. It first topped the album chart in October 1986, just weeks before "You Give Love a Bad Name" topped the Hot 100. "Slippery when wet first topped the album chart in October of 1986, just a couple of weeks before you give love a bad name topped the hot 100."
**Latest Chart-Topping Album (2018)** : Bon Jovi scored their latest chart-topping album just weeks before this discussion, under circumstances described as "shady." "Bon Jovi scored their latest chart-topping album under shady circumstances in just weeks ago."

## songs
**You Give Love a Bad Name (1986)** : The 1986 number one hit that solidified Bon Jovi's place in the hair-metal genre, hitting number one on the Hot 100 on the week ending November 29, 1986. It spent only one week at the top, but was a "monster hit" and not the band's last chart-topper. This song marked a significant shift in how metal-adjacent rock was received on the charts and radio, signaling a move towards a more guitar-oriented direction for pop in the latter half of the 80s, much like "Walk This Way" did for rap. Co-written with Desmond Child, it was a "ruthlessly effective radio smash" that demonstrated the band's early understanding of front-loading a song with its catchiest hook. "That, God Help Me, is you give love a bad name. The 1986 number one hit by those hair-metal prom kings, Bon Jovi."
**Runaway (1984)** : Bon Jovi's debut single, which took about 18 months to become a hit after John Bon Jovi first recorded it in 1982 with a different, unofficial group of musicians he called the "All-Star Review." Its popularity on a New York top 40 station in 1983 led to John being signed to a major label contract with Mercury Records. Although it cracked the top 40 in April 1984, it was in and out of the national top 40 in just one week, and the band would not reach the upper echelons of the Hot 100 for another two and a half years. "Here they are with Runaway, Bonjovie."
**Only Lonely (1985)** : The first single released from the band's second album, "7800 Fahrenheit," which peaked at number 54 in the spring of 1985. At this time, radio programmers showed little interest in Bon Jovi, as the pop charts were dominated by artists like Madonna, Wham!, and Tears for Fears. "The album's first single, Only Lonely, peaked at number 54 in the spring of 1985."
**Livin' on a Prayer (1987)** : A powerful, Springsteen-lite, blue-collar anthem, co-written by Desmond Child, John Bon Jovi, and Richie Sambora, released just two weeks after "You Give Love a Bad Name" dropped from the number one spot. The song revived the talk box sound for the 80s, opening with what is now considered arguably the most famous talk box hook in pop history. It reached number one on the Hot 100 in February 1987 and, unlike "You Give Love a Bad Name," maintained its top position for four weeks. "Prayer reached number one on the hot 100 in February 1987, an unlike bad name, it stayed on top for four weeks."

## curiosities
**Rock and Roll Hall of Fame Inductee (2018)** : Bon Jovi was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2018, an induction described as the most controversial of that year's nominees. The band had outgrossed all other inductees, possibly even combined when live tour grosses were considered, and is beloved by rock fans worldwide. "This year's Class of Inductees includes such luminaries as Sister Rosetta Tharp, The Moody Blues, Nina Simone, Dyer Straits, and The Cars. But one act on the roster, the most controversial of this year's nominees, Let Alone Inductees, has outgrosed all of these other acts."
**Enduring Chart Dominance**: Bon Jovi has been "running roughshod" over the Billboard charts for most of the last 32 years since 1986. Even when changing tastes in the 1990s threatened to leave them behind, the band proved "resilient, irrepressible, [and] unkillable." "Bon Jovi have been running roughshod over the billboard charts for most of the last 32 years."
**Musical Influence and Innovation**: The band holds a unique place in the history of hard rock, heavy metal, and the pop charts, and is musically influential in ways that might not even be fully appreciated by their own fans. They were considered innovative in the mid-1980s. "Because Bon Jovi are influential, they hold a unique place in the history of hard rock, heavy metal, and the pop charts."
**Band Name Origin**: The band's name, Bon Jovi, was recommended by manager Don McGee, following a "Van Halen style" tradition of naming the group after its leader, John Bon Jovi, using his Italian last name. "Band manager Don McGee recommended naming the new group Van Halen style after its leader, whose Italian last name Bonjovie became the band name Bonjovie."
**Pre-Band "All-Star Review"**: Before forming the official band, John Bon Jovi recorded the song "Runaway" in 1982 with a completely different lineup of musicians. This unnamed crew was referred to by John as the "All-Star Review." "John Bonjovie first recorded it in 1982 with a completely different lineup of musicians, a band that didn't have an official name."
**Early Music Industry Exposure**: In the early 1980s, a young John Bon Jovi gained his first exposure to rock stars and recording studios through his cousin, Tony Bon Jovi, who was the co-owner of New York City's Power Station recording studio. John would sweep studio floors and occasionally sit in on tracking sessions for various artists, including members of Bruce Springsteen's E-Street band. "In the early 80s, Young John got his first exposure to both rock stars and the recording studio, courtesy of his cousin, Tony Bonjovie, co-owner of New York City's Power Station recording studio."
**Breakthrough with "Coattails"**: Unlike Van Halen's 1984 hit "Jump," Bon Jovi's breakthrough in 1986-1987 had significant "coattails," meaning their success opened up Top 40 radio playlists to other hard rockers with similar aesthetics, such as "piles of moose hair, double-necked guitars and spandex." "This was the key difference. Unlike Van Halen's 84 hit Jump, what distinguished Bon Jovi's 86-87 breakthrough was it had coattails."
**Quick Chorus Strategy**: Bon Jovi effectively employed the strategy of getting to the chorus quickly in their breakthrough hit, "You Give Love a Bad Name," a technique often associated with hit songs in the Spotify era decades later. "Let's give Bon Jovi their due. They figured this out three decades ago, and their breakthrough hit... didn't waste a second of your time waiting for the hook."

## albums
**Van Halen I (1978)** : The band's self-titled debut album, commonly referred to as "Van Halen I," which generated a Top 40 hit relatively quickly. On this album, lead guitarist Eddie Van Halen essentially invented an entire genre of post-blues, hammer-on, fleet-fingered, pyrotechnic metal. The album debuted on the Billboard album chart in March 1978, the same week its lead-off single, "You Really Got Me," broke into the Top 40. "An entire genre of post-Bloose, hammer on, fleet-fingered, pyrotechnic metal was essentially invented by Eddie Van Halen on the band's self-titled debut album, now commonly referred to as Van Halen I."
**1984 (1984)** : Also styled as "MCMLXXXIV" in Roman numerals on its cover, this album featured the breakthrough lead single "Jump." The decision to open the album with a song emphasizing synthesizers over guitar was a savvy move at a time when both MTV and Top 40 radio were at their peak synthesizer usage. "That year, by the way, would be the title of an album that would take Metal the last mile on the charts, giving it a number one single."

## songs
**You Really Got Me (1978)** : The lead-off single from Van Halen's debut album, a remake of The Kinks' 1964 number seven hit. It appeared on the Hot 100 about a month and a half before the album's release and broke into the Top 40 in March 1978. Despite showcasing vocalist David Lee Roth, it peaked at number 36 and was in and out of the Top 40 in just three weeks, hitting the same chart ceiling as other hard rock acts in the 1970s. "Its lead-off single was already on the Hot 100 about a month and a half before the album and it broke into the top 40 in March of 1978, the same week the eponymous Van Halen album debuted on the Billboard album chart."
**Jump (1984)** : The lead single from the album "1984," featuring a "monster synthesizer hook" that marked Van Halen's first time cracking the US Top 10 and served as a major breakthrough. It took just two weeks to enter the Top 40 and only seven weeks to reach number one, where it spent five weeks. By emphasizing synths over guitar, it was a "savvy move" that established hair metal as "hard rock for everybody," though its commercial success had "no coattails" as none of its three follow-up singles cracked the pop Top 10, and no other metal single followed it into the Top 10 for the rest of 1984. "Opening with a monster synthesizer hook, Jump was the lead single from Van Halen's 1984 album, 1984, or MCMLXXXIV, as it was styled in Roman numerals on the album's cover."

## curiosities
**Band Naming Convention**: The band was named after its leader, Eddie Van Halen, and his drummer brother, Alex Van Halen, a naming style later emulated by Bon Jovi. "Like the future band from New Jersey, this California Forson was named after its leader Eddie Van Halen and his drummer brother Alex Van Halen."
**Eddie Van Halen's Groundbreaking Guitar Work**: Eddie Van Halen, the band's lead guitarist, was one of rock history's most groundbreaking musicians. He essentially invented an entire genre of "post-blues, hammer on, fleet-fingered, pyrotechnic metal" on the band's self-titled debut album, "Van Halen I." "An entire genre of post-Bloose, hammer on, fleet-fingered, pyrotechnic metal was essentially invented by Eddie Van Halen on the band's self-titled debut album, now commonly referred to as Van Halen I."
**Contribution to Michael Jackson's "Beat It"**: In late 1982, Eddie Van Halen contributed a "searing guitar solo" to Michael Jackson's R&B rock hybrid "Beat It." This song reached number one on both the Hot 100 and Hot Black singles charts in the spring of 1983, effectively "primed pop radio" for Van Halen to score a chart-topper of their own. "In late 1982, Eddie contributed a searing guitar solo to Michael Jackson's R&B rock hybrid, Beated, a song that reached number one on both the Hot 100 and Hot Black singles charts in the spring of 1983."

# genre - Heavy Metal

## curiosities
**Defining Characteristics**: The boundaries of the term "metal" are "more zealously policed than almost any genre in popular music." Artists once considered essential can be downgraded to "Hard Rock," "Arena Rock," or "Glam," and songs once seen as "almost satanic" might now be played on classic rock or even adult contemporary radio. The goal posts for what constitutes metal are "ever shifting." "The term metal and its boundaries are more zealously policed than almost any genre in popular music."
**Emergence of Subgenres**: Over 30 years, various subgenres of metal have emerged, including death metal, speed metal, black metal, and grind core, further complicating the definition of "actual metal." "Especially after 30 years of such subgenres as death metal, speed metal, black metal and grind core."
**Softening for Pop Charts**: The 1980s saw a trend of softening "so-called metal songs" to make them more accessible for pop charts while still being considered metal. "what we are chronicling in this episode about Bon Jovi and their 80s peers was how much a so-called metal song could be softened and still be considered metal."
**Limited 70s Chart Success**: In the 1970s, it's debatable whether any truly "metal" song ever topped Billboard's Hot 100. Generally, to reach the top of the chart, hard rock had to be mixed with "small dollops of pop, Buggy Rock and White Funk." Leading avatars like Black Sabbath and Led Zeppelin struggled to reach the Top 40, let alone number one. "But in the unlikely event that you listener are a metalhead, first of all welcome to Hit Parade, and second I acknowledge you might not consider much of the music we discuss in this episode to be actual metal."
**Emergence as a Pop Fixture (1984)** : By 1984, metal was officially "emerging as a fixture on both top 40 playlists and MTV's airwaves," indicating a growing mainstream acceptance. "Metal was now officially emerging as a fixture on both top 40 playlists and MTV's airwaves heading into 1984."
