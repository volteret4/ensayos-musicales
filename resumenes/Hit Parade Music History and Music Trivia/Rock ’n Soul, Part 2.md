## albums
**Voices (1980)** : This album marked a turning point for Hall and Oates at the start of the 1980s, where they finally honed their distinctive sound after experimenting with various genres in the 1970s. The LP featured "Kiss on My List" and "Every Time You Go Away," though the latter was not released as a single from this album. "on an album called Appropriately Voices."
**Private Eyes (1981)** : Released before the summer of 1981 was over, this album was packed with more new wave-lining blue-eyed soul, co-written with Sarah and Janna Allen, which established them as chart regulars. Its launch coincided with the near simultaneous launch of MTV, making it their first hit of the MTV era. The album's title track, "Private Eyes," rose to number one faster than "Kiss on My List." "The new LP was called Private Eyes, and thanks to the near simultaneous launch of the music video channel, MTV, Hall and Oats found themselves even more on trend."
**H2O (1982)** : This album, released in late 1982, was titled as a pun on their names and featured an irresistible baseline for its first single, "Maneater." It powered to double platinum status and generated multiple hits, including "Maneater," "One on One," and "Family Man." "In a pun on their names, Hall and Oats's late 1982 album would be called H2O."
**Rock and Soul Part One (1983) - Comprehensive Compilation** : This ambitiously titled album, released Christmas 1983, was the first comprehensive career-spanning compilation for Darryl Hall and John Oates. It included two new hit-bound tracks: "Adult Education" and "Say It Isn't So." Despite its title, a "Part Two" was never released in the subsequent four decades. "Arriving in record stores, that same Christmas was the first comprehensive career-spanning Darryl Hall and John Oats compilation album. The ambitiously titled Rock and Soul Part One."
**Big Bam Boom (1984)** : This synth-heavy LP, released in 1984, was shaped with the help of Arthur Baker, credited as a "Mix Consultant." Its lead single, "Out of Touch," became Hall and Oates's last number one hit and led the album to reach the top five and go platinum out of the box. "Baker helped shape the sound of Big Bam Boom, the duo's synth heavy 1984 LP."
**Live at the Apollo (1985)** : Recorded during a prestigious gig at New York's legendary Apollo Theatre in May 1985, this live LP captured Hall and Oates performing with special guests David Ruffin and Eddie Kendricks of The Temptations. This performance was reprised at Live Aid in July of the same year. "not only a Darryl Hall and John Oats live at the Apollo LP."
**Ooh Yeah! (1988)** : This comeback album was their first on Arista Records after signing a top-dollar deal. Its lead single, "Everything Your Heart Desires," reached number 3 on the Hot 100, initially suggesting a strong return. However, subsequent singles performed less well, and the album peaked at number 24. "Accordingly, the first Hall and Oates single on Arista sounded very much like the adult contemporary soft pop that was the label's specialty. Everything your heart desires, the lead single from the comeback album, U Yeah, reached number 3 on the Hot 100, suggesting that Hall and Oates were back like they'd never left."
**Change of Season (1990)** : For this second Arista album, Hall and Oates collaborated with rock frontman John Bon Jovi in a bid to stay relevant. Its lead single, "So Close," peaked at number 11, and the album reached number 60, making it their first studio album to miss the Top 40 since 1974. "The written with Bon Jovi so close, the lead single from 1990's Change of Season album was just that."
**Marigold Sky (1997)** : This album marked Hall and Oates' return after a long hiatus in the 1990s. It was characterized by mellow soul pop and inaugurated their "autumnal phase" as adult contemporary hitmakers, with modest expectations. "Marigold Sky, the album and the expectations for it were much more modest. It was mellow soul pop and it inaugurated the duo's autumnal phase as adult contemporary hitmakers."

## songs
**Kiss on My List (1981)** : This song, co-written by Darryl Hall with Janna Allen (Sarah Allen's sister), featured Janna's infectious chorus. Despite being perceived as purely romantic, Darryl Hall claimed it was an anti-love song about someone sheepish about sharing feelings. Originally recorded as a demo for Janna, Tommy Metola insisted Hall keep it, with Hall and Oates adding backing vocals and instruments. Released as a single in January 1981, it took twelve weeks to reach number one and stayed there for three weeks. "Released as a single in January of 1981, Kiss on My List took a dozen weeks to reach number one, where it stayed for three weeks."
**You Make My Dreams (1981)** : Despite never reaching number one, this song is one of the most popular in the Hall and Oates catalog, spun over 34,000 times on US terrestrial radio and streamed over 102 million times in 2021. It's their biggest Spotify hit ever. In summer 1981, following "Kiss on My List," it peaked at number five, stuck behind other hits. The song's use in a fantasy sequence in the 2009 film *500 Days of Summer* contributed to its digital era resurgence. "You make my dreams, despite never reaching number one, is one of the most popular songs in the Darryl Hall and John Oats catalog."
**Private Eyes (1981)** : The title track of their 1981 LP, this song was their first hit of the MTV era. The music video, encouraged by Tommy Matulla, was simple, featuring Hall, Oates, and the band dressed as "old-timey gum shoes." It quickly rose to number one in less time than "Kiss on My List" had. "Private Eyes, their first hit of the MTV era, and the title track of their 1981 LP, Hall and Oats used this visual simplicity to their advantage."
**I Can't Go for That (No Can Do) (1982)** : This critically acclaimed and influential single emerged from Darryl Hall's experimentation with a Roland Compurism drum machine and keyboard in the studio, with John Oates adding a simple guitar riff. Co-written with Sarah Allen, it was effortlessly funky and genre-defying, evoking hip hop. In January 1982, it topped both the Hot 100 and Hot Soul singles charts, making Hall and Oates the first white act to top both charts simultaneously. The song's airtight baseline was widely imitated, notably influencing Michael Jackson's "Billie Jean." "I can't go for that. Parentheses No Can Do. A title Darryl Hall and co-writer Sarah Allen came up with was effortlessly funky."
**Did It in a Minute (1982)** : This new wave track was the third Top 10 single pulled from the *Private Eyes* LP. Co-written by Janna Allen, it reached number nine. "The new wavy did it in a minute. Co-written by Jana Allen reached number nine."
**Your Imagination (1982)** : This percolating song was the fourth single from the *Private Eyes* album, cracking the Top 40 at number 33. "And a fourth single, the percolating your imagination cracked the top 40 at number 33."
**Maneater (1982)** : This song is Hall and Oates's top airplay track, spun over 64,000 times on radio in the year prior to the transcript, nearly double the rate of "You Make My Dreams." Co-written with Sarah Allen, the duo later claimed it was a metaphor for a rough city, but it was widely regarded as a classic song about a "gold-digging woman." It became Darryl Hall and John Oates's biggest pop hit, spending a full month atop the Hot 100 and powering the *H2O* album to double platinum status. "According to MRC data, its Hall and Oats's top airplay track spun over 64,000 times on the radio just last year."
**One on One (1983)** : A ballad from the *H2O* album, this song was a hit in both pop and R&B charts, reaching number 7 on pop and number 8 on R&B. "including the number 7 pop, number 8 R&B Ballad, one on one."
**Family Man (1983)** : This crunching synth rocker is a cover of a minor 1982 hit by British multi-instrumentalist Mike Oldfield. The Hall and Oates version topped out at number 6 in the summer of 1983. "And the crunching synth rocker Family Man, a cover of a minor 1982 hit by British multi-instrumentalist Mike Oldfield."
**Jingle Bell Rock (1983)** : By the end of 1983, Hall and Oates were such bankable stars that they recorded a remake of Bobby Helms' Christmas chestnut. Complete with a kitschy 50s-style video, it became an immediate MTV staple and still gets spun nearly 25,000 times on US Radio each holiday season. "Their remake of Bobby Helms' Chestnut, Jingle Bell Rock, complete with a kitschy 50 style video, became an immediate MTV staple."
**Say It Isn't So (1983)** : This supple pop and R&B track served as the first single from their compilation album, *Rock and Soul Part 1*. It rose to number two for three weeks over the 1983-84 holiday season. Its success led Casey Kasem on *American Top 40* to declare Hall and Oates the "most successful duo of the rock era," surpassing The Everly Brothers. "And serving as the set's first single, a supple pop and B track called Say It Isn't So."
**Adult Education (1984)** : This biting synth funk number was one of two new tracks recorded for *Rock and Soul Part 1*. Nile Rogers later remixed it to be more club-friendly, and his "clattery remix" pushed the song to number 8 on the Hot 100. "a biting synth funk number called Adult Education."
**Out of Touch (1984)** : This track, their last number one hit, was the lead single from the *Big Bam Boom* LP. Like previous peak Hall and Oates singles, it blended mechanized rock, R&B, and club music with a "Big Booming production sound." Arthur Baker's remix, which deconstructed the track and made the beat louder, was preferred for the music video and many urban radio stations. By December 1984, it was atop the Hot 100. "Out of touch, like prior peak Hall and Oats singles, was several things at once, mechanized rock, crossed with R&B and club music, and a Big Booming production sound."
**Method of Modern Love (1985)** : A single from the *Big Bam Boom* album, this song reached number 5 on the charts. It features an interpolation of its "METHOD" refrain in Method Man's 1993 song "Method Man." "The number five hit, Method of Modern Love."
**Some Things Are Better Left Unsaid (1985)** : This song from the *Big Bam Boom* album reached number 18. "The Atmosphere some things are better left unsaid, a number 18 hit."
**Possession Obsession (1985)** : A rare single from *Big Bam Boom* featuring a John Oates lead vocal, this song was remixed by Arthur Baker and reached number 30. "and a rare single with a John Oats lead vocal, Possession Obsession, which was also remixed by Arthur Baker and reached number 30."
**The Way You Do The Things You Do / My Girl (Live, 1985)** : This medley of Motown classics by The Temptations was performed by Hall and Oates with special guests David Ruffin and Eddie Kendricks at the Apollo Theatre and Live Aid. A single of this live mashup of oldies reached number 20 a couple of months after Live Aid. "The ForSums medley of the way you do the things you do and my girl reached number 20, impressive for a live mashup of oldies."
**Everything Your Heart Desires (1988)** : The lead single from their comeback album *Ooh Yeah!*, this track reached number 3 on the Hot 100, suggesting a strong return to form for the duo on their new label, Arista. "Everything your heart desires, the lead single from the comeback album, U Yeah, reached number 3 on the Hot 100, suggesting that Hall and Oates were back like they'd never left."
**Downtown Life (1988)** : This single from the *Ooh Yeah!* album did not perform as well as the lead single, reaching number 31 on the charts, indicating a short-lived comeback for the duo. "Subsequent singles fell short like the number 31 hit Downtown Life."
**So Close (1990)** : The lead single from their 1990 album *Change of Season*, co-written with John Bon Jovi, peaked at number 11 in December of that year, coming "so close" to the Top 10 but not quite making it. "The written with Bon Jovi so close, the lead single from 1990's Change of Season album was just that."
**Don't Hold Back Your Love (1991)** : This follow-up single from *Change of Season* proved to be a near miss for Hall and Oates, peaking at a "heartbreaking" number 41 in 1991, signaling the end of their era as a Top 40 pop act. "When Hall and Oates follow up single, don't hold back your love, also proved a near miss, peaking at a heartbreaking number 41 in 1991."
**Promise Ain't Enough (1997)** : This song from the *Marigold Sky* album helped Hall and Oates get back on radio, where it peaked at a respectable number six on the Adult Contemporary chart. "Promise ain't enough from Marigold Sky got them back on the radio, where it peaked at a respectable number six on the AC chart."
**Do It for Love (2002)** : This song performed even better than "Promise Ain't Enough," topping the Adult Contemporary chart in 2002. "In 2002, they did even better with Do It for Love, which topped that chart."

## curiosities
**Philadelphia Roots and Early Sound Searching** : Darryl Hall and John Oates met in the Philly Soul scene of the 1960s. They spent the 1970s trying to find their distinctive sound, veering through folk, R&B, rock, and disco, experiencing both major chart highs and humbling lows. "On our last episode, we talked about how Darryl Hall and John Oates met in the Philly Soul scene of the 1960s. Then spent the 70s trying to find their sound, veering from folk to R&B to rock to disco, with major chart highs and humbling lows."
**"Kiss on My List" Title Misconception** : Many listeners mistook the title of "Kiss on My List" for "Lips on My List." Darryl Hall clarified that the word was "Kiss" and that he conceived the song as an anti-love song, sung by someone sheepish about sharing feelings, despite its reception as a breezy, easy pean to infatuation. "And about that chorus, the word in the title is Kiss on My List, not lips. Though many listeners mistook it for lips, the song was perceived as purely romantic, whereas Darryl Hall claimed it was an anti-love song, sung by a dude who's sheepish about sharing his feelings."
**MTV Era and Music Videos** : Darryl Hall and John Oates were making music videos as far back as the 70s, encouraged by Tommy Matulla, even though they didn't much like making them. These early clips were cheap, low-concept affairs, often shot on videotape on a sound stage, and Hall didn't always get the lip-syncing right. With the launch of MTV and their *Private Eyes* album, they used this visual simplicity to their advantage and were considered "the first true MTV stars." "Darryl and John didn't much like making music videos, but as far back as the 70s, encouraged by Tommy Matulla, they were making them."
**"I Can't Go for That" R&B Chart Breakthrough** : Prior to "I Can't Go for That," Hall and Oates had made Billboard's R&B chart a half-dozen times since "Sara Smile" but had never cracked the Top 20. It was remarkable when, on January 30, 1982, they topped both the Hot 100 and Hot Soul singles charts with this song, becoming the first white act to top the R&B chart since KC and the Sunshine Band in 1977, and the first white act ever to top both charts in the same week. "That's what made it remarkable when in the Billboard issue dated January 30, 1982, Darryl Hall and John Oats were on top of both the Hot 100 and Hot Soul singles with their skeletal synthesized funk bomb."
**Michael Jackson's "Billy Jean" Influence** : During a break at the "We Are the World" recording session, Michael Jackson sheepishly confessed to Darryl Hall that he "stole Billy Jean from you," acknowledging the influence of "I Can't Go for That (No Can Do)'s" baseline. Hall reassured him, "it's all right man, it's something we all do." While not identical, a mashup by DJ Peace Cat illustrates the strong similarities in their baselines. "Years later, at the recording session for We Are the World, Michael Jackson approached Darryl Hall during a break in recording and sheepishly told him, I hope you don't mind but I stole Billy Jean from you."
**"Maneater" Baseline Origins** : For "Maneater," Hall and Oates "stole from the best" by incorporating a replica of the signature baseline played by Motown legend James Jamerson of The Funk Brothers on The Supremes' 1966 hit "You Can't Hurry Love." This baseline was particularly popular in 1982, also appearing in hits by The Jam and Phil Collins' remake of "You Can't Hurry Love." "On the Supreme's 1966 number one classic, you can't hurry love, the signature element is its baseline, played by Motown legend James Jamerson of the Funk Brothers. That baseline has been ripped off a lot."
**Most Successful Duo of the Rock Era** : When "Say It Isn't So" rose to number two for three weeks in 1983-84, Casey Kasem on *American Top 40* announced that Hall and Oates had surpassed The Everly Brothers as the "most successful duo of the rock era" since the late 1950s. They still hold this title today. "And with their current hit, this new two-sum surpass the Everlies. Say it isn't so. Here are Darryl Hall and John Oats, the new Top Duo of the Rock Era."
**Darryl Hall's Solo Work and Collaborations (1984)** : In 1984, Darryl Hall "spread his talents promiscuously." He sang backup on hits for INXS and post-punk icon Elvis Costello. He also co-wrote the title track "Swept Away" for Diana Ross's 1984 album, which became a number 18 pop and number 3 R&B hit, and he co-produced the song, sang backup, and played guitar on it. "Darryl Hall, in particular, spread his talents promiscuously throughout the year. He sang backup on hits for Australian band in excess. And in a rare pop crossover for post-punk icon Elvis Costello."
**We Are The World (1985)** : Both Darryl Hall and John Oates participated in USA for Africa's "We Are the World." Darryl Hall had a brief but potent solo vocal in the chorus. "Not only did they both sing in the chorus of USA for Africa's We Are the World, Darryl got a brief but potent solo vocal."
**Sun City (1985)** : Hall and Oates were among the very few "We Are the World" participants to also contribute to Little Steven Van Zant's anti-apartheid anthem "Sun City." John Oates was showcased in the video singing alongside Lou Reed and Ruben Blades, and Darryl Hall again had a brief solo turn. "Then later in the year, when Little Stephen Van Zant, a masked and eclectic group of rock, rap, soul, jazz, blues, and Latin musicians for his anti-apartheid anthem, Sun City, Hall and Oats were one of the very few We Are the World participants to do that charity mega single too."
**Live Aid Performance (1985) - Hometown Heroes** : At the July 1985 Live Aid concert in Philadelphia (the former John F. Kennedy Stadium), Hall and Oates were one of the biggest acts on the bill. Though they lived in New York City, the Philly crowd received them as "beloved, prodigal sons." They were placed on the bill just an hour before the end of the 14-hour marathon, performing after only Mick Jagger with Tina Turner, Bob Dylan/Keith Richards/Ron Wood, and the "We Are the World" singalong. They arguably were the last billed performers with current material on the charts. "The two sites where Live Aid happened were Wembley Stadium in London, and the former John F. Kennedy Stadium in Philadelphia. That meant that for Holland Oats, the American edition of Live Aid was essentially a hometown gig, even though they both lived in New York City for years."
**Live Aid Performance (1985) - Most Hit-Packed Set** : Hall and Oates performed the most hit-packed set of anyone at Live Aid that day, including their own number one hits like "Out of Touch" and "Maneater." They also performed a medley of Motown classics by The Temptations with special guests David Ruffin and Eddie Kendricks, which included The Temptations' 1965 chart-topper "My Girl" (a song a young Darryl Hall idolized). No other performer at Live Aid, in London or Philadelphia, played a set with bigger Hot 100 hits. "Their set was the most hit pack of anyone's that day. If you count not only their own number one hits out of touch and man-eater, but also the songs included in a medley of Motown Classics by The Temptations performed by the duo with special guests David Ruffin and Eddie Kendrick's of The Temptations."
**Paul Young's Cover of "Every Time You Go Away" (1985)** : Paul Young's cover of Darryl Hall's ballad "Every Time You Go Away," originally from the 1980 Hall and Oates LP *Voices* (but never issued as a single by them), became a smash hit on both sides of the Atlantic, reaching number four in the UK and number one in the US. On the day of Live Aid, July 13, 1985, Young's cover was in the Hot 100's top 10 and reached number one two weeks later. Darryl Hall was "a little salty" about his song becoming better known by someone else. "Paul Young turned every time you go away into a smash. On both sides of the Atlantic, number four in the UK and number one in the US."
**Apollo Theatre Performance with The Temptations (1985)** : In May 1985, Hall and Oates performed a prestigious gig at New York's legendary Apollo Theatre, where they first performed with David Ruffin and Eddie Kendricks of The Temptations. This performance was reprised at Live Aid. The duo capitalized on their clout by issuing a live LP and a single with Ruffin and Kendricks, bringing the two Temptations back to the Top 40 for the first time in a decade. "This Apollo gig, by the way, was where Hall and Oats first performed with the temptations David Ruffin and Eddie Kendrick's."
**Burnout and Hiatus (Late 1980s)** : After an incredible five-year run, the duo was burned out. Darryl Hall spoke in interviews about the need to take a break, leading to a hiatus from John Oates. "The duo were burned out after their incredible five-year run. And in interviews, Darryl Hall spoke about the need to take a break."
**Darryl Hall Solo Album (1986)** : In 1986, Darryl Hall released his first solo album since the "beleaguered" *Sacred Songs*, titled *Three Hearts in the Happy Ending Machine*. It spawned an immediate Top 5 hit with "Dreamtime," co-produced by Dave Stewart. The album was well-reviewed and produced other hits, but peaked at number 27 on the album chart, lower than *Hall and Oates Live at the Apollo*, and failed to go gold. "In 1986, Darryl Hall issued his first solo album since the beleaguered sacred songs. It was called Three Hearts in the Happy Ending Machine. And it spawned an immediate top five hit with the abulliant psychedelic rocker Dream Time, co-produced by the Urivmix Dave Stewart."
**John Oates Songwriting Hit for Icehouse (1987)** : During the duo's hiatus, John Oates co-wrote "Electric Blue" with Icehouse singer Iva Davies. Oates admitted the catchy synth-pop track "could easily have been a Hall and Oates" song. Released as an Icehouse single in 1987, it reached number 7 on the Hot 100 by May 1988. "Oates teamed with Ice House singer Iva Davies to write Electric Blue, a catchy synth pop track which he admitted could easily have been a Hall and Oates."
**Decline in Chart Performance (Early 1990s)** : After a strong comeback with "Everything Your Heart Desires," subsequent singles like "Downtown Life" (number 31) from *Ooh Yeah!* fell short. Their 1990 album *Change of Season* peaked at number 60, and its follow-up single "Don't Hold Back Your Love" peaked at number 41 in 1991, marking the end of their days as a Top 40 pop act. "But it proved a short lived comeback. Subsequent singles fell short like the number 31 hit Downtown Life."
**Influence on Hip Hop (Late 1980s - 1990s)** : At the same time Hall and Oates were falling off as pop stars, they were emerging as a formative influence in hip hop. De La Soul's 1989 single "Say No Go" (from *3 Feet High and Rising*) was built from samples of "I Can't Go for That." This song also appeared in singles by The Two Live Crew and Heavy D. "I Can't Go for That" was their most sampled recording. "Because at the very moment they were falling off as pop stars, Hall and Oates were emerging as a formative influence in hip hop."
**Wu-Tang Clan Sampling (1993)** : On the Wu-Tang Clan's 1993 debut *Enter the Wu Tang 36 Chambers*, rapper Method Man's title song "Method Man" is built on an interpolation of the "METHOD" refrain from Hall and Oates' 1985 hit "Method of Modern Love." "On Enter the Wu Tang 36 Chambers, the 1993 debut by the Wu Tang Clan, the title song by rapper Method Man is built on an interpolation of the METHOD refrain from Hall and Oates 1985 hit Method of Modern Love."
**B-Legit Collaboration (1996)** : In 1996, Bay Area rapper B-Legit not only built his single "Getto Smile" out of Hall and Oates' hit "Sara Smile," but he also got Darryl Hall himself to sing on the track. "And in 1996, Bay Area rapper B Legit not only built his single Getto Smile out of Hall and Oates's hit Sarah Smile, he got Darryl Hall himself to sing on."
**Long Hiatus (1990s)** : Darryl Hall and John Oates took a much longer hiatus for most of the 1990s after their chart performance declined. "Darryl Hall and John Oates would never return to the upper reaches of the Hot 100, and they took a much longer hiatus for most of the 1990's."
**"Live from Darryl's House" Web Series (2007)** : Just before the 2007 holiday season, Darryl Hall started a web series of live, largely acoustic recordings from his upstate New York home, called "Live from Darryl's House." Shot with multiple cameras and equipment, it maintained a low-tech, homespun quality. John Oates was the first guest, and later, younger performers like Patrick Stump (Fall Out Boy), Chromeo, and Gym Class Heroes clamored to play. "Just before the holiday season of 2007, pretty much on a whim, Darryl Hall started a web series of live, largely acoustic recordings from his home in upstate New York. He called it simply live from Darryl's house."
**Hollywood Appearances (2006)** : Hall and Oates showed a sense of humor by guesting on a 2006 episode of *Will & Grace*. "Hollywood also started reconnecting with Hall and Oates. They showed they had a sense of humor by guesting on a 2006 episode of Will and Grace."
**"500 Days of Summer" and "You Make My Dreams" (2009)** : The acclaimed 2009 film *500 Days of Summer*, starring Joseph Gordon-Levitt and Zooey Deschanel, featured Hall and Oates' "You Make My Dreams" in a joyous, choreographed dance sequence. This scene caught a wave of flash mob dance sequences on YouTube and helped turn "You Make My Dreams" into Hall and Oates' digital era blockbuster, making it their top Spotify song and second biggest radio recurrent after "Maneater." "The 500 Days of Summer scene caught a wave of flash mob dance sequences that were blanketing YouTube in the Oates. And the film was popular enough to turn You Make My Dreams into Hall and Oates' digital era blockbuster."
**Jimmy Wayne Country Cover (2009)** : In late 2009, country singer Jimmy Wayne recorded a cover of "Sara Smile" that gave Darryl Hall and John Oates their first songwriting credits on Billboard's Hot Country Songs Chart. Wayne's cover reached number 31 on the Country Chart. "In late 2009, country singer Jimmy Wayne recorded a cover of Sarah Smile that got Daryl Hall and John Oates' songwriting credits on Billboard's Hot Country Songs Chart for the first time."
**Rock and Roll Hall of Fame Induction (2014)** : Eligible since 1997, Hall and Oates were not nominated until the 2010s. Roots drummer and historian Questlove (Amir Questlove Thompson), a member of the Rock Hall Nominating Committee, passionately advocated for their candidacy. They were inducted on their very first ballot appearance in 2014, with Questlove doing the honors. "As far back as 1997, Daryl Hall and John Oates had been eligible for the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame. But they were not so much as nominated until the 2010s."
**Questlove's Induction Speech and Legacy** : During their Rock and Roll Hall of Fame induction in 2014, Questlove highlighted the duo's importance to the black community and their ability to cross boundaries. He noted their collaboration with David Ruffin and Eddie Kendricks in 1985, which sparked a revival of interest in The Temptations and classic Motown, and how their music became prized among hip-hop artists like Wu-Tang Clan, J Dilla, and De La Soul. "Questlove's testimonial was one more reminder that Darryl Hall and John Oates' music crossed the spectrum and defied category. Even as rock fans and Rolling Stone magazine were ignoring them, fans of Soul knew that they were important."
**Call for More Philadelphia Artists in Rock Hall** : In their Rock and Roll Hall of Fame acceptance speech, John Oates expressed gratitude for their Philadelphia origins, while Darryl Hall criticized the institution for overlooking other Philly-based acts like The Stylistics, The Delfonics, Harold Melvin & The Blue Notes, Lenny Barry, and Chubby Checker. "And Darryl Hall took the institution to task, not for waiting so long to induct them, but for ignoring other Philly-based acts. What happened to the stylistics and the delphinics? How melvin in the blue notes? Lenny Barry, Shelby Checker, the better to be more Philadelphia artists in this place."
**Comfortable Afterlife as Touring Musicians** : With nothing left to prove, Darryl Hall and John Oates enjoy a comfortable afterlife as touring musicians. Their latest tour in 2021 ranked among the year's top 50 grossers, even amidst concert challenges, and they continue to play their number five hit (which "really should have been one of their half-dozen hot 100 number one hits") for younger audiences. "Now, with nothing left to prove, Darryl Hall and John Oates enjoy a comfortable afterlife as touring musicians. Their latest tour ranked among the year's top 50 grocers, even in 2021, a minefields of a year for concerts."

# instrument - Drum Machine

## curiosities
**Pop Experimentation in Early 1980s** : The period of 1981-1982 was a "good time for pop artists experimenting with primitive drum machines," with classic pop tracks emerging from inquisitive rock stars using gizmos from companies like Roland, Korg, and Linn. Darryl Hall's tinkering with the Roland Compurism produced "I Can't Go for That," which rivaled Phil Collins' "In the Air Tonight" for sonic influence, marking a significant technological shift in pop music production. "This, by the way, was a good time for pop artists experimenting with primitive drum machines."

# instrument - Roland Compurism

## curiosities
**Darryl Hall's Experimental Use (1982)** : One night in the studio, after most of the band had left, Darryl Hall was "fiddling with a primitive drum machine called the Roland Compurism." Liking its dry, brittle beat, he layered keyboards and had John Oates add a skeletal guitar riff, resulting in "I Can't Go for That (No Can Do)." This experimentation produced one of the biggest and most influential experimental tracks of the era. "One night in the studio, when most of their band had gone home, Darryl Hall was fiddling with a primitive drum machine called the Roland Compurism."

**Influence of Motown Baseline (1982)** : The signature baseline played by Motown legend James Jamerson of The Funk Brothers on The Supremes' 1966 hit "You Can't Hurry Love" was frequently "ripped off" and particularly popular in 1982. Replicas of this baseline appeared in hits by The Jam ("Town Called Malice"), Phil Collins (remake of "You Can't Hurry Love"), and Hall and Oates ("Maneater"), illustrating a pervasive musical influence. "That baseline has been ripped off a lot. And it was especially popular in 1982."
