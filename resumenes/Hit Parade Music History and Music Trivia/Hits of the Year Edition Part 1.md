# curiosity

**Billboard's Hot 100 Foundation (1958)** : The Hot 100 chart was founded in 1958 and has always used a "bespoke formula" to rank songs. This formula initially averaged record sales and radio airplay, and for the last decade plus, it has also incorporated streamed tracks. "Since its founding in 1958, the Hot 100 has always had a bespoke formula, averaging record sales and radio airplay."
**Intricate Year-End Hot 100 Formula** : The formula for the year-end Hot 100 is intricate enough to create "actual suspense" over the results, meaning chart enthusiasts cannot always predict how the final math will shake out. "That formula is intricate enough that there's some actual suspense over the year-end Hot 100 result."
**Year-End Billboard 200 Album Chart** : This annual survey ranks the best-performing albums of the year. While historically driven by sales, it now combines sales, tracks, and streams, which makes the formula more compelling but results still "fairly predictable," often topped by albums that spent the most weeks at #1. "The year end Billboard 200 album chart is also widely followed."
**Year-End Guideline 1: Chart Longevity Trumps Chart Peak** : This guideline states that a song's overall time on the chart, or its "longevity," is more crucial for year-end Billboard chart placement than its peak position. For example, a song with fewer weeks at #1 but more total weeks on the chart can outrank a song that spent many weeks at #1 but for a shorter overall period. "Year end guideline one. Heart longevity trumps chart peak."
**Year-End Guideline 2: The Last Christmas Factor** : This guideline explains how sales and airplay accumulated in the closing weeks of a calendar year can significantly benefit a song or album's chances on the *following* year's Billboard year-end charts. This is because Billboard's chart year typically runs from early December through late November, so late-year hits get a head start on the next chart cycle. "Year end guideline 2. The Last Christmas Factor."
**Year-End Guideline 3: Murky Analog Chart Methodology** : In the "Analog Charts era," prior to computerization, Billboard's chart methodology was "murky," leading to inconsistencies like the re-ranking of the 1966 year-end #1 song (initially "California Dreamin'," later "Ballad of the Green Berets") and "ghosted hits." "Year end guideline 3. The methodology used to be murky."
**Ghosted Hits in Analog Chart Era** : During the "Analog Charts era," certain songs that peaked near the end of the year were sometimes "left off the year end surveys entirely," even if they topped the Hot 100. Examples include The Beach Boys' "Good Vibrations," The Partridge Family's "I Think I Love You," and Prince and the Revolution's "Purple Rain." "Radio analyst Sean Ross, a recent guest on Hit Parade the Bridge, discussing his lost hits project, has identified dozens of so-called ghosted hits that never got a year end ranking at all."
**Computerization and SoundScan Era (1991)** : Beginning in 1991 with the "Sound Scan era," Billboard's charts became computerized, dramatically improving data integrity. This era ensured scrupulous ranking of all hits and made it common for big hits straddling two calendar years to appear on both year-end rankings. "Fortunately, Billboard's data integrity has improved dramatically over the years, especially since their charts were computerized in the Sound Scan era, beginning in 1991."
**Year-End Guideline 4: Superstardom Doesn't Necessarily Help** : This guideline highlights that achieving superstar status with multiple Hot 100 toppers does not guarantee a year-end Hot 100 number one song. This is often because fans "spread their love around to multiple songs by that hitmaker," preventing any single track from accumulating enough points to top the annual list. "Year end guideline four. Superstardom doesn't necessarily help."
**Year-End Number Ones and Stardom/Longevity** : A Billboard year-end number one song is considered "something of a phenomenon" that doesn't guarantee future success or chart longevity for the artist. It "might lead to piles more hits or none at all," and it can either define the year in music or become a forgotten trivia answer. "In short, whether it comes from Whitney Houston or Sam The Sham, a Billboard year and number one song has to be viewed as Melly Mel might say as something of a phenomenon."
