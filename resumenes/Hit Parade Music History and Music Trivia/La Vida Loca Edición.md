# genre - Latin Music

## curiosities
**Universe of Sounds, Styles, and Rhythms** : Latin music is described not merely as a genre, but as an entire universe of sounds, styles, and rhythms, developed over centuries and across generations by Afro-Caribbean and Iberian descendants. It encompasses a vast array of best-selling styles such as salsa, merengue, bachata, Mexican Ranchera, Bossa Nova, Samba, and Son. "But Latin music is more than a genre. It's an entire universe of sounds, styles and rhythms, developed over centuries and across generations of Afro-Caribbean and Iberian descendants."
**Influence on Mainstream English Pop** : Latin music has significantly shaped mainstream English-language pop through various elements, including the brass instrumentation characteristic of mariachi groups and the irresistible rhythm of the piano clave, which is derived from Latin jazz and salsa. These elements are often taken for granted in popular music. "the sounds of Latin music have also helped shape mainstream English language pop, whether it's the brass of Amariachi group."
**Limited Success on U.S. Flagship Charts** : Historically, the success of Spanish-language music has been quite limited on mainstream U.S. flagship charts like the Hot 100 Songs Chart or the Billboard 200 album chart. Many songs considered legendary within the Latin music world have never even appeared on these charts, highlighting an "overwhelming Anglophone tendency" in U.S. pop. "On hit parade, we tend to focus on all genre charts like the Hot 100 Songs Chart, or the Billboard 200 album chart. And, on these surveys, the success of Spanish language music has been far more limited."
**Latin Boomita, Version 1.0 (1999-2000)** : This period, kicked off by Ricky Martin's success in 1999, saw a surge of Latin stars on the American charts. By 2000, however, it began to show strain as nearly all the hits, despite their Latin rhythms and instrumentation, were in English and often produced by non-Latin collaborators aiming for Anglophone crossover. Critics and many longtime fans regarded this wave as "inauthentic and watered down." "Ricky kicked off what would come to be known as Latin Bumita, version 1.0 on the American charts."

"But as late as the mid-90s, Spanish language hits were basically treated as novelties, fun for parties or dancing, but not much more."

**"X Factor" for 70s and 80s Crossover** : In the 1970s and 1980s, for Latin music to infuse the American hit parade, particularly if a song was sung in Spanish, there almost always needed to be an "X factor" or a meta-narrative/backstory to propel the song up the charts and help it cross over to Anglophone audiences. **Increased Bilingualism in Pop (Post-1987)** : Following the chart-topping success of Los Lobos' all-Spanish "La Bamba" in 1987, it became more plausible for mainstream pop records to incorporate lyrics in Spanish or Portuguese.
