# artist - Elton John

## albums
**Madman Across the Water (1971) - Home of Levon and Tiny Dancer** : The LP produced Elton John's lead-off single "Levon," which cracked the American Top 40, and the follow-up hit "Tiny Dancer." Both are heavily orchestrated story songs. Today the follow-up is far better known than the single that originally charted higher. "Another heavily orchestrated story song from that same Elton John LP."

## songs
**Levon (1971) - Lead-Off Single, Number 24 in 1972** : The lead-off single from Madman Across the Water. It is a story song with lyrics by John's partner Bernie Taupin about a fictional character named Levon, built on Elton's piano and plush orchestration. It reached number 24 on the Hot 100 in February 1972, Elton John's first entry into the American Top 40 from this album. It still does well, with over 25 million US Spotify plays, and Elton John fans and classic rock radio listeners likely know it. "Anchored by Elton's piano and bathed in plush orchestration."
**Tiny Dancer (1972) - Legacy Hit That Missed the Top 40** : The follow-up to Levon, also a heavily orchestrated story song from the same LP. It made the Hot 100 in 1972 but peaked at number 41, 17 spots lower than Levon. It missed the American Top 40, was never counted down by Casey Kasem, and was on and off the Hot 100 in just seven weeks. As of the episode it had more than 570 million Spotify plays, about 23 times as many as Levon. At US radio it is consistently one of Elton John's five most-played 70s songs. "If you've been alive in the 21st century, you can probably sing every word to tiny dancer."

## curiosities
**Five Most-Played 70s Songs at US Radio** : At US radio, Tiny Dancer sits among Elton John's five most-played 70s songs, alongside Bennie and the Jets, Your Song, Rocket Man, and Don't Go Breaking My Heart. "tiny dancer is consistently one of Elton John's five most played 70s songs."
**Lyricist Partnership with Bernie Taupin** : Bernie Taupin, John's songwriting partner, wrote the lyrics for Levon. "a majestic story song with lyrics by John's partner, Bernie Topin."

## songs
**I Melt With You (1983) - Number 78 Peak, Massive Legacy** : This British new wave song peaked at number 78 on the Hot 100 in 1983, not even a quarter of the way up the chart. It is now a staple of classic hits radio. According to Luminate, it was played on US terrestrial radio 74,000 times in the last 12 months (2021–22), more airplay than most Christmas carols. In the same period it was streamed nearly 31 million times, more than some recent Taylor Swift songs. It was an early MTV classic. "couldn't even make it one quarter of the way up the Hot 100."

## curiosities
**Theories for the Low 1983 Peak** : Possible reasons for the low peak: the song sounded edgier in 1983 than it does now, perhaps too edgy for pop radio then. Its label, Sire Records, was better known at the time for hip acts like the Ramones and Talking Heads than for scoring pop hits, at least before it started promoting Madonna. The public simply wasn't ready for the song in 1983 and came around to it over four decades. "No, the public just wasn't ready for I Melt With You in 1983."
**The Rocky Horror Picture Show of Hits** : I Melt With You is compared to The Rocky Horror Picture Show. That film was the third-highest-grossing film released in 1975, just below Jaws and One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest, but it made most of that money one midnight show at a time in the years after 1975 and took years to find its audience. "You might say I Melt With You is the Rocky Horror Picture Show of Hits."
**One-Hit Wonder** : Modern English is a one-hit wonder, unlike most of the hitmakers discussed. "Most of the hit makers I'll be discussing, unlike modern English, were not one hit wonders."

## songs
**You Can Call Me Al (1986) - From Number 44 to Number 23** : The first single from Graceland, issued in late summer 1986. It missed the Top 40, peaking at number 44, and soon fell off the chart. "You Can Call Me Al is ridiculously catchy."
**50 Ways to Leave Your Lover (1975) - Only Solo Number One** : Simon's only solo number one, spending three weeks atop the Hot 100 in February 1976. In the last year it had 2,100 radio plays, far fewer than You Can Call Me Al, though it matches that song on streaming. "50 Ways to Leave Your Lover is Paul Simon's only solo number one hit."

**Generational Shift at Oldies Radio** : Al's radio lead is largely generational. Oldies stations now target Generations X and Y rather than baby boomers, and they play more 80s music than 70s music. "Oldies radio stations, which are now targeted at generations X and Y, not baby boomers, nowadays play more 80s music than 70s music."
**Simon & Garfunkel Classics** : Simon's Simon & Garfunkel classics, such as The Sound of Silence, Mrs. Robinson, and Bridge Over Troubled Water, may define him more than his solo hits. For anyone younger than boomer age, his solo legacy hit is arguably You Can Call Me Al. "Paul Simon's solo legacy hit is arguably you can call me Al."

# curiosity

**Definition of a Legacy Hit** : A song whose classic status isn't captured by its original chart performance but which may now be among the first songs an artist is remembered for. Some missed the Top 10, the Top 40, or the Hot 100 entirely. Some went to number one but are bigger now than in their day. The clearest examples are acts with two major singles, where a Top 10 hit is forgotten and a lower-charting one became the song for the ages. "Their classic status isn't captured by their original chart performance."
**Radio as the Truth Serum of Hit Making** : With callout research and ratings data, radio programmers know what keeps listeners from changing the station, so heavy rotation reveals true legacy status. "I call Radio the truth serum of hit making."
**Number One vs. Number Two Is Often an Accident** : Charts are compiled one week at a time, so timing and circumstance decide a lot. The gap between a number one and a number two often comes down to how the data broke that week. "The difference between a number one hit and a number two hit is often an accident."
**Luminate Data Window** : Unless noted, the radio and streaming figures come from Luminate, the provider of the raw numbers behind Billboard's charts, collected between September 1, 2021 and August 31, 2022. "these numbers were collected between September 1st, 2021, and August 31st, 2022."
**Retail-Single Rule Kept Classics Off the Hot 100** : For almost all of the 20th century, Billboard required a song to be issued as a standalone retail single to be chart-eligible. That is why Here Comes the Sun, Landslide, Vienna, and Stairway to Heaven do better on radio than they ever did on the charts. "a song had to be issued as a retail single."
**Category 1 - Deus Ex Machina** : Songs lifted by an outside cultural force such as sports, movies, TV, or sampling. Examples include Sweet Caroline, Don't Stop Believin', Twist and Shout, Bohemian Rhapsody, Son of a Preacher Man, Lust for Life, Old Time Rock and Roll, and Edge of Seventeen. "affected by an outside cultural force."
**Category 2 - Formatting** : Songs that didn't fit radio's format boxes in their day and underperformed, such as I Melt With You, Super Freak, and Dancing with Myself. "songs that didn't fit into radio's format boxes in their heyday and as a result, underperformed on the charts."
**Category 3 - Zeitgeist** : As public mood about an artist shifts over time, so do opinions about their songs. Listeners may like a song at first and grow to love it later, as with Van Morrison and Whitney Houston. "Over time as the public mood shifts about certain artists, so do opinions about their songs."
**Movie-Driven Legacy Hits** : Twist and Shout in Ferris Bueller's Day Off, Bohemian Rhapsody in Wayne's World, Son of a Preacher Man in Pulp Fiction, and Lust for Life in Trainspotting were not their artists' highest-charting hits but are now among their most-played or most-streamed tracks. "we could probably compile a list of legacy hits affected by the movies alone."
**No Perfect Yardstick for Ranking Legacy Hits** : Ranking the biggest legacy hits is hard because the songs peaked all over the Hot 100, and radio programmers and streaming listeners now prefer different songs. "There's no perfect yardstick."
