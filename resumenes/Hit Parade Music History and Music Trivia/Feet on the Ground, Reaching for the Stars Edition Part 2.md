# artist - Casey Kasem

## labels
Warner Records

## songs
**Letter from Elena (1964) - Spoken-Word Beatlemania Single** : After arriving in Los Angeles, Casey Kasem had a hit record under his own name, which was rare for a radio DJ. It was a minor hit that bubbled under the Hot 100 at number 103. Kasem didn't sing or play an instrument. It was a spoken-word single released to capitalize on the peak of Beatlemania. Kasem reads a real letter from a fan of his radio show who met and hugged George Harrison, her favorite Beatle, while George and Paul were walking with guards to their car. The melody underneath is the Beatles' "And I Love Her" from the A Hard Day's Night soundtrack. Kasem said he got a ton of listener feedback, which is why Warner Records released it as a single in the first place. "It was a spoken word single released to capitalize on the peak of Beatlemania."

## curiosities
**Birth as Kamal Amin Kasem - Detroit, 1932** : Kamal Amin Kasem was born in Detroit in 1932 to Lebanese parents who ran a grocery store. His beginnings were humble despite how polished he later sounded. Early on there weren't many DJing opportunities for a Lebanese American named Kamal Kasem. "Kamal Amin Kaseym was born in Detroit in 1932 to a pair of Lebanese parents who ran a grocery store."

**Eddie Chase's Make Believe Ballroom - The 1949 Countdown Vision** : At age 17, in 1949, Kasem began dreaming about radio when he heard Detroit DJ Eddie Chase host Make Believe Ballroom. The show featured a top ten list, with entries such as Eileen Wilson's romantic ballad "Be My Love" at number six. Kasem thought the countdown could be the whole show. He told Rob Durkee, author of American Top 40: The Countdown of the Century, that a national countdown was the kind of show he'd want to do. "That's the kind of show I'd want to do, a national countdown."

**High School Radio Club and Wayne State Radio Serials** : Young Kamal was a member of his Detroit high school radio club, where he covered local sports. At Wayne State University he voiced radio serials, including The Lone Ranger and Challenge of the Yukon. "When he got to Wayne State University, he voiced radio serials, including the lone ranger and challenge of the Yukon."

**Korean War DJ - American Forces Korea Network, 1952** : In 1952 Kasem was drafted into the Korean War. He worked as a DJ and announcer on the American Forces Korea Network. Afterward he returned to Michigan to focus on DJing, with short stints at stations in Flint and Detroit. "In 1952, Kaseym was drafted into the Korean War, where he worked as a DJ and announcer on American Forces Korea Network."

**Origin of the Name "Casey" - Casey at the Bat** : At Detroit's WJBK, a fellow DJ suggested Kamal go by "Casey," after the 1888 baseball poem "Casey at the Bat" by Ernest Thayer, which was well-known American lore by the 1950s. Listeners and fellow radio employees were already mangling his name and calling him Casey anyway. He branded himself on air as "Casey at the mic," and the name stuck. "So Kaseym branded himself on air as Kasey at the mic and the name stuck."

**Station Tour - Cleveland, Buffalo, San Francisco, Oakland** : After adopting the name Casey Kasem, he developed a polished radio voice and worked at stations in Cleveland, Buffalo, San Francisco, and Oakland, California. "He developed a polished radio voice and worked at stations in Cleveland, Buffalo, San Francisco, and Oakland, California."

**Who's Who in Pop Music Found in the Garbage - KEWB Oakland, 1962** : In 1962 the general manager at KEWB in Oakland told Kasem to change his on-air format. He was unsure what to do until moments before airtime, when he found a copy of the magazine Who's Who in Pop Music in the garbage. He fished it out and pulled as many facts as he could about the artists KEWB was playing. That night he introduced an Elvis Presley hit with patter about Elvis being born in a three-room shack and going on to live in a $30 million estate. This was his first proto-AT40 brainstorm, and deploying facts about artists and songs became his trademark. "That's when he found a copy of the magazine, Who's Who In Pop Music, in The Garbage."

**KRLA Los Angeles - Dress Rehearsal for AT-40** : Kasem took his fact-driven style from Oakland to Los Angeles and KRLA, the top-rated AM pop station. In a KRLA promo he presented the "KRLA Top 32," billed as Southern California's authentic guide to the most popular music, a dress rehearsal for what he would become famous for a decade later. "The K-R-L-A top 32 in Dex's Southern California's authentic guide to the most popular music heard 24 hours a day on K-R-L-A."

**Shebang with Dick Clark - First Weekly TV Exposure, 1964** : By 1964 Kasem was an emerging LA media personality who had hosted dance hops on local TV. That attracted famed producer and American Bandstand host Dick Clark. In 1964 Clark hired Kasem as co-host of a daily teenage music show called Shebang, which put him on camera weekly for the first time. One broadcast came from Anaheim Stadium, hosted by the Angels organization, and featured a "sweetheart tree letter" from a viewer. "In 1964, Clark hired Kaseym as co-host of a daily teenage music show called Shabang that put Kaseym on camera weekly for the first time."

**Film and TV Bit Parts - Biker Movies and Hawaii Five-O** : While in LA, Kasem auditioned for movie, TV and voice-over work. He landed bit parts in several low-budget biker movies and a part on the hit CBS police procedural Hawaii Five-O. "He got bit parts in several low budget biker movies and a part on the hit CBS police procedural TV show Hawaii 50."

**Voice of Shaggy on Scooby-Doo** : Kasem's most enduring non-radio work was voicing Shaggy, the tremulous stoner sidekick to the title dog on Scooby-Doo. The show launched on CBS in 1969 and ran for some 300 episodes. Through AT-40's first two unprofitable years, voice work like Scooby-Doo and TV ads provided most of his income. "Kaseym's most enduring non-radio work turned out to be voicing a major character on a long-running cartoon."

**Founding American Top 40 with Don Bustany - Late 1968** : In late 1968 Kasem contacted hometown friend and fellow Lebanese American broadcaster Don Bustany. They formed a production company to incubate their countdown idea. They connected with the syndicator Watermark, which contracted with Billboard for the rights to count down the top 40 of the Hot 100. Jingle writer Jim Kirk wrote the theme "Shukatoum," whose melody was later sung by a radio chorus to identify the show. "In late 1968, Kaseym contacted a hometown friend and fellow Lebanese American broadcaster Don Bistani and they formed a production company to incubate their countdown idea."

**AT-40 Premiere - July 4, 1970** : American Top 40 launched on July 4, 1970, on just seven radio stations and the Armed Forces Radio feed, counting down Billboard's chart for the week ending July 11, 1970. Casey was 38 and already sounded like he'd been doing the show for years. The first song was Marvin Gaye's "The End of Our Road" at number 40. Next came Mark Lindsay's "Silver Bird" at number 39. The first number one was Three Dog Night's "Mama Told Me Not to Come." "On July 4th, 1970, American Top 40 launched on just seven radio stations and the broadcast feed of Armed Forces Radio."

**"Keep Your Feet on the Ground and Keep Reaching for the Stars" - Signature Sign-off** : Kasem closed his first AT-40 broadcast with the aphorism that became his trademark. He'd been saying it on the radio off and on since the late '50s, but AT-40 made it legendary. "Until then, keep your feet in the ground and keep reaching for the star."

**Rapid Affiliate Growth and Folksy Phrasing** : In the first six months AT-40 went from seven stations to 75, then to nearly 120 by summer 1971. Kasem added folksy phrases like "from the Great Lakes to the Rio Grande" to remind listeners the show was national. "In the first six months, it went from seven stations to 75."

**The Art of the Tease - New Orleans Trivia** : Kasem mastered the tease, keeping listeners tuned in by withholding the answer to a trivia question. One listener from Oregon asked which city had been named most often in Top 40 song titles. Kasem said a southern city appeared in six Top 40 titles, the sixth then in the countdown, and promised the answer in ten minutes. After the break he revealed it was New Orleans. "The tease was the man's art form."

**Working for Free Until 1973** : For AT-40's first two years Kasem essentially worked for free, waiting for the show to break even. By 1973 enough affiliates were paying that it turned a profit. "For the first two years, Kason was essentially working for free, waiting for the show to break even."

**Genre Breadth and Black Artists on Southern Pop Stations** : Affiliates had to play AT-40 in its entirety, so all 40 Hot 100 hits aired, including R&B, funk and soul. AT-40 may have been the only time certain pop or rock stations, especially in the Southern US, played hits by Black artists, such as James Brown's "Sex Machine." Per Rob Durkee's book, some stations complained to Watermark, but Kasem, an old-school R&B fan, counted down every hit. A typical week might include pop, rock, R&B, country, Latin, disco and occasional jazz crossovers. Kasem told Durkee he expected the show to last at least 20 years. "I knew the formula worked. I knew people tuned in to find out what the number one record was."

**Show Innovations - Stereo (1973) and Four Hours (1978)** : AT-40 first broadcast in stereo in 1973. In 1978 it expanded from three hours to four because song lengths kept drifting higher through the '70s; the Eagles' "Hotel California" single edit ran over six minutes. The extra hour gave Kasem room for trivia and listener questions. "In 1978, the show expanded from three hours to four."

**Invention of the Long-Distance Dedication - August 26, 1978** : Inspired by "Letter from Elena," Kasem told Don Bustany and the team at AT-40's 1970 launch that if a similarly moving fan letter arrived, they should air it. It had to be real, music-related, romantic and broadly relatable, and he wouldn't force it. It took eight years. On the August 26, 1978 show he read a letter from James, a teenage boy in Louisiana. James had fallen for a girl named Desiree who moved to Germany with her army family, and he asked for Neil Diamond's "Desiree." Kasem then invited more letters, asking only that the dedicated song had been a hit. "Well, James, you got it. Here's your long-distance dedication."

**Flood of Dedication Mail** : Mail poured in. For six months staff sent postcards confirming requests had been received, but by spring 1979 that became impossible. The long-distance dedication became a permanent AT-40 feature for two decades, and Kasem soon read two per show. Rob Durkee tallied about 1,500 LDDs from 1978 to 1999. The most dedicated song was Kenny Rogers' 1982 hit "Through the Years." "Soon, Kaseyme was reading two LDDs per show just to keep up with the volume."

**Hollywood Walk of Fame, 520 Stations and America's Top 10** : By the '80s a record 520 stations carried AT-40, and Kasem had a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame. In 1980 he expanded the franchise to TV with the syndicated America's Top 10. It was a half-hour show, typically on Saturday mornings, focused on the very top of the Billboard charts. "A record 520 stations were now broadcasting the show."

**Ghostbusters Cameo - 1984** : Kasem was famous enough by 1984 to make an audible cameo in the summer blockbuster Ghostbusters, his voice coming out of Sigourney Weaver's radio. "His voice coming out of Sigourney Weaver's Radio."

**The "Dead Dog Tape" - Snuggles Outtake** : In the mid-1980s producers placed a long-distance dedication right after that week's number 13 hit, the Pointer Sisters' "Dare Me." The letter came from a family whose dog Snuggles had died, and they were dedicating Henry Gross's 1976 hit "Shannon," a song about a dog. Kasem objected to the tonal shift and launched into a profanity-laden rant, demanding a "concerted effort" not to follow up-tempo records with death dedications. The aired version was smooth, but the outtake was bootlegged from DJ to DJ until the audio was terrible. One DJ likened it to hearing Julie Andrews cursing on the set of Mary Poppins, and others played bleeped versions on morning shows. It could have derailed his career, but it showed how seriously he took his transitions and how much goodwill he had. "It became infamous and could have derailed Kasey Kason's career."

**Laughing It Off with Conan O'Brien - 1994** : Kasem appeared on Late Night with Conan O'Brien in 1994 and let Conan tease him about the tape. When O'Brien said "you got really mad," Kasem replied that he used every expletive there is. After Kasem explained the circumstances, Conan said, "you were right to flip out." "Well, Kasey replied, I did flip out. I flipped out all right."

**Leaving AT-40 for Westwood One - 1988** : In 1988 Kasem announced his departure after 18 years, following a contract dispute with ABC Radio Networks, which had acquired Watermark in 1982. ABC refused to raise his multi-million-dollar contract because it believed the show, and possibly Top 40 radio itself, was declining. In May 1988 he signed a five-year, $15 million deal with Westwood One. His last original AT-40 aired August 6, 1988, with his usual sign-off; the final song he counted down was Steve Winwood's "Roll With It." "Kassam announced he had signed a five-year, fifteen-million dollar contract with a new radio syndicator, Westwood One."

**Casey's Top 40 - January 1989** : Kasem's new show, Casey's Top 40, premiered in January 1989 on a new slate of stations when he was 56. It used Radio & Records' contemporary hits chart, which was based entirely on airplay and featured less rap and heavy metal. When 2 Live Crew's "Me So Horny" cracked the Hot 100 Top 40 in late 1989, Kasem never played it. "Kassie Kassam never played it at all."

**Buying Back American Top 40 - 1998 Relaunch** : Two years after Shadoe Stevens' AT-40 was canceled in early 1995, Kasem and Don Bustany bought back the American Top 40 name and trademark. They relaunched it in 1998 with Casey at the mic, still using the Radio & Records chart. "For Casey's fans, it was a return to a warmly familiar voice."

**Handing Off to Ryan Seacrest - End of 2003** : At the end of 2003, at 71, Kasem announced he'd leave AT-40 because the four-hour show had become too much. Ryan Seacrest, a rising DJ and American Idol host, replaced him and still hosts a modern AT-40. Kasem kept hosting the weekly adult contemporary countdown American Top 20. "The four-hour show was becoming too much for him."

**Final Retirement - July 4, 2009** : In 2005 Kasem renewed with Premiere Radio Networks. His show was later cut to 10 songs and retitled American Top 10. In 2009, at 77, he declined to find another syndicator or replacement host. He retired on July 4, 2009, the 39th anniversary of the first AT-40, one year shy of hosting as many years as there are songs in the Top 40. His last number one, on the AC chart, was Taylor Swift's "Love Story." "He fell just one year shy of counting down the hits as many years as there were songs in the Top 40."

**Illness and Death - June 15, 2014** : Kasem was first diagnosed with Parkinson's disease in 2007 but kept working two more years. In 2013 his daughter Kerri Kasem announced he had Lewy body dementia, which left him unable to speak. He died June 15, 2014, at 82, in a Gig Harbor, Washington hospital. A long, protracted battle between his wife Jean Kasem and his children from his first marriage over his elder care and burial generated lurid headlines. He was eventually laid to rest in Oslo, Norway. "Casey Kasim died on June 15th, 2014, at age 82, in a gig harbour Washington hospital."

**Humanitarian Legacy** : Tributes highlighted Kasem's years of humanitarian work for the Arab American community, progressive politicians, and the unhoused. "Highlighting Kasim's years of humanitarian work for the Arab American community, progressive politicians, and the unhoused."

**Dana Carvey's SNL Impression** : Kasem's earnest, dramatic-pause-laden voice was riffed on by countless comedians. One was Saturday Night Live's Dana Carvey, whose sketch featured an absurd trivia question about a band that never existed and ended with a garbled version of the signature sign-off. "Probably my favorite was Saturday Night Live's Dana Carvey, who at least did it with affection."

**"The Hits Get Bigger as the Numbers Get Smaller"** : Kasem conveyed detailed information very economically. A typical line noted Deniece Williams' "Let's Hear It for the Boy," which had spent two weeks at number one, was in its second week at number three. "The hits get bigger as the numbers get smaller."

# label - Billboard

## curiosities
**AT-40 Enshrined the Hot 100 as the Industry Standard** : Through the 1960s the Hot 100 was known within the industry but not to the public. Billboard competed with charts from Cashbox and Record World. Licensing it to a syndicated radio show via Watermark made it the music business standard for all time. "Using Billboard's chart on a syndicated radio show would enshrine it as the music business standard for all time."

# curiosity

**Shadoe Stevens' AT-40 Era (1988–1995)** : Shadoe Stevens took over AT-40 the week after Kasem left in 1988. A couple of years later the show switched from the Hot 100 to the Billboard Top 40 Radio Monitor, but the switch didn't slow its ratings erosion. Stevens lasted just under seven years, and his edition was canceled in early 1995. "His edition of American Top 40 was canceled in early 1995."

**The End of Hot 100 Countdowns** : As stations narrowcast in the 1990s toward tighter demographic niches, the Hot 100's Top 40 proved too eclectic, and some AT-40 affiliates skipped rap songs entirely. No major countdown would ever again use the Top 40 of the actual Hot 100. "No major countdown would ever again use the Top 40 of the actual Hot 100, a blow for chart fanatics."

**Ryan Seacrest's American Top 40** : Ryan Seacrest replaced Kasem at the end of 2003 and still hosts a modern version of AT-40. "To this day, Seacrest still hosts a modern version of AT-40."

**Typical Long-Distance Dedications** : Most letters were not ambitious. They came from homesick people who had moved away from a best friend, lovelorn singles pining for a missed connection, and parents dedicating songs to their children, and Kasem read them all. "Most of the letters were not this ambitious."
