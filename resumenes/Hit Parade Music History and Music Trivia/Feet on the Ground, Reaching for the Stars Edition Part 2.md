## albums
**Letter from Elena (1964) - Spoken Word Single** : This minor hit, which bubbled under the Hot 100 at number 103, was a spoken word single released by Casey Kasem to capitalize on the peak of Beatlemania. The track features Kasem reading a letter from a fan named Elena about meeting The Beatles, set to the instrumental melody of The Beatles' hit "And I Love Her" from the *Hard Day's Night Soundtrack*. This early experience with fan letters would later inspire the "Long-Distance Dedication" segment on American Top 40. "Letter from Elena may have been a footnote in Kasey's career, but it too would pay dividends later when Kaseym added a different kind of fan letter to American top 40."

## curiosities
**Birth and Early Life - Detroit (1932)** : Born Kamal Amin Kasem in Detroit in 1932 to Lebanese parents who operated a grocery store. He started dreaming about a radio career at age 17 after hearing Detroit DJ Eddie Chase's "Make Believe Ballroom" in 1949, envisioning a national countdown show. "Kamal Amin Kaseym was born in Detroit in 1932 to a pair of Lebanese parents who ran a grocery store."
**Early Radio Career and Military Service (1949-1952)** : Kasem was a member of his high school radio club in Detroit, covering local sports. At Wayne State University, he voiced radio serials like "The Lone Ranger" and "Challenge of the Yukon." In 1952, he was drafted into the Korean War, serving as a DJ and announcer on the American Forces Korea Network. "Then, in 1952, Kaseym was drafted into the Korean War, where he worked as a DJ and announcer on American Forces Korea Network."
**Origin of "Casey" Moniker (1950s)** : While working at Detroit's WJBK, a fellow DJ suggested he go by "Casey," inspired by Ernest Thayer's 1888 baseball poem "Casey at the Bat," because listeners and colleagues often mispronounced his birth name, Kamal. He then branded himself on air as "Casey at the mic," and the name became permanent. "When Kamal Kaseym was at Detroit's WJBK, a fellow DJ suggested that Kaseym go by Kasey, as in Kasey at the bat."
**Development of Artist Fact-Sharing (1962)** : While working at KEWB in Oakland in 1962, his general manager told him to change his DJ format. Moments before going on air, he found a "Who's Who In Pop Music" magazine in the garbage and decided to pull facts about artists, like Elvis Presley's birth in a three-room shack and subsequent $30 million estate, making this his trademark. "Deploying facts about artists and songs became Kaseym's trademark."
**KRLA Top 32 in Dex (Mid-1960s)** : After transferring to Los Angeles, Kasem worked at the top-rated AM radio pop station KRLA, where he did a dress rehearsal for his future countdown show, presenting "The K-R-L-A top 32 in Dex's Southern California's authentic guide to the most popular music." "In this K-R-L-A promo, you can hear Kaseym doing a dress rehearsal for what he would become most famous for a decade later."
**TV Hosting Role on "Shabang" (1964)** : Attracted the attention of producer Dick Clark, who hired Kasem as co-host for "Shabang," a daily teenage music show in 1964, marking his first regular on-camera appearance. "In 1964, Clark hired Kaseym as co-host of a daily teenage music show called Shabang that put Kaseym on camera weekly for the first time."
**Voice of Shaggy on "Scooby-Doo" (1969-Ongoing)** : His most enduring non-radio work was voicing the character Shaggy Rogers, the tremulous stoner sidekick, on the CBS cartoon "Scooby-Doo," which launched in 1969 and ran for over 300 episodes. He continued this work, along with TV advertisements, to earn income before American Top 40 became profitable. "But Kaseym's most enduring non-radio work turned out to be voicing a major character on a long-running cartoon. The taggy, the tremulous stoner sidekick to the titular mutt on Scooby Doo, which launched on CBS in 1969 and would go on to run for some 300 episodes."
**Co-Creation of American Top 40 (1968-1970)** : In late 1968, Kasem contacted his friend Don Bistani to form a production company and develop his teenage vision of a national countdown show. They partnered with Watermark, who secured rights to use Billboard's Hot 100 chart, leading to the launch of American Top 40 on July 4, 1970. "In late 1968, Kaseym contacted a hometown friend and fellow Lebanese American broadcaster Don Bistani and they formed a production company to incubate their countdown idea."
**Trademark Sign-Off - "Keep Your Feet on the Ground..." (Late 1950s-1970)** : Kasem's iconic sign-off, "Keep your feet on the ground and keep reaching for the star," was used on the radio since the late 1950s but became legendary through American Top 40. He used it on the very first AT-40 broadcast. "He'd actually been seeing it on the radio off and on since the late 50s, but AT-40 would make it legendary."
**Early American Top 40 Profitability (1970-1973)** : For the first two years of American Top 40, Kasem worked for free, with the show not turning a profit until 1973, relying on his income from voice-over work for "Scooby-Doo" and TV advertisements. "For the first two years, Kason was essentially working for free, waiting for the show to break even, and earning most of his income from his other voice over work like Scooby Doo and TV advertisements."
**"Pop-demist" Philosophy and Genre Diversity** : Kasem embraced musical variety on American Top 40, counting down all 40 songs on the Hot 100, which often included R&B, Funk, Soul, Pop, Rock, Country, Latin, Disco, and Jazz crossover singles. This approach sometimes challenged pop or rock stations, especially in the South, to play hits by black artists, and solidified his belief that a full range of popular music was worthy of appreciation. "He knew musical variety meant great radio."
**American Top 40 Innovations - Stereo, Length, LDD (1973-1978)** : American Top 40 introduced stereo broadcasting in 1973 and expanded from three to four hours in 1978 to accommodate longer song lengths and new content, including trivia and the "Long-Distance Dedication" segment. "In 1978, the show expanded from three hours to four."
**Origin of Long-Distance Dedications (1970-1978)** : Inspired by the genuine fan letter that led to his "Letter from Elena" single, Kasem proposed to his production team in 1970 that they air similar "moving, music-related, romantic, and broadly relatable" fan letters. It took eight years for the right letter to arrive, debuting on August 26, 1978, with a dedication of Neil Diamond's "Desiree" from a fan named James. "What Kasey loved about the letter from Elena experience was the letter was real."
**Prom King LDD Attempt (1979-1980)** : In April 1979, a high school junior named Barry from Minnesota dedicated Cheryl Ladd's "Think It Over" on AT-40, hoping she would agree to be his prom date. Cheryl Ladd politely declined but confirmed her call with Kasem's team. "In April 1979, a high school junior from Minnesota named Barry asked Kaseyme to play Think It Over by Cheryl Ladd, who was both a pop singer and one of the stars of TV's Charlie's Angels. In the hopes that Ladd herself would think it over and be his date to the prom."
**Most Dedicated Song - Kenny Rogers (1978-1999)** : According to Rob Durkey's book on American Top 40, Kenny Rogers' 1982 hit "Through the Years" was the most frequently dedicated song among the roughly 1500 Long-Distance Dedications aired between 1978 and 1999. "he determined that the most dedicated song was Kenny Rogers, Mockish, 1982 hit through the years."
**Pet Shop Boys LDD (1986)** : In April 1986, a woman from Trinidad dedicated "West End Girls" by the Pet Shop Boys to Neil Tennant, whom she had met in a London pub two years earlier before their fame, encouraging him to pursue his dreams; the song was number 5 on the countdown that week on its way to number 1. "She dedicated their debut hit West End Girls, which was at number 5 on the countdown that very week on its way to number 1 to Neil Tennant himself, congratulating him for making it."
**Hollywood Walk of Fame Star (1980s)** : By the 1980s, Kasem's status as a cultural icon was cemented, leading to him receiving a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame. "Kasey Kason was a cultural icon with a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame."
**"America's Top 10" TV Show (1980)** : In 1980, Kasem expanded the American Top 40 franchise to television, hosting "America's Top 10," a syndicated half-hour show that aired on Saturday mornings and focused on the top hits of the Billboard charts. "In 1980, Kasey expanded the AT-40 franchise to television when he began hosting the syndicated America's Top 10, a half-hour show that typically ran on Saturday mornings with a tight focus on the very top of the Billboard charts."
**Ghostbusters Cameo (1984)** : Kasem made an audible cameo in the 1984 summer movie blockbuster "Ghostbusters," with his voice heard coming out of Sigourney Weaver's radio. "Kasey Kason was also well known enough by 1984 that he made an audible cameo in the summer movie blockbuster Ghostbusters, his voice coming out of Sigourney Weaver's Radio."
**"The Dead Dog Tape" Incident (Mid-1980s)** : An infamous bootleg studio outtake circulated among radio professionals, featuring Kasem's expletive-laden rant after producers scheduled a Long-Distance Dedication about a deceased dog named Snuggles, dedicated with Henry Gross's "Shannon," immediately after The Pointer Sisters' up-tempo "Dare Me." Kasem's serious objection to the jarring tonal shift demonstrated his dedication to the show's flow and sincerity. "It became known colloquially as the dead dog tamerie."
**Interview with Conan O'Brien about "Dead Dog Tape" (1994)** : Kasem publicly addressed "The Dead Dog Tape" incident, appearing on "Late Night with Conan O'Brien" in 1994. He acknowledged using "every expletive there is," and O'Brien ultimately agreed that Kasem was justified in his frustration over the poor transition. "Kaseyms laughed off the incident, even appearing on late night with Conan O'Brien in 1994 and letting Conan tease him about it."
**Departure from American Top 40 (1988)** : After 18 years, Kasem announced his departure from American Top 40 in 1988 due to a contract dispute with ABC Radio Networks, which had acquired syndicator Watermark. He believed the network undervalued his services and the future of top 40 radio, signing a new $15 million contract with Westwood One. "In 1988, Casey Kassam announced his departure from American top 40 after 18 years with the show."
**Launch of "Casey's Top 40" (1989)** : In January 1989, at age 56, Kasem launched his new countdown show, "Casey's Top 40," with Westwood One, using the "Radio & Records contemporary hits chart" instead of Billboard's Hot 100, which focused more on radio airplay. "Meanwhile, Kassam's new show, simply called Kassie's Top 40, premiered on a new slate of radio stations in January 1989."
**Buyback and Relaunch of American Top 40 (1998)** : In an unexpected turn, Kasem and his co-creator Don Bistani bought back the "American Top 40" name and trademark two years after its cancellation under Shadow Stevens, relaunching the show with Kasem at the mic in 1998, still utilizing the "Radio & Records chart." "Casey Kasim and his old friend and colleague Don Bostani bought back the American Top 40 name and trademark and relaunched the show in 1998 with Casey at the mic."
**Second Departure from American Top 40 (2003)** : At the end of 2003, at age 71, Kasem announced he would leave American Top 40 again, finding the four-hour show too demanding. He was replaced by Ryan Seacrest. "At the end of 2003, at age 71, Casey Kasim announced he would leave American Top 40."
**Hosting "American Top 20" and "American Top 10" (2003-2009)** : After leaving the main American Top 40, Kasem continued hosting a weekly adult contemporary hits countdown called "American Top 20." In 2005, he renewed his deal with Premiere Radio Networks, and his show was reduced to "American Top 10." "While Seacrest took over, Casey continued hosting a weekly adult contemporary hits countdown called American Top 20."
**Retirement from Radio (2009)** : On July 4, 2009, the 39th anniversary of the first American Top 40, Kasem, aged 77, retired from radio entirely, choosing not to find another syndicator or replacement host. He ended his career one year short of counting down hits for as many years as there are songs in the Top 40. "Finally, in 2009, Casey, age 77, decided against finding another syndicator or replacement host and announced he would simply retire from radio on July 4th, 2009."
**Health Issues and Death (2007-2014)** : Diagnosed with Parkinson's disease in 2007 (later identified as Louis Body dementia in 2013), which left him unable to speak, Kasem died on June 15, 2014, at age 82, in a Gig Harbor, Washington hospital. "A few months after Kerry's announcement, Casey Kasim died on June 15th, 2014, at age 82, in a gig harbour Washington hospital."
**Family Disputes and Burial (2014)** : Following his death, there were widely reported disputes between his wife, Jean Kasem, and his children from his first marriage regarding his elder care and burial. His body was eventually laid to rest in Oslo, Norway. "His body was eventually laid to rest in Oslo, Norway."

**American Top 40 - Founding (1970)** : Co-created by Casey Kasem and Don Bistani, American Top 40 launched on July 4, 1970, on just seven radio stations and Armed Forces Radio. "In the first six months, it went from seven stations to 75. Then leapt to nearly 120 the following summer of 71."
**American Top 40 - "New Orleans" Trivia Answer** : Casey Kasem revealed New Orleans as the city most frequently named in song titles that made the top 40, having been mentioned in six such hits. "Casey revealed that the answer was New Orleans."
**American Top 40 - Expansion to Stereo (1973)** : American Top 40 began broadcasting in stereo in 1973, moving beyond its initial mono format. "The show was first broadcast in stereo, not mono, in 1973."
**American Top 40 - Expansion to Four Hours (1978)** : In 1978, American Top 40 expanded from its original three-hour format to four hours, partly to accommodate the increasing length of popular songs and to allow more room for special content like trivia and listener questions, including the "Long-Distance Dedication." "In 1978, the show expanded from three hours to four."
**American Top 40 - Long-Distance Dedication Popularity (1979)** : After its debut in 1978, the "Long-Distance Dedication" segment became incredibly popular, with mail pouring into the AT-40 offices. The staff initially sent postcards to acknowledge requests but were overwhelmed by spring 1979, leading to the segment becoming a permanent feature for two decades, often with two dedications per show. "The long-distance dedication became a permanent feature of AT-40 for the next two decades. Soon, Kaseyme was reading two LDDs per show just to keep up with the volume."
**American Top 40 - Record Station Count (1980s)** : By the 1980s, American Top 40 had become a radio fixture, broadcast on a record 520 stations across the country. "A record 520 stations were now broadcasting the show, and Kasey Kason was a cultural icon with a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame."
**American Top 40 - Host Transition to Shadow Stevens (1988)** : Following Casey Kasem's departure from American Top 40 in 1988, Shadow Stevens took over as the countdown host starting April 13th, introducing Bobby McFerrin's "Don't Worry, Be Happy" as his first song. "The very next week, April 13th, Kassam's AT-40 successor, Shadow Stevens, took right over as countdown host."
**American Top 40 - Shift Away from Hot 100 Chart (Early 1990s)** : In the early 1990s, American Top 40, under Shadow Stevens, switched from using Billboard's Hot 100 to the "Billboard Top 40 radio monitor" chart, based solely on radio airplay. This reflected a trend of stations "narrowcasting" and avoiding the eclectic mix of the Hot 100, which included rap and heavy metal that some stations skipped entirely. "A couple of years later, the original American Top 40 with Shadow Stevens switched from the Hot 100 to the Billboard Top 40 radio monitor."
**American Top 40 - Cancellation (1995)** : Shadow Stevens' run as host lasted just under seven years before his edition of American Top 40 was canceled in early 1995 due to eroding ratings. "His edition of American Top 40 was canceled in early 1995."
**American Top 40 - Host Transition to Ryan Seacrest (2003)** : At the end of 2003, Ryan Seacrest, a rising radio DJ and "American Idol" TV host, replaced Casey Kasem as the host of American Top 40. **Billboard Magazine - Role in American Top 40 (1970)** : Billboard magazine contracted with Watermark to provide rights for American Top 40 to count down the top 40 of its flagship Hot 100 chart. This arrangement helped enshrine the Billboard Hot 100 as the music business standard, as it was previously one of several competing charts. "Watermark, who contracted with Billboard magazine to get the rights to countdown the top 40 of their flagship Hot 100 chart."
**Billboard Magazine - Chart Competitors (1960s)** : In the 1960s, Billboard's Hot 100 was largely known within the recording industry but competed with other major music charts from magazines like Cashbox and Record World before American Top 40 cemented its status. "Billboard's was one of several major music charts, competing with surveys from magazines like Cashbox and Record World."
**Billboard Magazine - Fan Disgust Over Chart Change (1992)** : Chris Malanfee wrote to Billboard in May 1992, expressing his "disgust" over American Top 40's decision to switch from the Hot 100 to an all-radio chart, lamenting the loss of a countdown that could feature diverse genres like Nirvana and Geto Boys. **Watermark - American Top 40 Syndication (1968)** : Watermark was the radio syndication company that partnered with Casey Kasem and Don Bistani's production company to launch American Top 40. They contracted with Billboard magazine to secure the rights to use the Hot 100 chart. "They connected with a radio syndication company called Watermark, who contracted with Billboard magazine to get the rights to countdown the top 40 of their flagship Hot 100 chart."

"ABC radio networks, which had acquired Watermark, the company that syndicated AT-40 back in 1982."
**Westwood One - Kasem's New Syndicator (1988)** : After leaving American Top 40 due to a contract dispute with ABC Radio Networks, Casey Kasem signed a five-year, $15 million contract with Westwood One, a new radio syndicator, to launch his new show, "Casey's Top 40," in 1989. "In May of 1988, Kassam announced he had signed a five-year, fifteen-million dollar contract with a new radio syndicator, Westwood One, and would launch a new countdown show with them."
**ABC Radio Networks - Contract Dispute (1988)** : ABC Radio Networks, which had acquired Watermark in 1982, was involved in a multi-million dollar contract renewal dispute with Casey Kasem in 1988. Their unwillingness to increase his contract, believing top 40 radio was in decline, led to Kasem's departure from American Top 40. "Behind the scenes, he had been embroiled in a contract renewal dispute with ABC radio networks, which had acquired Watermark, the company that syndicated AT-40 back in 1982."
**Premiere Radio Networks - Kasem's Later Syndicator (2005)** : In 2005, Casey Kasem renewed his deal with Premiere Radio Networks to continue hosting his adult contemporary countdown shows, "American Top 20" and later "American Top 10," after his second departure from the main American Top 40. "In 2005, Casey renewed his deal with Premiere Radio Networks to continue hosting."

**Don Bistani - American Top 40 Co-Creator** : Don Bistani, a hometown friend and fellow Lebanese American broadcaster, was contacted by Casey Kasem in late 1968 to form a production company and incubate the idea for a national countdown show, leading to the co-creation of American Top 40. "Kaseym contacted a hometown friend and fellow Lebanese American broadcaster Don Bistani and they formed a production company to incubate their countdown idea."
