## labels

## albums
**Labour of Love (1983) - All-Covers Homage to Their Reggae Influences** : In 1982 UB40's UK hits were getting smaller, and in early 1983 one single missed the British top 40 entirely, so the band rebooted with this album. It consists entirely of covers, ten songs the group grew up with, all by reggae artists such as Jimmy Cliff, The Wailers, Boy Friday and Winston Groovy. Red Red Wine was the lead single, and the band considered it a reggae cover because they only knew Tony Tribe's version. On the Billboard album chart it peaked at No. 39 on its first run. It went gold in America (half a million US sales) by summer 1986, three years after release. During the 1988 revival A&M pressed new CDs and cassettes of the album. It then reached a new 1988 peak of No. 8 on the Billboard album chart and ultimately went platinum. "Labor of Love, as its title suggests, was an affectionate homage to UB40's influences."

**Labour of Love II (1989) - Covers of Big Pop Classics** : This was the follow-up after Red Red Wine's US No. 1, released in late 1989. Rather than remaking hardcore reggae deep cuts, UB40 covered "big unmistakable pop classics of yesterday." Its first big hit, in 1990, was The Way You Do the Things You Do, which returned UB40 to the US top 10 at No. 7. The album also yielded Here I Am (Come and Take Me), a No. 6 hit in early 1991. "But after Red Red Wine they went for the jugular and hit the jackpot."

## songs
**Red Red Wine (1983) - UK No. 1, Later US No. 1 in 1988** : UB40 modelled their version not on Neil Diamond's original, which was unknown to them, but on Tony Tribe's 1969 rocksteady version. Tribe had picked up the tempo from Diamond, and UB40 slowed it back down about half a step, turning it from rocksteady into "a kind of swaying lover's rock." The song's chart history ran as follows:
- **UK, 1983:** It went to No. 1 in September 1983. It was their first UK No. 1 and returned them to the top 10.
- **US, first run:** It debuted on the Hot 100 in January 1984, which was the group's first US chart appearance of any kind. It entered the top 40 two months later and peaked at No. 34 for the week ending March 31, 1984.
- **US re-entry, 1988:** It re-entered the Hot 100 at No. 85 on August 13, 1988. Billboard listed it as a re-entry in its 16th chart week. It was back in the top 40 three weeks later and leapt to No. 28 a week after that, making it their biggest US hit since I Got You Babe. It reached the top five less than a month later.
- **US No. 1:** It hit No. 1 for the week ending October 15, 1988, nine weeks after re-entering the chart. It spent a dozen weeks in the top 40 on that second run.

In the UK it was their first No. 1, and in America it became their first No. 1 as well. "Red Red Wine was a true trans-lantic hit."

**Food for Thought (1980) - No. 4 UK Breakthrough** : This was the lead of UB40's three straight UK top 10 singles by the end of 1980. It peaked at No. 4 and was a diatribe against world hunger. Like most of their early hits, it was an original. "leading with their number four single, Food for Thought, a diatribe against World Hunger."

**I Think It's Going to Rain Today (1980) - Randy Newman Cover B-Side Hit** : This was a B-side hit from UB40's first wave, covering a classic Randy Newman song. It was the only non-original among UB40's eight straight UK top 40 singles in their first three years. "Notably, their B-side hit, I think it's going to rain today, a cover of a classic, Randy Newman song."

**I Got You Babe (1985) - Duet with Chrissie Hynde** : This was a remake of the Sonny & Cher classic, recorded with their mentor Chrissie Hynde of the Pretenders. It became UB40's second UK No. 1 in summer 1985. In the US it reached No. 28 on the Hot 100 that same summer, slightly better than Red Red Wine's first US run. "In the UK, this cover became UB40's second number one hit in the summer of 1985."

**Breakfast in Bed (1988) - The Single That Red Red Wine Killed** : This was a cover of a 1968 Dusty Springfield single, again recorded with Chrissie Hynde. It reached No. 6 in the UK. A&M promoted it to US radio in 1988, but when KZZP's Guy Zapoleon and his staff were serviced the single, it led them to dig into UB40's back catalogue and revive Red Red Wine instead. As other stations followed, the momentum of Breakfast in Bed was killed. "In fact, breakfast in bed never even made the hot 100 at all."

**The Way You Do the Things You Do (1990) - Temptations Cover** : This was the first big hit from Labour of Love II. It returned UB40 to the US top 10 at No. 7 and charted higher on the Hot 100 than the Temptations' original. "UB40 returned to the US top 10 with their cover of the temptations the way you do the things you do."

**Here I Am (Come and Take Me) (1991) - Al Green Cover** : This came from Labour of Love II and was a No. 6 US hit in early 1991. It charted higher than Al Green's original. "they did even better with their take on Al Green's Here I Am, Common Take Me, a number six hit in early 1991."

**Can't Help Falling in Love (1993) - Biggest Hit of Their Career** : This Elvis Presley cover spent seven weeks at No. 1 on the Hot 100. It was the third biggest hit of 1993, after I Will Always Love You and Whoomp! (There It Is), and it charted higher than Elvis's original. "UB40 had the biggest hit of their career when their cover of Elvis Presley's Can't Help Falling in Love spent seven weeks on top of the hot 100."

## curiosities
**Band Name from Unemployment Form 40** : UB40 formed in late 1978 in Birmingham, England, a little less than a decade after Tony Tribe's hit. They named themselves after the government unemployment-benefit paperwork, form 40. "They named their group after the government paperwork you filled out when living on the dull, unemployment benefit, form 40."

**Biracial Eight-Piece from Multicultural Birmingham** : The group had eight members, half white and half black. They came together in a Birmingham awash in ska, rocksteady and reggae, thanks to the city's tens of thousands of Jamaican diaspora and descendants. "This group of eight members, half white, half black, came together in a Birmingham awash in ska, Rocksteady, and reggae."

**Never Knew Neil Diamond Wrote Red Red Wine** : Guitarist Robin Campbell told Fred Bronson, for The Billboard Book of Number One Hits, that the band only knew Tony Tribe's 1969 version. That version had charted at No. 46 in the UK when the members were kids. "we had no idea Neil Diamond had anything to do with it. The only version we were aware of was the Tony Tribe version."

**Discovered by Chrissie Hynde in a Pub** : Chrissie Hynde saw UB40 playing in a pub and gave them a slot opening for her band, the Pretenders. She became their mentor and later recorded with them. "UB40 got their break when Chrissy Hind saw them playing in a pub and gave them a slot as an opener for her band, the pretenders."

**Catching the Two-Tone Wave** : UB40 broke fairly quickly at a time when England was mad for two-tone ska and other new wave/reggae blends. They "picked up the ball" just as two-tone bands like the Specials were tailing off. They scored eight straight UK top 40 singles in their first three years. "UB40 were lucky enough to catch a wave as it crested."

**Astro's Toasting Bridge on Red Red Wine** : The most original addition UB40 made to the song was the bridge, where Terence Wilson (aka Astro) toasted. Toasting is the traditional Afro-Caribbean form of rap, a 1960s Jamaican precursor to hip hop. The bridge was unusual for an early-'80s hit, when rap bridges were only starting to appear on records like Blondie's Rapture. By 1988, the rise of new jack swing and early rap crossovers made Astro's toasting seem "rather prescient." "UB40's version of Red Red Wine devoted a sizable chunk of its running time to Astro toasting over a beat."

**Casey Kasem's 1984 Introduction** : American Top 40 introduced the song during its first US run in 1984. "Here's the 8-man reggae band with a recent number one song and great Britain. It's UB40."

**Same US Label Throughout** : UB40 had not changed American labels between 1983 and 1988, which made the revival possible. A&M figured it would make money either way. "Luckily, the band hadn't changed American labels in all that time."

**Covers That Outcharted the Originals** : UB40's covers of the Temptations, Al Green and Elvis Presley all charted higher on the Hot 100 than the originals, and so did Red Red Wine relative to Neil Diamond's single. The song took three tries to top the charts: one by Diamond and two by UB40. "the dud 1968 Neil Diamond single that took three tries to top the charts, one try by Diamond and two tries by UB40."

**Biggest Beneficiary of the Fad They Started** : UB40 had spent the mid-1980s as a reggae-pop covers band with "middling success." The "would have been, should have been" fad they triggered benefited them more than any other act. "But the act that benefited most from the would have been should have been FAD was the band that started at all in 1988, UB40."

## albums
**Just for You (1967) - Second Album, Contains Red Red Wine** : This was only Diamond's second album as a recording artist. It contained his original recording of Red Red Wine. "in 1967, when he wrote and recorded just for you, the album containing Red Red Wine, and only Diamond's second album as a recording artist."

## songs
**Red Red Wine (1968) - Original Version, No. 62 Flop** : Diamond wrote and first recorded the song. It is a morose, regretful saloon ballad about a man trying to drink away the memory of a woman who left him. Its production used strings and gently plucked guitar and "wants to be a slow dance number," but it was judged "a little overproduced." It peaked at No. 62 on the Hot 100 in spring 1968 in a three-week chart run, making it one of his worst performers of that era. "Honestly, it was a dud, and it arguably even broke his stride."

**Cherry Cherry (1966) - First Top 10 as an Artist** : This was Diamond's first top 10 hit as a performer. "He scored his first top 10 as an artist in 1966, with Cherry Cherry."

**I'm a Believer (1966) - First No. 1 as Songwriter** : Diamond recorded the song himself first, then gave it to the Monkees, who took it to No. 1. It was his first No. 1, earned as a songwriter by 1967. "A song Diamond recorded himself first, before bequeathing it to the monkeys."

**Solitary Man (1966) - Breakthrough Single** : This was his 1966 breakthrough single, in a ruminative acoustic mode, with "stately grace." Red Red Wine is described as "solitary man, Part 2." "you can hear Diamond in this ruminative mode on his 1966 breakthrough single."

**Girl, You'll Be a Woman Soon (1967) - Last Top 10 for Two Years** : This was a brooding acoustic ballad and a No. 10 hit in spring 1967. It was Diamond's last top 10 for more than two years. "Girl You'll Be a Woman Soon was a number 10 hit in the spring of 67."

**New Orleans (1968) - Missed the Top 40** : This single was paired with Red Red Wine as the two singles that ended his run of six straight top 40 hits from 1966–67. "Diamond fell off with his next pair of singles, New Orleans and Red, Red, Wine, both of which missed the top 40."

**Sweet Caroline (1969) - Top Five Comeback** : This top five smash in summer 1969 "righted the ship" after a half dozen singles missed the top 40 in 1968–69. It is associated with Boston Red Sox fans. "righted the ship by the summer of 69, with his top five smash, Sweet Caroline."

## curiosities
**Brill Building Breakthrough** : Diamond, from Brooklyn, broke through as a writer at the Brill Building in Midtown Manhattan. Unlike his fellow Brooklynite and Brill Building writer Carole King, he didn't wait until the '70s to become a front-line artist. He often wrote songs for himself that became hits for others. "Neil Diamond broke through as a writer at the Brill Building."

**Six Straight Top 40s, Then a Slump** : Diamond scored six straight top 40 hits in 1966–67. Then a half dozen of his singles missed the top 40 in 1968 and 1969. "In fact, a half dozen Diamond singles missed the top 40 in 1968 and 1969."

**One of the 1970s' Ten Biggest Hitmakers** : By the 1970s Diamond was a perennial Hot 100 chart-topper. Billboard chart historian Joel Whitburn ranks him among that decade's ten biggest hitmakers. "he became, according to Billboard chart-history and Joel Whitburn, one of the decade's ten biggest hitmakers."

**Rock and Roll Hall of Famer Still Touring** : The transcript describes Diamond as a Rock and Roll Hall of Famer who still records and tours successfully. "Perhaps you've heard of this gentleman who still records and tours very successfully today."

**Performs Red Red Wine UB40-Style Live** : Diamond profits from songwriting royalties on the transformed hit and regards it as a transformed work. Live, he often uses the rocksteady tempo of Tony Tribe and UB40 and even performs Astro's toast-style rap break. "And Neil even takes the toast-style rap break of UB40's Astro out for a spin."


# artist - Tony Tribe

## genres
Rocksteady

## songs
**Red Red Wine (1969) - Rocksteady Reinvention, UK No. 46** : Tribe, a Jamaican rocksteady singer, reconfigured Neil Diamond's song from the ground up. He kept its fundamental sadness but brought it up-tempo enough to give it a lilt, turning a slow-dance record into a "drinking your cares away record." It was a hit in Jamaica and peaked at No. 46 in the UK in 1969. This was the version UB40's members heard as kids in Birmingham, and their cover was modelled on it. The track is described as a bridge between Diamond's flop and the song's second life. "His version is utterly transformed."

## curiosities
**Sad Drinking Song Turned Fun Drinking Song** : Tribe's reinvention set up UB40's eventual success. "Two decades earlier, Tony Tribe had turned Neil Diamond's sad drinking song into a fun drinking song."

## songs
**When I'm With You (1983/1989) - Power Ballad Revived to No. 1** : This was Sheriff's one and only hit, a Canadian power ballad that peaked at No. 61 in 1983. In late 1988, inspired by Red Red Wine, Minneapolis radio programmer Brian Phillips pulled it from his station's library and put it into rotation, and it went viral. It re-entered the Hot 100 at No. 84 in late November 1988, the same week Red Red Wine slipped out of the top 40. Capitol reissued the single in fall 1988, and it reached No. 1 by February 1989. "by February of 1989, Sheriffs One and Only Hit had repeated UB40's feet and reached number one on the Hot 100."

## curiosities
**Promoting a Mirage** : Sheriff had broken up four years before the revival. Capitol played along but was annoyed. Its vice president of promotion told the LA Times: "we should be looking forwards rather than backwards... There's no future in it." "The single climbed the Hot 100 rapidly, but capital was, in essence, promoting a mirage."

**Members Formed Alias** : Sheriff's lead singer and guitarist quickly formed a new group, Alias, to capitalize on the revival. "cleverly the band's lead singer and guitarist quickly formed a new group, Alias."

# genre - Rocksteady

## curiosities
**Bridge Between Ska and Reggae** : Rocksteady was the predominant Jamaican music of the mid-to-late 1960s. It bridged the frenetic, up-tempo ska of the early '60s and the smokier, groovier, more "woke" and more durable reggae. It has a steady, mid-tempo pulse but still rocks gently. "As its name suggests, Rocksteady has a steady pulse, but it still rocks, albeit gently."


# genre - Reggae

## curiosities
**A Genre Built on Covers** : Reggae is built on Jamaican sound systems sharing and reimagining beats and songs. When Jamaican artists cover Anglo pop songs, they often reconfigure the structure from the ground up. "Reggae in all its permutations is famous for covers."

**Toasting as Hip Hop Precursor** : Toasting is the traditional Afro-Caribbean form of rap. In the 1960s it was the Jamaican precursor to what became hip hop. "The traditional Afro Caribbean form of rap that in the 60s was the Jamaican precursor to what became hip hop."

**UK Staple vs. US Fad** : In England reggae has been part of the pop mix since the '60s. In the US it arrives in small waves, then recedes:
- **Early 1970s:** A small boom of light reggae singles topped the Hot 100, such as Johnny Nash's I Can See Clearly Now and Eric Clapton's cover of Bob Marley's I Shot the Sheriff.
- **Late 1970s:** Reggae receded on US pop charts during the disco years.
- **Early 1980s:** Another boom brought top 10 US hits in 1983 from Culture Club, Guyana's Eddy Grant (reggae-funk) and Birmingham's Musical Youth (traditional reggae).

"In the US, we tend to treat Reggae like a trendlet or a fad."

**A Dozen Reggae No. 1s** : Red Red Wine is one of roughly a dozen songs with a reggae rhythm to top the Billboard Hot 100 over the last half century. "It's one of roughly a dozen songs that have topped the Billboard Hot 100 over the last half century that's sport a reggae rhythm."

**Balked, Then Embraced the Red Red Wine Revival** : At first A&M resisted Guy Zapoleon's urging to re-release the nearly five-year-old single. # curiosity

**Guy Zapoleon and KZZP Phoenix Revive Red Red Wine** : Guy Zapoleon was a radio legend and in 1988 the program director of KZZP-FM in Phoenix, Arizona. His staff were serviced the Breakfast in Bed single, dug into UB40's catalogue, and on hearing Red Red Wine again after four years reportedly said "see now that's a hit." He tested it on his Saturday-night dance show Party Patrol. Requests came in "hot and heavy," so he moved it into regular rotation, ignoring UB40's current single. He then urged A&M to re-release it, and other stations followed until the song "essentially went viral." "It happened because of a rogue radio DJ and a bit of luck."

**"Would Have Been, Should Have Been" Philosophy** : Zapoleon described his career-long practice to Fred Bronson in The Billboard Book of Number One Hits. "If the current music wasn't up to snuff, I would dig into a pile of songs that I thought should have been a hit."

**The Two-Year Re-release Fad** : Red Red Wine accidentally kicked off a two-year fad of programmers raiding their vaults. By mid-1989 the Hot 100 was "a wash in three, five, and even nine year old singles," including songs by Sheriff, Synch, Benny Mardones and Peter Gabriel. The industry tried to co-opt the fad by having bands re-record old hits (Real Life, Modern English). By 1990 it was spent, and few acts' careers benefited. "Guy's Opolians would have been, should have been, experiment had become a phenomenon."

**Second Runs Created Permanent Oldies** : Even revived songs that charted lower the second time, such as Into the Night and In Your Eyes, gained long-term radio fortunes. "Just charting a second time put these songs into permanent oldies rotation."

**First Cassingle of Red Red Wine** : In 1983–84 the cassette single ("cassingle") hadn't been invented, so Red Red Wine was issued only on 45 RPM vinyl. The 1988 re-release was the first time the single could be bought on tape. Copies of Labour of Love were shipped to stores like Sam Goody and Strawberries. "this 1988 re-release was the first time the Red Red Wine single could be bought on tape."

**Late-80s Radio Climate and the Cocktail Effect** : By the late '80s, new wave and reggae had largely fallen out of favor at top 40 radio. The charts were dominated by diva pop (Whitney Houston, Madonna) and hair metal (Bon Jovi, Def Leppard). The rise of new jack swing and rap's first pop crossovers gave radio a taste for hip-hop sounds. The summer 1988 Tom Cruise movie Cocktail spawned two vaguely island-flavored No. 1s: the Beach Boys' Kokomo and Bobby McFerrin's Don't Worry, Be Happy. "if it sounded like an adult beverage with an umbrella sticking out of it, they were all in."

**The Brill Building Song Factory** : The Brill Building is a landmark in Midtown Manhattan. In the '50s and '60s it was "quite literally a song factory," where songwriters, often in pairs, worked in small rooms with upright pianos. Its name came to stand for an era of polished professional songwriter pop. "The name Brill Building came to stand in for an entire era of polished professional songwriter pop."

**Red Red Wine's Multiple Lives** : The song went from a No. 62 hit to a No. 34 hit to a No. 1, enduring from the late '60s to the late '80s. It reached No. 1 five years after UB40 recorded it and two decades after it was written. "It's about a No. 1 hit that was originally a No. 62 hit, and then years later, a No. 34 hit."

**AllMusic Critic David Jeffries on Red Red Wine** : Critic David Jeffries of AllMusic is cited as having written about UB40's transformation of the song; the quote itself is missing from the transcript. "David Jeffries, a critic for the music website All Music, later wrote that quote."
