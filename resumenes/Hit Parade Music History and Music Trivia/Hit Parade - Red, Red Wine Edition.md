## albums
**Labor of Love (1983)** : This album was an affectionate homage to UB40's influences, consisting entirely of covers of ten songs the group grew up with by reggae artists like Jimmy Cliff, The Wailers, Boy Friday, and Winston Groovy. It went gold in America by the summer of 1986, three years after its release, signifying half a million US sales, despite never climbing higher than number 39 on the Billboard album chart. In 1988, following the unexpected success of "Red Red Wine," newly pressed copies of the album on CD and cassette were released, and it reached a new 1988 peak of number eight on the Billboard album chart and ultimately went platinum. "Labor of Love, as its title suggests, was an affectionate homage to UB40's influences."
**Labor of Love 2 (1989)** : This album followed the success of "Red Red Wine" and featured remakes of big, unmistakable pop classics rather than hardcore reggae deep cuts. It spun off multiple hits, including covers of The Temptations' "The Way You Do The Things You Do," Al Green's "Here I Am (Come and Take Me)," and Elvis Presley's "Can't Help Falling in Love." "They came back in late 1989 with the album Labor of Love 2, of course."

## songs
**Food for Thought (1980)** : This was UB40's lead single from their first wave of hits, reaching number four on the British charts. It was a diatribe against World Hunger and one of their early original compositions. "leading with their number four single, Food for Thought, a diatribe against World Hunger."
**I Think It's Going to Rain Today (Early 1980s)** : This was a B-side hit for UB40, a cover of a classic Randy Newman song, notable as one of their occasional remakes in their early career. "Notably, their B-side hit, I think it's going to rain today, a cover of a classic, Randy Newman song."
**Red Red Wine (1983, re-released 1988)** : Originally recorded for their "Labor of Love" album, this song was UB40's version of a Tony Tribe track, which they didn't realize was a Neil Diamond original. They slowed the tempo to a "swaying lover's rock" and added a toast-style rap break by Astro. It became their first UK number one in September 1983. In America, it initially peaked at number 34 in March 1984. In 1988, a Phoenix radio programmer, Guy Zapoleon, rediscovered it, leading to a viral spread on US radio. A&M Records re-released the single on cassette (Cassignle) and CD, and it re-entered the Hot 100 on August 13, 1988, ultimately reaching number one for the week ending October 15, 1988. It spent a dozen weeks in the top 40 during its second run. "Red Red Wine was a true trans-lantic hit. It was UB40's first No. 1 both in America and in England, their home country."
**I Got You Babe (1985)** : A cover of the Sonny & Cher classic, recorded with Chrissie Hynde of The Pretenders. It became UB40's second number one hit in the UK in the summer of 1985. In America, it reached number 28 on the Hot 100 the same summer. "In 1985, they recorded a remake of the sunny and share classic, I got UBAB, with their mentor, Chrissy Hind of the Pretenders."
**Breakfast in Bed (1988)** : This was a cover of an old Dusty Springfield single from 1968, recorded with Chrissie Hynde, and was UB40's current single being promoted in the US in the summer of 1988 when "Red Red Wine" was rediscovered. It reached number 6 in the UK, but never made the Hot 100 in the US due to the "Red Red Wine" phenomenon. "This time, they were remaking an old Dusty Springfield single from 1968, and once again, they teamed up with their old pal, Chrissy Hind."
**The Way You Do The Things You Do (1990)** : A cover of The Temptations' classic, released from "Labor of Love 2." It became a number seven hit in the US top 10. "UB40 returned to the US top 10 with their cover of the temptations the way you do the things you do. A number seven hit."
**Here I Am (Come and Take Me) (1991)** : UB40's take on Al Green's song, from the "Labor of Love 2" album. It performed even better than their previous cover, reaching number six in early 1991. "From the same album, they did even better with their take on Al Green's Here I Am, Common Take Me, a number six hit in early 1991."
**Can't Help Falling in Love (1993)** : A cover of Elvis Presley's iconic song, which became the biggest hit of UB40's career. It spent seven weeks on top of the Hot 100 and was the third biggest hit of 1993. This cover charted higher than Elvis's original single. "And two years after that, UB40 had the biggest hit of their career when their cover of Elvis Presley's Can't Help Falling in Love spent seven weeks on top of the hot 100."

## curiosities
**Band Formation and Name (1978)** : The group formed in late 1978 in Birmingham, a multicultural British city with a large Jamaican diaspora, and consisted of eight members, half white and half black. They named themselves after the government paperwork (Unemployment Benefit, Form 40) for those living on unemployment benefits. "UB40 formed in late 1978, a little less than a decade after Tony Tribe hit single."
**Breakthrough and UK Success (Late 1970s - Early 1980s)** : UB40 got their break when Chrissie Hynde of The Pretenders saw them playing in a pub and offered them an opening slot. They quickly found success in England, catching the wave of two-tone ska and other new wave/reggae blends, scoring three straight UK top 10 singles by the end of 1980 and eight straight UK top 40 singles in their first three years. "UB40 got their break when Chrissy Hind saw them playing in a pub and gave them a slot as an opener for her band, the pretenders."
**US Chart Struggle and Album Sales (1984-1986)** : Despite their UK success, UB40 initially had a spotty history on the US charts. While "Red Red Wine" peaked at number 34 and "I Got You Babe" at number 28, they weren't setting the colonies on fire. However, their "Labor of Love" album quietly sold well, going gold in America by 1986 despite a relatively low album chart peak. "American pop fans don't listen to much reggae to begin with."
**"Red Red Wine" Rediscovery and Chart Topping (1988)** : UB40's US label, A&M Records, was promoting their new single "Breakfast in Bed" in 1988 when Guy Zapoleon, a program director for KZZP FM in Phoenix, rediscovered "Red Red Wine" from the band's catalog. Zapoleon decided to put the five-year-old track into rotation on his station's "Party Patrol" show, leading to an overwhelming number of requests. The song went viral across other US pop stations, effectively killing the momentum of "Breakfast in Bed," and forcing A&M to switch its promotional focus to the older track. "And that's when everything changed for UB40, thanks entirely to one of those programmers."
**Benefit from "Would Have Been, Should Have Been" Fad (Late 1980s)** : UB40 was the band that benefited most from the "would have been, should have been" fad initiated by Guy Zapoleon. Their career trajectory, which had seen them become a reggae pop covers band with moderate success, dramatically shifted after "Red Red Wine" topped the charts. This led to a subsequent album of covers, "Labor of Love 2," which brought them their biggest career hits. "But the act that benefited most from the would have been should have been FAD was the band that started at all in 1988, UB40."

**Girl You'll Be a Woman Soon (1967)** : A brooding, majestic single released in 1967 that reached number 10. It marked his last top 10 hit for over two years, preceding a period where his singles missed the top 40. "Girl You'll Be a Woman Soon was a number 10 hit in the spring of 67."
**Red Red Wine (1967)** : Written and first recorded by Neil Diamond in 1967 for his album "Just for You." His original version was a "morose with retful saloon ballad" about drinking away memories of a lost love, peaking at number 62 on the Hot 100 in the spring of 1968 with only a three-week chart run. The production, with strings and a gently plucked guitar, was considered "overproduced" and neither "fish nor fowl" for a slow dance. "Red, Red, Wine was among the worst performers in this period, peaking on the Hot 100 at number 62 in the spring of 1968 in just a three-week chart run."
**Sweet Caroline (1969)** : A top five smash hit that marked Neil Diamond's return to success in the summer of 1969, following a period where his singles had missed the top 40. It is now considered one of his chest-thumping sing-along anthems. "Diamond, who, by the way, righted the ship by the summer of 69, with his top five smash, Sweet Caroline."
**America (Unknown Year)** : One of Neil Diamond's chest-thumping sing-along anthems, mentioned as characteristic of his later work. "We now think of Diamond as a purveyor of chest thumping sing-along anthems like Sweet Caroline or America."

## curiosities
**Early Career and Brill Building Connection (1960s)** : Neil Diamond began his career breaking through as a writer at the Brill Building in Midtown Manhattan. Unlike peers like Carole King, he sought success as a front-line artist early on, scoring hits both as a writer and performer in the mid-1960s. "Neil Diamond broke through as a writer at the Brill Building."
**Rock and Roll Hall of Famer and Blue-Jeans Bard (Unknown Year)** : Recognized as a significant figure in music history, being a Rock and Roll Hall of Famer. He is also described as a "blue-jeans bard" and someone who "loves a hot August night." "That friend is Neil Diamond, rock and roll, fall of famer, blue-gene bard, a man who loves a hot August night, and once and probably called himself the jazz singer."
**"Jazz Singer" Self-Description (Unknown Year)** : Diamond once referred to himself as "the jazz singer," hinting at a theatrical or performative aspect of his persona. "and once and probably called himself the jazz singer."
**Hitmaker of the 1970s (1970s)** : According to Billboard chart-history and Joel Whitburn, Neil Diamond emerged as one of the decade's ten biggest hitmakers in the 1970s, establishing himself as a perennial Hot 100 chart-topper. "by the 1970s, Diamond emerged as a perennial hot 100 chart-topper, and he became, according to Billboard chart-history and Joel Whitburn, one of the decade's ten biggest hitmakers."
**Reaction to UB40's "Red Red Wine" (Later Years)** : Neil Diamond is reportedly charmed by UB40's transformed version of "Red Red Wine." Beyond the financial benefit of songwriting royalties for a previously minor single, he regards their rendition as a transformed work. When performing it live, he often adopts the rocksteady tempo of Tony Tribe and UB40, and even performs Astro's toast-style rap break. "It's pretty clear he's charmed by it. Financially, it's all upside for him."

# genre - Rocksteady

## curiosities
**Bridge Genre (Mid to Late 1960s)** : Rocksteady was the predominant Jamaican music of the mid to late 1960s, serving as a bridge between the more frenetic, up-tempo Ska of the early 60s and the smokeier, groovier, more "woke," and ultimately more durable Reggae genre. It is characterized by a steady, gentle, mid-tempo pulse. "Rocksteady was a bridge between the early 60s, more frenetic, up-tempo genre, SCA, and the smokeier, gruvier, more woke, and ultimately more durable genre, Reggae."

# genre - Reggae

## curiosities
**Covers Tradition (All Time)** : The Reggae genre, in all its permutations, is famous for covers, with the entire genre built on Jamaican sound systems sharing and reimagining beats and songs. Jamaican artists often reconfigure the very structure of Anglo-pop songs from the ground up when covering them. "Reggae in all its permutations is famous for covers. Indeed, the whole genre is built on Jamaican sound systems, sharing and reimagining beats and songs."
**US vs. UK Reception (1960s-1980s)** : Americans have a spotty history with Reggae, often treating it as a "trendlet or a fad," with small flurries of Reggae and Dancehall tracks hitting the charts in waves before receding. In contrast, in England, Reggae has been a regular part of the pop stew since the 1960s, with Jamaica-derived beats routinely charting. "Americans have a spotty history with Reggae. In England, Reggae has been a regular part of the pop stew since the 60s."
**Early 1970s US Boom (Early 1970s)** : The early 1970s saw a small boom of light Reggae singles topping the US Hot 100, exemplified by Johnny Nash's "I Can See Clearly Now" and Eric Clapton's cover of Bob Marley's "I Shot The Sheriff." However, reggae more or less receded on the US pop charts during the late 70s disco years. "In the early 70s, for example, we saw a small boom of light Reggae singles topping our hot 100, like Johnny Nash's classic, I Can See Clearly Now."
**Early 1980s US Boom (Early 1980s)** : The early 1980s experienced another small boom time for the Jamaican sound on the US charts, with various forms adapted by British new romantic acts like Culture Club, turned into reggae funk hybrids by artists like Eddy Grant, or delivered as straight-up traditional reggae by groups like Musical Youth. "The early 80s was another small boom time for the Jamaican sound on the US charts."

**Swaying Style (1980s)** : Lover's rock is a type of reggae characterized by a slower, swaying tempo, exemplified by UB40's version of "Red Red Wine" when they slowed it down from the rocksteady tempo. "turning it from rock steady into a kind of swaying lover's rock."

# label - A&M Records

## curiosities
**Persistence in US Market (1980s)** : A&M Records, UB40's US label, consistently tried to break the band in America by releasing singles and promoting them to radio programmers, even after several albums had not achieved significant chart success. "UB40's US label, ANM Records, kept trying to break the band here, continuing to release singles and promoting them to American radio programmers, the men who picked the hits their stations will play."
**Initial Hesitation on "Red Red Wine" Re-release (1988)** : When Phoenix radio programmer Guy Zapoleon requested a re-release of UB40's "Red Red Wine" due to overwhelming listener demand, A&M initially balked. This was because they had a current UB40 album and single ("Breakfast in Bed") to promote, and such re-releases were not how the industry typically worked. "At first, ANM balked. For one thing, they had a current UB40 album and single to promote."
**Switching Promotional Focus (1988)** : As "Red Red Wine" went viral on US radio, essentially killing the momentum of UB40's current single, A&M Records eventually switched its promotional focus to the nearly five-year-old track. They likely decided to make money either way, even if the new record was a dud. "By late summer, ANM switched its promotional focus from UB40's current record to Red Red Wine."

# label - Capitol Records

## curiosities
**Reluctance to Reissue Old Hits (1988)** : Capitol Records, the label for the defunct band Sheriff, reluctantly reissued their old hit "When I'm With You" in 1988, inspired by UB40's success with "Red Red Wine." The label brass expressed annoyance, viewing it as a "waste of time" to expose old records by a disbanded group with "nothing to build" for the future. "Capital records, Sheriffs label, played along, reissuing the single in the fall of 88."

# curiosity

**Brill Building - Song Factory (1950s-1960s)** : The Brill Building, an actual landmark building in Midtown Manhattan, served as a "song factory" in the 1950s and 60s. Songwriters, often in pairs, would craft some of the greatest songs of rock and roll's first wave in small rooms with upright pianos. The name came to represent an entire era of polished, professional songwriter pop. "That's the actual landmark building in Midtown Manhattan, that in the 50s and 60s, doubled as quite literally a song factory."
**Toasting - Jamaican Precursor to Hip Hop (1960s)** : Toasting is a traditional Afro-Caribbean form of rap that emerged in the 1960s and served as the Jamaican precursor to what later became hip hop. UB40 member Astro's toasting on "Red Red Wine" was notable for a hit single in the early 80s, when rap bridges were just starting to appear on Anglo pop records. "When group member Terence Wilson, aka Astro, did some toasting. The traditional Afro Caribbean form of rap that in the 60s was the Jamaican precursor to what became hip hop."
**US Billboard Hot 100 Reggae Toppers (Last Half-Century)** : "Red Red Wine" by UB40 is one of roughly a dozen songs that have topped the Billboard Hot 100 over the last half-century that feature a reggae rhythm. This highlights the relatively rare occurrence of reggae-infused songs reaching the pinnacle of the US pop charts. "It's one of roughly a dozen songs that have topped the Billboard Hot 100 over the last half century that's sport a reggae rhythm."
**Radio Programmer "Would Have Been, Should Have Been" Strategy (1988)** : Guy Zapoleon, a radio legend and program director for KZZP FM in Phoenix, Arizona, employed a strategy he called "would have been, should have been." If current music wasn't up to snuff, he would "dig into a pile of songs that I thought should have been a hit." This strategy led to the rediscovery and eventual number one success of UB40's "Red Red Wine." "One of the things I've always used throughout my career, Zepolian told Fred Bronson in the Billboard Book of Number One hit, is what I call would have been should have been."
**Late 1980s Pop Music Landscape (Late 1980s)** : By the late 1980s, Top 40 radio had largely seen New Wave and Reggae fall out of favor. The charts were dominated by "diva pop" (Whitney Houston, Madonna) and hard rock/hair metal (Bon Jovi, Def Leppard), alongside the rise of "harder R&B" like New Jack Swing and early pop crossover rap hits, which introduced pop radio to the hip-hop sound. "By the late 80s, New Wave and Reggae had largely fallen out of favor at Top 40 Radio."
**"Cocktail" Movie Influence on Music Trends (1988)** : The 1988 movie "Cocktail," starring Tom Cruise, spawned two number one hits ("Kokomo" by The Beach Boys and "Don't Worry, Be Happy" by Bobby McFerrin) that had a vaguely "island flavor." This suggested that while Americans might not have been interested in pure reggae, they were receptive to music that sounded like an "adult beverage with an umbrella sticking out of it." "On the other hand, the late 80s also saw the rise of Harder R&B music, like New Jack's Swing."
**"Would Have Been, Should Have Been" Fad (1988-1989)** : Inspired by Guy Zapoleon's success with "Red Red Wine," a "programmer mutiny" occurred where radio programmers began "raiding their vaults for old records they thought deserved a second chance." This phenomenon led to the Hot 100 being "awash in three, five, and even nine year old singles" by mid-1989. "The programmer Mutiny was on. Guy's Opolians would have been, should have been, experiment had become a phenomenon."
**Music Industry Attempt to Co-opt the Fad (Late 1980s - Early 1990s)** : In typical fashion, the music industry tried to co-opt the "would have been, should have been" fad by sending bands back into the studio to re-record their old hits to make them sound more current. While this worked for some (e.g., Real Life's "Send Me an Angel"), it generally didn't for others (e.g., Modern English's "I Melt With You"), and the fad itself was spent by 1990. "In typical fashion, the music industry tried to co-op the FAD, actually sending bands back into the studio to re-record their old hits to make them more current sounding."
**Long-Term Fortunes of Re-charted Songs (1989 Onwards)** : Even if a re-released song didn't chart higher the second time (e.g., Benny Mardones' "Into the Night" or Peter Gabriel's "In Your Eyes"), merely having a second chart run often improved the song's long-term fortunes. It placed these songs into permanent oldies or adult contemporary radio rotation. "Just charting a second time put these songs into permanent oldies rotation."
**Cassignle Invention and Timing (1983-1988)** : The "cassinglle" (cassette single) had not been invented in 1983 and 1984, meaning singles like the original "Red Red Wine" were only issued on 45 RPM vinyl. The 1988 re-release of "Red Red Wine" was the first time the single could be bought on tape. "By the way, a funny aside, in 1983 and 1984, the so-called Cassingle hadn't been invented yet."
