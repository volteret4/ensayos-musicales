## songs
**Rio (1977) - Narrative Promotional Video** : In the summer of 1977, the 35-year-old Nesmith filmed a promotional video for his latest single "Rio" on a rocky bluff overlooking the Pacific Ocean on a Los Angeles beach. He sat in a deck chair beside three women who gave him flirty looks while sipping cocktails, all staged for the camera and film crew. He put thousands of dollars of his own money into the project, hoping it would push "Rio" up the charts, but the song didn't do as well as he hoped. Unlike the lip-synced sound-stage clips common in the 1960s, Nesmith gave "Rio" a narrative, using images to complement the song and innovative editing to add color and excitement, and when it aired on television music shows it immediately stood out. "with Rio, Mike Nezmeth deliberately tried something different."

## curiosities
**Struggling Solo Career After The Monkees** : After his heyday as a member of the 1960s boy band The Monkees, Nesmith struggled to find consistent success as a solo artist. He experimented with many ways to promote his music. "he has struggled to find consistent success in his solo career."

**Tie-In Novel for an Album** : Among his promotional experiments, Nesmith once released a tie-in novel to accompany one of his albums. "At one point he even released a tie-in novel to accompany one of his albums."

**Collapsing Umbrella and Mocking Crew on the Rio Shoot** : During the "Rio" shoot, a large beach umbrella behind him collapsed in the wind and ruined the tape, forcing a pause. While Nesmith waited at the craft services table, he overheard two crew members snickering about the "absurd project." They scooted away when they realized he could hear them; he simply poured himself a coffee and smiled, convinced there was a market for narrative-driven music videos. "this time he'll be sure to have the last laugh."

**Not the First Music Video** : "Rio" was not the first music video. In the 1960s, bands appeared on live TV shows like American Bandstand in the US and Top of the Pops in the UK. As the bigger acts became harder to fit around touring schedules, many recorded themselves lip-syncing on sound stages instead, and those clips had little artistry. "Mike didn't make the first ever music video with Rio."

**Pitching Pop Clips to John Lack at Warner (1979)** : In the summer of 1979, the 37-year-old Nesmith visited Warner executive John Lack in New York City with a VHS tape. It played the "Rio" video, then a video by a British band, then video after video, while Lack stared transfixed and nodded. Nesmith was pitching "Pop Clips," a program showcasing artists from around the world through their music videos, introduced by "video jockeys" the way disc jockeys present music on radio. Every network he had met had turned him down until then, but Lack bought the show on the spot for Warner's new children's channel, Nickelodeon. "He agrees to buy Pop Clips on the spot."

**Producing Pop Clips for Nickelodeon (1980)** : Nesmith had to create a new episode every week and sourced most of the videos from the UK and Europe. The show began broadcasting in late 1980. Warner executives soon worried about adult-oriented content in some videos, including scantily clad dancers, heavy rock, and drug references, none of which fit a children's channel. "tastes and standards differ on the other side of the Atlantic."

**Split with John Lack Over the 24-Hour Channel** : Lack met Nesmith to discuss a 24-hour channel devoted exclusively to music, and Nesmith started work right away. Their visions then diverged. Lack wanted traditional, established DJs to introduce videos, while Nesmith pushed for new video jockeys and edgy stand-up comedians. These creative differences became insurmountable and the two parted ways. "Mike's artistic sensibilities just don't fit with John's commercial ambitions."

**Indirect Father of MTV** : Nesmith's idea of narrative music videos eventually led to the creation of MTV, which began broadcasting on August 1, 1981. His "Rio" video was even included in the VHS tape John Lack used to pitch the network in January 1981. "Mike's groundbreaking idea will eventually lead to the creation of the MTV Network."

## curiosities
**Nickelodeon and Pop Clips** : Warner executive John Lack bought Mike Nesmith's Pop Clips in 1979 for Warner's new children's channel, Nickelodeon. It became a modest hit, but on a children's channel it missed the most desirable audience: teenagers. "its home on a children's channel means it has missed the most desirable audience of teenagers."

**Warner–American Express Merger and the MTV Approval** : In January 1981 at Rockefeller Plaza, a recent merger meant the 35-year-old John Lack needed approval from both Warner head Steve Ross and American Express CEO Jim Robinson. Robinson backed the idea first. Ross admitted he had been skeptical until his teenage daughter told him he'd be mad to pass on it. "this new channel would be the hottest thing that ever happened to TV."

**Spare Satellite Transponder** : Lack's pitch focused on costs. Record labels would supply videos for free to promote their artists, just as radio stations don't produce the songs they play. Warner Communications also had an unused satellite transponder, so the channel could launch cheaply. Approval required launching within six months, both to beat competitors in the booming cable market and so the transponder would start paying for itself. "Warner walks that spare transponder to start paying for itself as soon as possible."

# genre - Disco

## curiosities
**Disco Bust and the Industry Slump (1980–81)** : In the six months before January 1981, the disco boom collapsed, record sales fell for the first time in years, and TV music shows dropped in the ratings. This stagnation made record executives reluctant to invest in new ideas, though music video would ultimately save them. "The disco boom has gone bust, and record companies have seen a fall in sales for the first time in years."

# curiosity

**Bob Pittman Joins MTV** : John Lack recruited 27-year-old Bob Pittman, seen by many as a music industry prodigy. Pittman proposed dropping hour and half-hour slots in favour of non-stop music videos, with only a concert film or documentary every Saturday night. It was a risky bet without must-see shows to anchor viewing, but Lack agreed. "all music all the time."

**Naming MTV** : "TV1" was rejected as too generic, and "TVM" (M for music) was a strong candidate. Then a young Warner programmer suggested "MTV," music television, and it stuck. "a young programmer at Warner offers an alternative, MTV, music television."

**Launch Night Mistake** : On launch night, videos aired in the wrong order, so the new hosts introduced the wrong songs. Only a few thousand people were watching, so the error had little impact. Over the following months, the channel built a small but devoted audience, especially among teenagers. "the hosts are talking about the wrong songs."

**"I Want My MTV" Campaign (March 1, 1982)** : The "I Want My MTV" ad campaign featured Madonna, Mick Jagger, and David Bowie. Their combined star power pushed MTV into the national consciousness and made the music video the industry's most important promotional tool. "a new ad campaign for the channel changes everything."

**MTV's Later Shift to Reality TV** : Over later decades, the rise of the internet and media fragmentation led MTV to move away from music videos toward reality TV. By then, John Lack and Bob Pittman had left to form their own media companies. "the men who brought music videos to the masses and changed an industry forever."
