## albums
**Tie-in Novel Promotion** : Mike Nesmith once released a tie-in novel to accompany one of his albums, demonstrating his early and varied efforts to promote his music beyond traditional methods. This venture was part of his experimentation with different promotional strategies in his solo career, which included investing in music videos. "At one point he even released a tie-in novel to accompany one of his albums."

## songs
**Rio (1977)** : In the summer of 1977, Mike Nesmith filmed a promotional video for this single on a beach in Los Angeles, California. He invested thousands of his own money into the project, convinced there was a market for narrative-driven videos within the music industry. The video deliberately incorporated a narrative, used images to complement the song, and featured innovative editing, making it stand out when broadcast on television music shows. Although the song itself did not achieve the chart success he hoped for, this pioneering video was a crucial step towards a new way of enjoying music and eventually led to the creation of the MTV Network. "The song won't do as well as he hoped, but this is just the beginning of a new way of enjoying music."

## curiosities
**Early Video Pioneer** : In the summer of 1977, at 35 years old, Mike Nesmith was actively creating a promotional video for his single "Rio" in Los Angeles, despite encountering snickering and mockery from crew members who deemed it an "absurd project." Nesmith, undeterred, was convinced of a market for narrative-driven videos in the music industry and invested thousands of his own dollars into this vision, which he believed would eventually lead to the creation of the MTV Network. "Despite the doubts of others, Mike Nezmeth is convinced there is a market for narrative-driven videos within the music industry."
**Pop Clips Program Concept** : In the summer of 1979, then 37 years old, Mike Nesmith pitched his program idea, "Pop Clips," to Warner Executive John Lack in New York City. The concept was to showcase artists from around the world through their music videos, with the intention that these clips would be introduced by "video jokies" (VJs), mirroring how disc jockeys play music on the radio. Nesmith had been turned down by every other network before John Lack agreed to buy the program for Nickelodeon. "The idea is to showcase artists from around the world via their music videos."
**Creative Differences with John Lack** : While working with John Lack on developing a 24-hour music channel, significant creative differences emerged, particularly regarding the choice of channel anchors. John Lack preferred traditional DJs to introduce the music videos, envisioning established voices that audiences would know and trust. In contrast, Mike Nesmith advocated for a more experimental approach, pushing for new video jokies and edgy stand-up comedians. These disagreements ultimately proved insurmountable, leading the two men to part ways as their artistic sensibilities and commercial ambitions diverged. "It's increasingly obvious that Mike's artistic sensibilities just don't fit with John's commercial ambitions, so the two agree to part ways."

# label - Warner Records

## curiosities
**Initial Skepticism for MTV** : In January 1981, Steve Ross, the head of Warner, initially expressed skepticism regarding John Lack's ambitious proposal for a 24-hour music video network. His approval was critical due to a recent merger between Warner and American Express. Ross only granted the "green light" after his teenage daughter enthusiastically endorsed the idea, famously telling him he'd be "mad to pass on it" and that this new channel would be "the hottest thing that ever happened to TV." "Still Warner's Steve Ross is not saying much, but seems like a bad sign."
**Resource Contribution to MTV Launch** : Warner Communications played a crucial role in enabling the low-cost launch of MTV. John Lack highlighted that Warner Communications possessed a spare satellite transponder that was not being utilized at the time, which allowed the music channel to be launched efficiently using existing technology and resources. This availability was a key factor in convincing executives to approve the network, as it meant the transponder could start generating revenue as soon as possible. "As an added benefit, John knows Warner Communications has a spare satellite transponder currently not being used."

# curiosity

**Early Music Video Production (Pre-MTV)** : Before the advent of MTV, music performances on television in the 1960s, such as on American Bandstand or Top of the Pops, often featured bands lip-syncing to their latest singles on sound stages. This practice became common as the increasing popularity and demanding touring schedules of major acts made live TV appearances difficult to arrange, though these early videos largely lacked narrative or artistic depth. "But the more popular some of these bands and singers became the harder it was to fit in TV appearances around their touring schedules."
**Disco Boom and Bust (Late 1970s/Early 1980s)** : The music industry faced significant challenges in the six months leading up to January 1981, primarily due to the "disco boom" having gone bust. This decline resulted in a noticeable fall in record sales for the first time in years, making record company executives hesitant to invest in new, untested ideas like a 24-hour music video channel. "The disco boom has gone bust, and record companies have seen a fall in sales for the first time in years."
**Pop Clips Program (Late 1980)** : "Pop Clips" was a television program conceptualized by Mike Nesmith to globally showcase artists through their music videos, intended to be introduced by "video jokies" (VJs). After John Lack purchased the idea for Warner's children's channel, Nickelodeon, the show began broadcasting in late 1980. However, Warner executives soon grew concerned about the "adult-oriented content" — including scantily clad dancers, heavy rock, and drug references — found in some of the European music videos Nesmith sourced. "The show begins broadcasting in late 1980."
**MTV Launch (August 1, 1981)** : MTV commenced broadcasting at midnight on August 1, 1981, from Fort Lee, New Jersey. The channel's debut featured John Lack's voice declaring, "ladies and gentlemen, rock and roll," overlaid on an image of a rocket taking off and footage of Neil Armstrong planting a flag on the moon, with the Stars and Stripes replaced by the letters "MTV." The very first music video shown was "Video Killed the Radio Star" by The Buggles. "It's approaching midnight on August 1, 1981 in Fort Lee, New Jersey, less than a year after John Lack got approval for his pioneering music channel."
**Initial MTV Launch Glitches (1981)** : During its inaugural broadcast at midnight on August 1, 1981, MTV experienced an early technical error where the newly introduced hosts discussed the wrong songs, a consequence of the music videos airing out of their intended order. Despite the frustration of executive Bob Pittman, this mistake had a minimal impact as only a few thousand people were watching the channel's initial launch. "After airing in the wrong order, the hosts are talking about the wrong songs."
**"I Want My MTV" Ad Campaign (March 1, 1982)** : Launched on March 1, 1982, this highly effective ad campaign featured some of the biggest names in pop music, including Madonna, Mick Jagger, and David Bowie. The combined star power of these artists propelled MTV into the national consciousness, fundamentally altering the music industry by establishing the music video as its most critical promotional tool. "The I Want My MTV promo features some of the biggest names in pop, including Madonna, Nick Jagger, and David Bowie."
**MTV's Pioneering Programming Philosophy** : Bob Pittman, recognized as a prodigy in the music industry, proposed a bold and unconventional programming strategy for MTV. Eschewing the traditional hourly and half-hourly program slots common on most channels, he advocated for "non-stop music videos," with the only exception being a concert film or documentary scheduled for every Saturday night. This "daring gambit" was rooted in the belief that "rock and roll is built on doing things differently" and was ultimately adopted by John Lack. "Bob knows that rock and roll is built on doing things differently."
**Evolution of MTV's Content** : Over the decades following its inception, MTV gradually shifted its programming focus away from music videos. This change was largely driven by the rise of the internet and the increasing fragmentation of the media landscape, leading the channel to concentrate instead on reality TV shows, a significant departure from its original mission to broadcast "music television." "Over the decades that follow, the rise of the internet and the fragmentation of the media landscape will see MTV move away from music videos to focus on reality TV shows."
**MTV Naming Process** : During the development phase of the music channel, several names were considered. "TV1" was quickly rejected for being too generic, while "TVM" (where the 'M' stood for music) was a strong contender. Ultimately, a young programmer at Warner suggested "MTV" (Music Television), which was deemed the perfect name and was subsequently adopted for the new network. "TVM, where the M stands for music, is a strong candidate until a young programmer at Warner offers an alternative, MTV, music television."
