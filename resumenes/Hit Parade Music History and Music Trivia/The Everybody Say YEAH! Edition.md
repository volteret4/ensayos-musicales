## albums
**The Jazz Soul of Little Stevie (1962) - Debut Album** : This album contained the original studio version of "Fingertips" as track one. Its title was considered a misnomer as it wasn't entirely jazz or soul, and Motown was not primarily a jazz label. The track "Fingertips" in this version had no vocals and featured Stevie playing bongos. Barry Gordy held back this album for months, and upon its release in the fall of 1962, it failed to chart and did not generate any hit singles. "The very title of this album was Something of a Miss Noma. The album was not entirely jazz and not exactly soul."
**Tribute to Uncle Ray (1962) - Planned Second Album** : This album was a deliberate marketing ploy by Barry Gordy to place young Stevie in the lineage of Ray Charles. Producer Clarence Paul privately called Gordy's idea "cynical." Wonder covered a half-dozen Ray Charles songs for this album. Gordy held it back for months, and when finally released back-to-back with "The Jazz Soul of Little Stevie" in the fall of 1962, it failed to chart and did not generate any hit singles. "Gordy was still so unsure of how to market the 12-year-old Wonder that he held back both albums, Jazz Soul and tribute to Uncle Ray for months."
**Little Stevie Wonder, the 12-Year-Old Genius (1963) - Live Album** : This live album topped the Billboard album chart in August 1963, making Little Stevie the first artist to simultaneously have the top single and album in America. It also became Motown's first-ever number one pop album, scored by an artist who had just turned 13. "making Little Stevie the first artist to have the top single and album in America simultaneously."
**Down to Earth (1966) - Socially Conscious Album** : The album cover sported a photograph of Stevie sitting with his harmonica on a stoop in a dirty, graffiti-painted ghetto. Its lead single, "A Place in the Sun," was socially conscious, despite Barry Gordy's preference for feel-good Motown music. "Later that year, Wonder's album Down to Earth sported on its cover a photograph of Stevie sitting with his harmonica on a stoop in a dirty, graffiti-painted ghetto."
**Signed, Sealed, Delivered (1970) - Transitional Album** : This album featured singles that Wonder worked on during the transition period leading up to his 21st birthday, while still under his old Motown contract. It included his cover of The Beatles' "We Can Work It Out." "He ended the run of singles from the signed, sealed, delivered album with a cover song even bolder than his Dylan cover of five years earlier."
**Where I'm Coming From (1971) - First Self-Produced Album** : Released days before Stevie Wonder's 21st birthday in the spring of 1971, this album was the first produced entirely by himself with no input from the Motown machine. All nine songs on the album were co-written by Wonder and Sairita Wright, including the hit "If You Really Love Me." "Wonder released the album where I'm coming from, the first produced entirely by himself with no input from the Motown machine."
**Music of My Mind (1972) - Radical Departure** : This album was Stevie Wonder's first recorded under his new Motown contract, after turning 21. It marked a radical departure from both the traditional Motown sound and Stevie's prior work, recorded with New York-based electronic music experts Malcolm Cecil and Robert Marguleth. Drenched in clavinet and synthesizers, it was built from keyboard improvisations that Wonder and his engineering team transformed into songs. Rolling Stone magazine lavished it with praise, noting Wonder's determination to fully cross over to both white and black rock and soul audiences. "music of my mind would be a radical departure from both the Motown sound and Stevie's own prior work."
**Talking Book (1972) - Groundbreaking Success** : This album was the follow-up to "Music of My Mind" and the first recorded under Stevie Wonder's new Motown contract. It notably included a message from Wonder in Braille on its cover. The album reached number three on the album chart by February 1973, marking Wonder's first top ten album since "The 12-Year-Old Genius." It ultimately spent more than 100 weeks on the Billboard album chart, becoming the longest run of any Wonder studio album, and even its deep cuts like "You and I" became radio perennials. "Talking Book, which included a message from Wonder in Braille on the cover, reached number three on the album chart by February."
**Innervisions (1973) - Critical Masterpiece** : Released in August 1973, days before Stevie Wonder was involved in a severe car crash. Though it is the only one of Wonder's "Imperial Phase" albums not to generate a number one pop hit, critics often rank it as Stevie's greatest single LP. Besides the number four hit "Higher Ground" and the seven-and-a-half-minute masterwork "Living for the City," the album's final track, "He's Mr. Noidle," was an evisceration of President Nixon. "Though it is the only one of Wonder's Imperial Phase albums not to generate a number one pop hit, Intervisions is often ranked by critics as Stevie's greatest single LP."
**Fulfillingness' First Finale (1974) - Direct Political Commentary** : This album featured Stevie Wonder's decision to be even more direct in his criticism of President Nixon, making the presidential diss his lead-off single, "You Haven't Done Nothing." This marked a continuation of Wonder's "white whale" theme of condemning the divisive president, which had also appeared in "Talking Book" and "Innervisions," albeit more obliquely. "On his next album however, 1974's tongue twisting, fulfilling this first finale, Stevie decided not only to be even more direct but to make the presidential diss his lead off singles."
**[Unnamed Album] (Implicit 1975)** : This album is implicitly one of the three albums Stevie Wonder produced between 1973 and 1976 that won the Grammy for Album of the Year and ranked among the greatest albums of 70s pop. Its existence, following Wonder's near-fatal car crash after the release of "Innervisions," makes the overall achievement of these albums even more remarkable. "For the next four years, 1973 through 1976, Stevie Wonder would produce three albums that would not only all win the Grammy for album of the year, but permanently rank among the greatest albums of 70s pop."

## songs
**Fingertips (Original Studio Version)** : This track was the first on Stevie Wonder's debut album, "The Jazz Soul of Little Stevie." It was named "Fingertips" because of the bongos Stevie was playing, and it featured no vocals. Barry Gordy feared the young Stevie's voice would not be supple enough for a standard Motown R&B or pop record. "This first track was called fingertips because of the bongos Stevie was playing."
**Fingertips Part Two (1963)** : This live recording became Stevie Wonder's only number one pop hit in his entire first decade as a recording artist, spending three weeks at the top. It is now rarely played on the radio, despite being enshrined by the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame as one of its "660 songs that shaped rock and roll." The single, divided from a nearly seven-minute live performance at Chicago's Regal Theatre in June 1962, was promoted to radio stations as Part Two due to Stevie's "protracted, chaotic, delightfully improvised refusal to leave the stage." It debuted on Billboard's Hot 100 the first week of summer 1963, reached the top 10 in a month, and number one three weeks later. It was the first ever live number one hit in Hot 100 history and Motown's second pop number one. "Fingertips was the first ever live number one hit in Hot 100 history."
**Fingertips Part One (1963)** : This was the first half of a nearly seven-minute live recording from Chicago's Regal Theatre in June 1962, which would eventually be released as one side of a 45 RPM single. It featured Stevie Wonder switching from bongos to his beloved harmonica and transforming the studio track for the stage, but did not contain the improvised vocal section that would make Part Two a hit. "Amazingly, everything we've played you so far is not the part of the song that would ultimately become a hit. The first half would eventually see release as Finger Tips Part 1"
**Workout, Stevie Workout (1963)** : This self-referential single was an attempt by Barry Gordy to capture Wonder's effortless stage exuberance in the studio after the success of "Fingertips." It was Stevie's last top 40 hit as "Little Stevie Wonder," stalling at number 33 in November 1963. "This exuberant track, Stevie's last top 40 hit as Little Stevie Wonder, stalled at number 33 in November 1963."
**Hey Harmonica Man (1964)** : This was another self-referential Stevie track, and the first to be promoted as simply "Stevie Wonder," dropping the "Little" moniker. However, the single only managed to reach number 29 in July 1964, less than a year after "Fingertips." "Another self-referential Stevie track, Hey Harmonica Man, was the first promoted as just Stevie Wonder."
**Uptight (Everything's All Right) (1966)** : Co-written with Sylvia Moy and Hank Cosby, this song was pivotal for Stevie Wonder's career, marking the divining line between his "Little Stevie" period and his budding manhood. It featured daring lyrics about a boy from the wrong side of the tracks dating a rich girl who may have been outside his race. The song returned Wonder to the top of the R&B chart and reached number three on the Hot 100 in February 1966, emboldening him at Motown. "Uptight, subtitled, Everything's All Right, was pivotal for Stevie's career."
**Blowin' in the Wind (1966)** : This was a cover of Bob Dylan's challenging, ambiguous folk song, which Stevie Wonder had been performing live for a couple of years. Ironically, in the summer of 1963, "Fingertips" had held Peter Paul and Mary's smash cover of the same Dylan classic at the number two spot. Wonder's own version went to number one on the R&B chart and into the pop top 10, reaching number nine. This represented Wonder's move towards more overtly socially conscious material. "Wonder took his own version to number one on the R&B chart and into the pop top 10, where it reached number nine."
**A Place in the Sun (1966)** : This socially conscious song was the lead single from Stevie Wonder's "Down to Earth" album. It was written by Motown writers Ronald Miller and Brian Wells, not by Stevie himself, reflecting a period where Barry Gordy still preferred feel-good Motown music over protest songs. "Down to Earth's lead single, written not by Stevie, but by Motown writers Ronald Miller and Brian Wells, was the socially conscious, a place in the sun."
**The Tears of a Clown (1967)** : Originally a melody line co-written by Stevie Wonder and Hank Cosby in 1966 but not completed, it was given to Motown legend Smokey Robinson, who added lyrics and recorded it with The Miracles. This immortal song fused Robinson's artful metaphorical lyrics with an adorable, calliope-like melodic hook and a driving baseline that was all Stevie, eventually becoming Smokey and The Miracles' only number one hit together. "The Tears of a Clown, and there's no one around. The immortal, the tears of a clown, fused the artful metaphorical lyrics of Smokey Robinson with adorable, callyope-like melodic hook and a driving baseline that was all stevy."
**I Was Made to Love Her (1967)** : Co-written with Sylvia Moy, this recording was driven by Stevie Wonder's declaiming vocal and his harmonica. By the peak of the Summer of Love, it had reached a lofty number two on the Hot 100, only held back from the top spot by The Doors' number one hit, "Light My Fire." It was a number one hit on the Billboard Rhythm and Blues chart. "I was made to love her, a recording driven by Wonder's declaiming vocal and his harmonica."
**Shoo-Be-Doo-Be-Doo-Da-Day (1968)** : This tongue-twisting song was another hit co-written by Stevie Wonder with Sylvia Moy. It was notable as the first hit to showcase Wonder's playing on the amplified electric keyboard called the Clavinet. The song reached the pop Top 10 and was a number one on the Billboard Rhythm and Blues chart. "The first hit to showcase Wonder's playing on the amplified electric keyboard called The Clavinette."
**For Once in My Life (1968)** : This love song, co-written by Ron Miller, was initially sat on by Barry Gordy for over a year. Early versions had been flops and were closer to easy listening, with Tony Bennett scoring a minor chart hit with his rendition. When Gordy eventually relented on Wonder's more uptempo version, it became a major hit, reaching number two on both the pop and R&B charts, though it was blocked from number one by Marvin Gaye's "I Heard It Through the Grapevine." It was a number one hit on the R&B chart. "When Gordy eventually relented on Wonder's more uptempo version, the song was a major hit, reaching number two on both the pop and R&B charts."
**My Cherie Amour (1969)** : Co-written by Stevie Wonder with Sylvia Moy and Hank Cosby in 1967, this sparkling ballad featured French lyrics inspired by The Beatles' francophone hit "Michelle." Barry Gordy initially didn't think much of it, and it was eventually issued on a single in 1969, at first as a B-side. Despite this, the song marched to number four on both the Hot 100 and the R&B chart. It was a number one hit on the R&B chart. "A sparkling ballad with French lyrics inspired by the Beatles' francophone hit Michelle, when My Shary Amore was finally issued on a single in 1969 and at first as a B-side, the song marched to number four on both the Hot 100 and the R&B chart."
**Yester-Me, Yester-You, Yesterday (1969)** : Motown scored one more top ten hit with this Wonder recording, which they had sat on for two years. By the time it became a number seven hit in the fall of 1969, it already sounded slightly dated, evocative of an earlier era of Motown and a younger version of Stevie. "By the time it became a number seven hit in the fall of 1969, it already sounded slightly dated to an earlier era of Motown and a younger version of Stevie."
**Signed, Sealed, Delivered I'm Yours (1970)** : This "barn burner" marked the first single on which Stevie Wonder took solo credit as the producer. It opened with a sitar, an instrument now a staple in rock thanks to The Beatles but relatively new to R&B, and built on this foundation with a pounding, danceable Motown beat. It became Wonder's biggest hit to date on Billboard's R&B chart, spending six weeks at number one, and reached number three on the Hot 100 by August. "The first single on which Stevie Wonder took solo credit as the producer was a barn burner, 1970s signed seal delivered on yours."
**It's a Shame (1970)** : This insinuating single was written and solely produced by Stevie Wonder for The Spinners, giving the Detroit-based group their biggest hit in a decade. Co-written by Wonder with his then-wife Sairita Wright and friend Lee Garrett, the recording notably included drums, bass, guitar, and keyboards all played by Wonder himself. It reached the R&B top five and the pop top 20 by the fall of 1970. "Stevie Wonder gave the spinners their biggest hit in a decade with its ashamed."
**We Can Work It Out (1971)** : This was a cover song, a remake of The Beatles' 1966 number one hit, which Stevie Wonder boldly reinvented, changing the John Lennon Paul McCartney arrangement top to bottom. It reached number 13 on the pop chart and number three on the R&B chart in the spring of 1971. "A remake of the Beatles 1966 number one hit We Can Work It Out that reinvented the John Lennon Paul McCartney arrangement top to bottom."
**If You Really Love Me (1971)** : This hit single came from Stevie Wonder's album "Where I'm Coming From," released just days before his 21st birthday. Co-written by Wonder and Sairita Wright, it reached both the pop and R&B top 10 by the fall of 1971. "including the hit If You Really Love Me, which reached both the pop and R&B top 10 by the fall of 1971."
**Superwoman (Where Were You When I Needed You) (1972)** : This eight-minute track from "Music of My Mind" was its longest, yet also its most accessible and radio-friendly. It offered a sweet-sounding but lyrically ascetic chronicle of the dissolution of Stevie's short marriage to Cyreta Wright. Spread across both sides of a 45, it reached number 33 on the Hot 100 in the summer of 1972, a remarkable achievement for such a languid, challenging soul ballad. "A sweet sounding but lyrically ascetic chronicle of the dissolution of Stevie's short marriage to Cyreta Wright."
**Superstition (1972)** : This funk rock song was inspired by Stevie Wonder's friendship with guitarist Jeff Beck in 1972, and was originally titled "Very Superstitious." Jeff Beck and Stevie Wonder first demoed it together, with the intention for Jeff Beck's group to release it. However, Barry Gordy and the Motown team insisted Stevie retain it as the first single for his next album. The final recording was virtually all Stevie, featuring his syncopated drumbeat, stabbing funk lines on his clavinet, and a Moog baseline, topped by a searing vocal, with only saxophonist Trevor Lawrence and trumpeter Steve Madyre as guests. The lyrics offered a veiled commentary on the 1972 US presidential election, reflecting Wonder's outrage over the emerging Watergate scandal and American public's confusion. It reached number one on the Hot 100 in January 1973, Stevie's first pop chart-topper in nearly 10 years, and he later performed an electric rendition with Sesame-specific lyrics on the program. "Superstition ain't the way."
**You Are the Sunshine of My Life (1973)** : This song was released as the second single from the "Talking Book" album. It recalled the sweet soul sound of Wonder's late 60s music, now drenched in his rich Fender-Rhodes keyboards. In a supremely confident move, the first four verse lyrics of the song were not even sung by Wonder himself but given to backing vocalists Jim Gilstrap and Lonnie Groves. It became an immediate radio standard, topping the Hot 100 by May 1973, as well as Billboard's Easy Listening Chart. "An immediate radio standard, You Are the Sunshine of My Life topped the Hot 100 by May of 73"
**Higher Ground (1973)** : This track from the "Innervisions" album reached number four on the pop chart. Days after the album's release and Stevie Wonder's car crash in August 1973, it is told, probably apocryphally, that one of his managers recovered Stevie in the hospital by singing the chorus to this very track, amazingly reviving him. "one of his managers recovered Stevie by singing the chorus to his own Intervisions track, Higher Ground, and amazingly revived him."
**Living for the City (1973)** : A seven-and-a-half-minute masterwork from the "Innervisions" album, this song was a fierce diatribe against a corrupt America and its marginalizing of black people, particularly the railroading of young black men into the prison system. It featured a spoken-word urban street scene in the middle and managed to reach the top ten, peaking at number eight in January 1974. Pitchfork later wrote that it "exudes Martin Luther King hope along with Malcolm X." "quote, exudes Martin Luther King hope along with Malcolm X."
**He's Mr. Noidle (1973)** : This seemingly gentle, wafting final track on the "Innervisions" album was, in fact, an evisceration of President Nixon. Its lyrics included phrases like "got a counterfeit dollar in his hand, the coolest one with the biggest mouth," continuing Stevie Wonder's oblique condemnations of the divisive president. "the seemingly gentle, wafting he's Mr. Noidle, was an evisceration of President Nixon."
**You Haven't Done Nothing (1974)** : This strutting funk song was the lead-off single from "Fulfillingness' First Finale," showcasing a confident Stevie Wonder at his peak. It was his most pointed Richard Nixon "beef record" ever, with lyrics unsparing and almost playfully mocking the President as the Watergate scandal reached its crescendo. As a pièce de résistance, Wonder hired The Jackson Five as his backing vocalists, even calling them out on the track. The song landed in July 1974, debuted on the Hot 100 the week ending August 3rd, and became the number one song in America three months later, six days after Richard Millhouse Nixon resigned the presidency. "You haven't done nothing was strutting funk, the sound of a confident Stevie Wonder at his peak."

## curiosities
**Early Life and Blindness (1950)** : Born premature in Saginaw, Michigan, in May 1950, Steveland Hardaway Judkins' blindness was caused by an overabundance of oxygen pumped into his hospital incubator, which ultimately detached his retinas. "Born premature in Saginaw, Michigan in May of 1950, Steveland's blindness was caused by an overabundance of oxygen pumped into his hospital incubator that ultimately detached his retinas."
**Child Prodigy (Pre-1960)** : Positively, young Stevie's reliance upon his ears gave him deep sonic sensitivity and prodigy-level abilities on a range of instruments, including piano, harmonica, and drums, all of which he picked up before the age of 10. "young Stevie's reliance upon his ears gave him deep sonic sensitivity, and prodigy level abilities on a range of instruments, including piano, harmonica, and drums, all of which he picked up before 10."
**Motown Signing (Age 11)** : Barry Gordy signed young Steveland at age 11 to his Tamla label, the first imprint of his Motown recording company, despite being unsure at first of what to do with the prodigious talent he was in awe of. "When Barry Gordy signed Young Steveland at age 11 to his Tamla label, the first imprint of his Motown recording company, he was in awe of the young man's talent, but unsure at first of what to do with him."
**"Second Coming of Brother Ray" Marketing (Early 1960s)** : Early in his career, Motown attempted an unusual marketing experiment, a "shameless marketing ploy," to deliberately place young Stevie in the lineage of Ray Charles, a blind African-American performer with natural instrumental gifts. Producer Clarence Paul privately called Barry Gordy's idea "cynical." "a deliberate attempt to place young Stevie in the lineage of another great artist, one who wasn't even on Motown."
**Showstopper on Motor Town Review (1962)** : In 1962, on Barry Gordy's live, multi-act Motor Town Review tour, Little Stevie was consistently a "showstopper" at every date, including those in the deep south where resistance to visiting black performers was fierce. "And at every date on the tour, Little Stevie was a showstopper."
**Improvised Stage Performance of "Fingertips" (1962)** : During the Motor Town Review, Stevie transformed "Fingertips" for the stage, switching from bongos to his harmonica, improvising vocals, doing call and response with the audience, and making up nearly gibberish lyrics. As a "wittiest trick," he even switched the melody on his harmonica to a snippet of "Mary Had a Little Lamb," extending his set-closing numbers past their official time despite not being supposed to improvise. Amidst the chaos of the band scurrying back to their instruments, bassist Joe Swift yelled "what key, what key." "Little Stevie didn't go quietly. Part one of the nearly seven minute recording sounded like this."
**"Fingertips" Act Debate (1962)** : Motown historians debate whether Stevie Wonder's protracted, improvised refusal to leave the stage during his "Fingertips" performance was an act, similar to James Brown's routine, or truly his irrepressible energy and competitive streak. Biographer Mark Ribowski quotes multiple Motown sources claiming it was truly young Stevie's "irrepressible energy, boundless charm, and competitive streak" in his quest to prove himself the ultimate headliner, a personality Barry Gordy recognized as key to his appeal. "Motown historians debate whether this was all an act."
**Billboard Chart Milestones with "Fingertips" (1963)** : "Fingertips Part Two" and its accompanying live album, "Little Stevie Wonder, the 12-Year-Old Genius," set a string of Billboard chart milestones. Stevie became the first artist to have the top single and album in America simultaneously. "Fingertips" was the first-ever live number one hit in Hot 100 history and Motown's second pop chart-topper. The LP was Motown's first number one pop album, scored by a kid who had just turned 13. "Indeed, Wonder's single and album set a string of Billboard chart milestones."
**Racial Significance of "Fingertips" (1963)** : "Fingertips" was still the number one song in America at the start of the August week when Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. led his March on Washington. Music historians later noted that in one of the most consequential weeks for race relations in American history, the top song was a quintessentially African-American single, a young black man inspiring a largely black crowd to clap along, gospel style, to an improvised melody. "Fingertips was still the number one song in America at the start of the August week when Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. led his March on Washington."
**Post-"Fingertips" Marketing Challenges (1963-1964)** : In the immediate wake of "Fingertips," Barry Gordy had Wonder record equally sassy and cheeky tracks like "Workout, Stevie Workout" and "Hey Harmonica Man" to capture his effortless stage presence in the studio. However, as young Stephen grew and his voice deepened, Motown staffers noted he was sometimes marketed as a "carnival side show attraction" or "circus freak," prized for instrumental abilities but not yet seen as a consummate artist. "Mark Rabowski quotes several Motown staffers noting that while Wonder was signed for his talent, he was at times marketed as a carnival side show attraction, or a quote circus freak."
**Creative Independence and Contract Dispute (1960s-1971)** : Throughout the 1960s, Motown maintained iron control over Stevie Wonder's material and its release. His contract, signed at age 11, paid him and his mother only a weekly stipend, with all royalties held in a trust inaccessible until he turned 21. This bound him to the label but also incentivized him to generate hits. Wonder, however, wanted to control his own art "top to bottom" and be free of the "hit factory," sowing seeds for autonomy even before turning 21. "Clearly, Stevie Wonder was outgrowing his contract, indeed his entire relationship to Motown Records."
**Post-Contract Free Agent Status (1971)** : By May 1971, Stevie Wonder was out of his original Motown contract and immediately became "the most valuable free agent in all of popular music." Between 1963 and 1971, he had generated 21 top 40 hits, including a dozen top 10 pop hits, and half a dozen R&B chart-toppers, demonstrating that Motown not only owed him access to his trust fund but "owed him period." "Stevie Wonder was now out of contract and instantly the most valuable free agent in all of popular music."
**Foundation for the "Greatest Creative Run" (1970s)** : Stevie Wonder's first decade established the foundation for his "epic 1970s," which Slate colleague Jack Hamilton "has rightfully called the greatest creative run in the history of popular music." This period of independence was "won but earned" during his early years. "The foundation for Wonder's epic 1970s, the period my slate colleague Jack Hamilton has rightfully called the greatest creative run in the history of popular music, that foundation was built in Wonder's first decade when he not only won but earned his independence."
**New Motown Contract (Post-21st Birthday)** : After gaining his independence, Stevie Wonder signed a new contract with Motown that guaranteed him a million-dollar advance and a double-digit royalty rate, which was significantly higher than what he or most Motown artists had ever received before. This established his independence and gained the attention of the rock press, including Rolling Stone magazine, which lavished praise on his album "Music of My Mind." "Stevie signed with Motown for a new contract that guaranteed him a million dollar advance and a double-digit royalty rate, much higher than anything Stevie or most Motown artists had ever received before."
**Friendship and Collaboration with Jeff Beck (1972)** : Stevie Wonder befriended guitarist Jeff Beck in 1972 after the former Yardbirds axeman covered one of Stevie's songs on a Jeff Beck Group album. Beck spent several days in the studio during the recording of Wonder's next album, and in thanks for his participation, Stevie pledged to write a funk rock song for Beck to cover, which would become "Superstition." "Stevie Wonder befriended guitarist Jeff Beck in 1972, when the former Yard Bird's Axe Man covered one of Stevie's songs on a Jeff Beck group album."
**Near-Fatal Car Crash and Recovery (1973)** : Days after the August 1973 release of his album "Innervisions," Stevie Wonder was in the front seat of a car in North Carolina that crashed into a truck carrying logs, with one log smashing through the windshield and knocking him unconscious. The story, "probably apocryphal," goes that days later in the hospital, one of his managers recovered Stevie by singing the chorus to his own "Innervisions" track, "Higher Ground," and "amazingly revived him." "Days after the August 1973 release of Intervisions, Wonder was in the front seat of a car in North Carolina that crashed into a truck carrying logs."
**Grammy Album of the Year Streak (1973-1976)** : For the four years between 1973 and 1976, Stevie Wonder produced three albums that not only all won the Grammy for Album of the Year but permanently rank among the greatest albums of 70s pop. The fact that he almost didn't survive the release of the first of these albums and recovered to generate the other two makes their existence all the more remarkable. "For the next four years, 1973 through 1976, Stevie Wonder would produce three albums that would not only all win the Grammy for album of the year, but permanently rank among the greatest albums of 70s pop."
**"White Whale" - Condemnation of President Nixon (1970s)** : During his "imperial run" of albums, President Nixon was "something of a white whale" for Stevie Wonder. Both "Talking Book" and "Innervisions" included tracks where Wonder condemned the divisive President, however obliquely in the case of "Superstition." This culminated in "Fulfillingness' First Finale," where he was even more direct in his diss with "You Haven't Done Nothing." "During this imperial run of albums, Nixon was something of a white whale for Stevie."

**Influence on Stevie Wonder** : Stevie Wonder's song "Sir Duke" is described as an "ode to Big Band pop and the music of Duke Elton", indicating this genre's significant influence on his musical style, particularly in that track. "Stevie's ode to Big Band pop and the music of Duke Elton, Sir Duke, returned him to the top of the charts."

# genre - R&B
## curiosities
**Canon of Standards** : Many non-single songs from Stevie Wonder's 'Songs in the Key of Life', such as "Loves in Need of Love Today", "Knocks Me Off My Feet", and "Pastime Paradise", are considered staples of the R&B canon, demonstrating the album's lasting influence on the genre. "The album contains songs that are now considered radio standards and staples of the R&B canon."

# label - Motown
## curiosities
**Artist Contract Renegotiation (1976)** : In 1976, Motown renegotiated Stevie Wonder's contract, granting him an unprecedented 20% royalty rate and a $13 million advance, reflecting his immense success and influence at the time. "Wonder had the clout to renegotiate his Motown contract again, bumping his royalty rate to a then unprecedented 20% and guaranteeing him a $13 million advance."
**Humorous Production Timeline (1976)** : Due to the extensive, over-year-long production of Stevie Wonder's album 'Songs in the Key of Life', Motown staffers in 1976 humorously expressed the delay by wearing t-shirts emblazoned with "We're Almost Finished". "Motown staffers in 1976 started wearing t-shirts that read, We're Almost Finished."

# concert - 1975 Grammys
## curiosities
**Album of the Year Winner** : At the 1975 Grammys, Stevie Wonder's album 'Fulfilliness' First Finale' was awarded Album of the Year, marking his second consecutive win for this prestigious category. "Fulfilliness First Finale took home album of the year."

# concert - 1976 Grammys
## curiosities
**Album of the Year Winner** : At the 1976 Grammys, Paul Simon's album won Album of the Year, interrupting Stevie Wonder's previous streak, as Wonder was "momentarily ineligible" that year. "Paul Simon interrupted Wonder's album of the year streake at the 1976 Grammys, a year when Wonder was momentarily ineligible."

# concert - 1977 Grammys
## curiosities
**Album of the Year Winner** : At the 1977 Grammys, Stevie Wonder's 'Songs in the Key of Life' was awarded Album of the Year, even though the album was still lodged in the top 10 of the album chart that week. "Stevie Wonder ascended the podium one last time to take home album of the year."

# concert - Grammys tribute to Stevie Wonder (2015)
## curiosities
**Celebration of an Icon** : In 2015, the National Academy of Recording Arts and Sciences organized a televised special, the "Grammys tribute to Stevie Wonder", to honor one of their most decorated artists, featuring performances by contemporary superstars like Farell Williams, Annie Lennox, Ariana Grande, Lady Gaga, and Beyoncé Knowles. "the National Academy of Recording Arts and Sciences paid tribute to one of their most decorated artists with the televised special Grammys tribute to Stevie Wonder."

# instrument - Fender Roads
## curiosities
**Stevie Wonder's Sound Signature** : The "Fender Roads" (likely a transcription error for Fender Rhodes) is identified as the instrument central to Stevie Wonder's distinctive "Fender Roads Funk" sound, as heard in tracks like "Boogie on Reggae One". "yet another flavor of Stevie's Fender Roads Funk."

# curiosity
**Albums Debuting at Number 1 (1970s)** : In the 1970s, albums rarely debuted at number 1 on the Billboard album chart; prior to Elton John's 1975 'Captain Fantastic and the Brown Dirt Cowboy', it was an unheard-of achievement. Stevie Wonder's 'Songs in the Key of Life' in September 1976 became only the third album of the decade to achieve this. "albums simply did not debut at number 1."
**Duke Elton Reference** : The transcript mentions "Duke Elton" in relation to Stevie Wonder's song "Sir Duke," describing it as an "ode to Big Band pop and the music of Duke Elton." This is likely a transcription error for "Duke Ellington," a famed American jazz pianist, composer, and bandleader. "Stevie's ode to Big Band pop and the music of Duke Elton."
**Neilson Music Tracking (1990s)** : Neilson music began tracking plays of terrestrial radio in the 1990s, providing data on song popularity and airplay, and revealing that early hits like Stevie Wonder's "Fingertips" have since fallen out of regular rotation compared to other works. "Since Neilson music began tracking plays of terrestrial radio in the 1990s, fingertips has never been among the 40 most played Stevie Wonder songs."
