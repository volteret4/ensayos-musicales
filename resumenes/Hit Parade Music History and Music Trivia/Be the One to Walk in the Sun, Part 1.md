## albums
**Blue Angel (1980) - Only Album with Blue Angel** : Blue Angel's one and only self-titled album, released on Polydor Records in 1980, showcased a new wave, retro rockabilly sound steeped in kitsch, with Lauper wearing vintage clothes in clashing patterns. Lauper had won her first recording contract with the band at "the fairly advanced age of 26." The record was informed by New York rock, but the band was not punk and never played CBGB. "It was a sound suffused with kitsch."
**She's So Unusual (1983) - Quadruple-Platinum Solo Debut** : Lauper's solo debut on Portrait Records (an Epic subsidiary) was produced by Rick Chertoff of Columbia Records, a sister label to Epic in the CBS family. Chertoff was working at the time on the debut album by Philadelphia band The Hooters, and their members Rob Hyman and Eric Bazilian became the backbone of Lauper's studio band. Chertoff saw Lauper more as a song interpreter than a songwriter, which is why the album ended up half covers; he "would ultimately be proved wrong on that score." The title came from a 1920s ditty by Helen Kane, the singer who inspired Betty Boop, and Lauper included a brief cover of it near the end of the album. Annie Leibovitz shot the cover photo: Lauper dancing at Brooklyn's Coney Island in a red prom dress, barefoot and in fishnet stockings. Released in fall 1983, it climbed the Billboard LP chart steadily through early 1984 and broke into the album top 10 three weeks after "Girls Just Want to Have Fun" peaked in March 1984. It peaked at No. 4 and was certified quadruple platinum by early 1985. It was the first debut LP by any artist to yield four top-five hits. "The LP was expert branding on Cindy's part."
**True Colors (1986) - Make-or-Break Sophomore LP** : Lauper's second studio LP followed a multi-platinum debut, and she was under pressure to show that her earlier success wasn't a fluke. She "brought backup, literally." The Bangles sang background vocals on the lead-off track "Change of Heart," and Aimee Mann of 'Til Tuesday was among the other guest vocalists. It is the one album where Lauper, The Bangles and Aimee Mann all crossed paths. It went platinum. "A lot was riding on Lopper's sophomore album, which was following up a multi-platinum debut."

## songs
**I'm Gonna Be Strong (Blue Angel cover)** : Blue Angel's slow-dance cover of the Gene Pitney song was the only Blue Angel song to chart anywhere in the world, reaching No. 37 in the Netherlands. Lauper's torch songs connected with audiences more than the band's bops did, even though she had a classic Phil Spector girl-group voice. "The only Blue Angel song to break on any chart anywhere in the world."
**Hungry (Blue Angel Demo)** : An early demo by the downtown New York band Blue Angel, recorded while they were still hungry for success. "This demo, appropriately enough, is called Hungry."
**Blue Christmas (Passaic New Year's Gig)** : Lauper gave a tipsy performance of the standard at a New Year's gig with Blue Angel in a dive in Passaic, New Jersey. She had invited new boyfriend Dave Wolff, and the performance did not scare him off. "Her tipsy performance of the standard Blue Christmas didn't scare Wolfe off."
**Money Changes Everything (1983) - Album Opener** : A cover of a song by the cult band The Brains, it opened She's So Unusual. Lauper felt the band was playing it in a dated, Dylanesque style, so she told guitarist Eric Bazilian and producer Rick Chertoff to go listen to The Clash. Bazilian and the band then copied that tough guitar sound on the final version. "Make believe you're playing London Calling, she said."
**When You Were Mine (1983) - Prince Cover** : A cover of what was described as Prince's new-waviest song of his early years. It was an album cut on his 1980 LP Dirty Mind that had only ever been released as a B-side. Lauper transformed it as well. "Finding the ache in Prince's jam."
**Girls Just Want to Have Fun (1983) - Debut Single, No. 2** : Robert Hazard wrote and demoed the song a few years earlier as an ode to girls and their frivolity, written from a male point of view. His version, about a boy warned by his parents to stop hanging around with good-time girls, was "quirky, horny, and kind of sexist." Rick Chertoff was convinced Lauper could turn it into something empowering. Lauper said "the word anthem kept coming up," so she took a more radical approach to the arrangement. She asked Eric Bazilian, who was playing a kind of white reggae, to keep the reggae feel but move toward Motown-esque riffs. She shifted the key to something more female-friendly, inspired by the 50s duo Shirley and Lee, in which Shirley sang in a higher key. Chertoff encouraged the hiccuping sound after the word "fun" to evoke Buddy Holly. He also brought in Ellie Greenwich (co-writer of "Be My Baby" and "Leader of the Pack"), who sang, helped arrange the vocals, and convinced Lauper to make the backing vocals a chant the way the Shangri-Las might sing it. The video co-starred Lauper's mother and an army of multicultural, multiracial friends. Driven by heavy MTV play, the single peaked at No. 2 on the Hot 100 in March 1984. "Lopper added the feminism and kept the fun."
**Time After Time (1984) - First No. 1** : Lauper co-wrote it with Hooters keyboardist Rob Hyman, partly to prove to her label and to Chertoff that she could write. Hyman had the phrase "a suitcase of memories"; the lines "lying in my bed, I hear the clock tick" and "the second hand unwinds" were Lauper's. She built the song to showcase the ballad voice she had used with Blue Angel. She took the title from a TV Guide listing for the 1979 time-travel sci-fi film Time After Time, starring Malcolm McDowell and Mary Steenburgen. Released as the second single, it went to No. 1, and Casey Kasem counted it down. Lauper was irritated that she had been discouraged from writing: "Why did you make me do all this other stuff? I could have been writing more songs all along." It became a standard. Miles Davis played it live as early as 1985, Tuck & Patti recorded a 1988 version popular on quiet storm and smooth jazz stations, and INOJ took it back to No. 6 on the Hot 100 in 1998. "They'd created a new standard."
**She Bop (1984) - Masturbation Song, No. 3** : Co-written with Steve Lunt as an unprecedented song about female masturbation, meant to sail over the heads of young kids. It referenced Blueboy magazine and included lines like "they say I better stop or I'll go blind," but stayed subtle enough to get radio play. It reached No. 3 by September 1984, just as school started. The PMRC still put it on its "Filthy Fifteen" list next to Prince's "Darling Nikki" and Judas Priest's "Eat Me Alive." That angered Lauper, both for the sex negativity and because she had tried to keep it PG. "I had made sure that I never mentioned touching myself so that little kids would never know."
**All Through the Night (1984) - Record Fourth Top-Five Hit** : A remake of a track by singer-songwriter Jules Shear, and Lauper's first single since "Girls Just Want to Have Fun" that was a cover. It peaked at No. 5 the week before Madonna's "Like a Virgin" entered the top 10. Billboard reported that Lauper was the first female artist to pull four top-five hits from one album, and the first artist of any kind to do it with a debut LP. "The first female artist to pull four top five hits from a single album."
**We Are the World (1985) - USA for Africa** : Lauper was among the artists invited onto the American charity mega-single by USA for Africa, and she gave the show-stopping vocal performance. "Cindy provided the show-stopping vocal performance."
**The Goonies 'R' Good Enough (1985)** : The theme song to the Steven Spielberg-produced, Richard Donner-directed kids' adventure film The Goonies. Lauper's new friends The Bangles made a cameo in the music video as pirates. "The bangles, who played pirates."
**True Colors (1986) - No. 1 Title Track** : The title track of her second LP spent its second week at No. 1 on the Hot 100 in the week ending November 1, 1986. "True colors by Cindy Lauper, the title track from her then new LP was in its second week at number 1 on the Hot 100."
**Change of Heart (1986) - True Colors Opener** : The lead-off track of True Colors, with background vocals by The Bangles. "The LP's lead-off track, Change of Heart, featured the fast-rising Los Angeles All Female"
**She's So Unusual (Helen Kane Cover)** : A brief cover of a 1920s ditty by Helen Kane, the singer who inspired Betty Boop, placed near the end of the album and used as its title. "Cindy included a brief cover of that track near the end of the album."

## curiosities
**Origins in Ozone Park, Queens** : Born Cynthia Ann Stephanie Lauper in 1953 and raised in Ozone Park, Queens. A teen friend encouraged her to spell her first name "Cyndi." She left home at 17 and spent her early 20s singing in several cover bands. "Left home at 17 and spent her early 20s singing with several cover bands."
**Damaged Four-Octave Voice (1977)** : The cover bands leaned so heavily on her four-octave voice that she had damaged her vocal cords by 1977, and she needed a singing coach to get her voice back. "It would not be the last time Cindy Lauper overcame long odds."
**Meeting John Turi and Forming Blue Angel (1978)** : Blue Angel formed in 1978 when saxophonist John Turi met the 25-year-old singer at a Greenwich Village nightclub. "A 25-year-old strong-lung singer named Cynthia."
**Never Played CBGB** : According to Lauper's memoir, Blue Angel mostly played uptown clubs plus Great Gildersleeves, a downtown club a block from CBGB. She was still a fan of first-wave punk, post-punk and new wave: Elvis Costello, early-punk-era The Police, and Chrissie Hynde's Pretenders. The retro-meets-new-wave sound followed campy acts like the New York Dolls and The B-52's. "Blue Angel were not punk, and they never played legendary club CBGB."
**Refusing to Go Solo** : Lauper turned down several offers to leave Blue Angel and go solo. She was loyal to John Turi and the band, and she didn't want to be pigeonholed as a balladeer. "She didn't want to be pigeonholed as a baladier."
**Bankruptcy and Screaming Mimi's** : After relations soured with Polydor and the band's manager, Lauper declared bankruptcy to get out of her contract. To pay the bills she spent two years as a salesgirl at Screaming Mimi's, a vintage clothing store known for ahead-of-the-curve looks that shaped her own style. New wave stars like Lene Lovich came through the shop, and Lauper was too afraid to talk to them. "Lauper, a former recording artist herself, was too afraid to talk to Loveach."
**Meeting Manager Dave Wolff (1981-1982)** : At a 1981 Christmas party she met Dave Wolff, a fast-talking, goofy but charming musician turned road manager. They started dating, and she hired him as her manager. By the end of 1982 he had gotten her signed to Portrait Records, a subsidiary of Epic, three years after Blue Angel flamed out. "She not only kept dating him, she hired him as her manager."
**Clothes Quote on Casey Kasem's Countdown** : When "Time After Time" hit No. 1, Casey Kasem quoted Lauper on how her style used to draw hostility. "People used to throw rocks at me for my clothes. Now they want to know where I buy them."
**Media-Manufactured Rivalry with Madonna** : The press and the industry, including Lauper's own record company, pitted her against Madonna in 1984; neither singer seemed interested in the feud. Lauper's only shady claim is that she wore a corset as a blouse before Madonna did. From her memoir: "you don't fucking knock another sister ever... our music wasn't even similar, and she was so smart about business and marketing. I never was." In a 2019 interview with New York classic rock DJ Shelli Sonstein she was still being asked about it. She said: "No, they pit women against women for it. I felt very bad at that time."
**Beat Madonna to the Top 10 and No. 1** : "Girls Just Want to Have Fun" made the top 10 months before Madonna did. Madonna's "Borderline" reached its No. 10 peak while "Time After Time" was already No. 1. Madonna's "Lucky Star" became her first top-five hit just weeks after "She Bop" peaked. "Another chart feat Lopper achieved first."
**Bangles as Opening Act (1985)** : By early 1985 The Bangles were opening on Lauper's tour, and the LA group and the New York singer took a shine to each other. Both acts were on CBS labels. "It was a synergistic pairing as the ladies were all signed to CBS labels."
**Never Nominated for the Rock Hall** : Neither Lauper, The Bangles nor Aimee Mann has even been nominated for the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame. "None of the subjects of this episode of hip-per-aid have been so much as nominated for the hall."

"One of those hits would soon wind up even bigger than true colors."

## curiosities
**Meeting in a Garage (1981)** : In 1981 the Peterson sisters from Northridge, California (guitarist Vicki and drummer Debbi) met Brentwood native Susanna Hoffs in a garage Hoffs had converted into an apartment and guitar practice space. All three were born at the turn of the 50s into the 60s and grew up on AM radio pop. They found they shared a love of British Invasion pop and that their voices harmonized instantly. "They literally met in a garage."
**The Bangs to The Bangles (1982)** : After adding bassist Annette Zilinskas and trying several names, by 1982 they settled on The Bangs, after the hairstyle, inspired by a reference in a 1965 fashion magazine article. When they learned an East Coast band already had the name, they changed it to The Bangles. "They were now the bangles."
**60s Songs on 80s Instruments** : Chiming guitars, vintage mod beats and sweet harmonies: would-be 60s songs played on 80s instruments. "These were would be 60s songs played on 80s instruments."
**Miles Copeland as Manager** : IRS label chief Miles Copeland managed The Bangles and raised their profile quickly. He got them opening slots on a tour by new wave band The English Beat and a guest spot on MTV's first indie music show, The Cutting Edge. "IRS label chief Miles Copeland served as the bangles manager."
**Susanna Hoffs on The Cutting Edge** : On MTV's The Cutting Edge, Hoffs explained the band's 60s-gone-80s ethos. "We all grew up listening to Top 40 Radio in the 60s which was everything from Dusty Springfield to Dilling to the Beatles to Motown stuff and living growing up in LA where you're always in a car."
**Annette Zilinskas Leaves** : Around 1983, bassist Annette Zilinskas, whose voice never blended as well with the Petersons and Hoffs, left for another band. The Bangles wanted a pro bassist, ideally one with girl-band experience. "Whose voice never quite harmonized as well with the Peterson sisters and Hoffs left the group for another band."
**Michael Steele Completes the Lineup** : Vicki Peterson recruited Michael Steele, formerly Micki Steele of the first version of The Runaways. Chris O'Leary wrote: "some bands only cement upon the arrival of a final member. The Beatles with Ringo. The Bangles with Steel. She elevated them." O'Leary also credited her with the Neo-60s sound, stronger harmonies, and songwriting on par with Hoffs and the Petersons. "Michael Steel blended seamlessly into The Bangles."
**Seen as the "Next Go-Go's"** : One DJ told Billboard, "we need stuff like this. We haven't had a girl group since The Go-Go's." Chris O'Leary argued the two bands were never really rivals, but rock radio had tacit limits on female artists, so one girl band's demise opened playlist room for another. "The two bands had never been rivals as much as legend has it."
**Pirates in The Goonies Video (1985)** : The Bangles cameoed as pirates in Cyndi Lauper's "The Goonies 'R' Good Enough" video. As of mid-1985 they had still not had a serious hit. "Clearly, the bangles had ingratiated themselves with enough famous people to help their career."
**Strong 1986** : In 1986 they had a couple of top-40 hits and a gold album on its way to platinum, all while backing up Lauper. "The Bangles were having a very good 1986."

## songs
**Voices Carry (1985) - Biggest Pop Hit, No. 8** : Mann wrote it about an embittered relationship and originally sang it with all "she" pronouns, performing it that way live, which suggested a lesbian relationship. Epic thought it sounded like a hit and pushed her to flip the pronouns, and she did. Even so, the song was very raw for a radio single. The cinematic, melodramatic video shows Mann in an abusive relationship with a controlling boyfriend who resents her band and her countercultural lifestyle. At one point he even forces himself on her. Shot around Boston, it climaxes at what is purported to be New York's Carnegie Hall, where Mann defiantly stands and yells the refrain amid a starchy audience. Critics praised it, one saying it "looks better than most feature films." It reached No. 8 on the Hot 100 by summer 1985 and remains her biggest pop hit. "It would be the biggest pop hit of Amy Mann's career."
**Change of Heart / True Colors Guest Vocal (1986)** : Mann was among the guest vocalists on Cyndi Lauper's True Colors. "Among the other guest vocalists on Lopper's album was a Boston-based songwriter and very distinctive vocalist."

## curiosities
**Kidnapped as a Child** : Born Aimee Elizabeth Mann in 1960 just outside Richmond, Virginia. After her parents split, her mother and a new boyfriend kidnapped her and took her to Europe; her father found her and brought her back to Virginia. "Kidnapped man and took her to Europe."
**Self-Taught Against Family Ridicule** : As a tween she taught herself bass and guitar despite her family's disinterest and even ridicule. "Amy taught herself bass and guitar over the disinterest and even the ridicule of her family."
**Punk as Escape** : As a teen in the 70s she was drawn to punk. She told The Guardian: "it was a revelation... Literally do whatever you want. That Patty Smith was out there and people were accepting her. Oh my god, there's a way out."
**Berklee Dropout (1978)** : She escaped Virginia by enrolling at Boston's Berklee College of Music in 1978, then dropped out after a year and a half to join the Boston music scene. "She dropped out after only a year and a half and dove into her Boston music scene."
**Young Snakes** : Mann formed the post-punk group Young Snakes, bringing her distinctive bass playing and voice. She was one of only three female singers on a 1981 Boston punk compilation. She grew tired of the band's angular, arty sound and told the LA Times: "the other two guys objected to any love songs or any songs that had melody. They were into lyrics that didn't mean much."
**Brief Stint in Ministry** : After Young Snakes she briefly joined Al Jourgensen's proto-industrial band Ministry, then closer to synth pop. There she learned to write songs more efficiently. "Mann learned how to write songs more efficiently."
**Pressure After Breakthrough** : Like Lauper, Mann was under pressure in 1986 to prove her earlier success wasn't a fluke. Unlike The Bangles, she had written her own breakthrough hit. "Mann at least wrote her breakthrough hit."

# genre - Paisley Underground

## curiosities
**Origin of the Name** : The LA scene included The Bangles, Rain Parade (with David Roback), The Three O'Clock (Michael Quercio's band, first called The Salvation Army) and The Dream Syndicate (Steve Wynn). Quercio tossed off the name in an interview and it stuck. By the end of 1982 the four bands were playing bills together. "A name Kursio from the three o'clock tossed off in an interview that stuck."
**Translators of the 60s** : Critic Chris O'Leary called the Paisley Underground acts "translators of the dead 60s into the fledgling 80s who treated it with more love than irony."

# label - IRS Records

## curiosities
**Indie Superlabel** : Home of The Go-Go's, IRS signed The Bangles on the strength of their debut EP. Label chief Miles Copeland also managed the band. "The Indy Superlabel that had signed the Go Go's."

# label - CBS Records

## curiosities
**Shared Corporate Family** : Epic, Portrait (an Epic subsidiary) and Columbia were all in the CBS family, so Cyndi Lauper, The Bangles and 'Til Tuesday were all on CBS labels. "It was a synergistic pairing as the ladies were all signed to CBS labels."

# concert - Rock and Roll Hall of Fame 2021

## curiosities
**Half the Class Living Women** : The 2021 class, inducted in Cleveland, was promoted as a diversity triumph because half were women: Tina Turner, Carole King and The Go-Go's. Unlike recent inductees Nina Simone, Whitney Houston and Sister Rosetta Tharpe, all three were alive, a first for the Hall. "Half the class are living women."
**Poor Track Record with Women** : The Hall's history with women has been especially poor, and The Go-Go's took a long time even to reach the ballot. "The institution's track record with women has been particularly poor."

# curiosity

**Women of Rock on the Late-1986 Charts** : In the closing months of 1986, women with rock foundations and pop sensibilities were doing well, including Tina Turner, Belinda Carlisle, Carly Simon, Aretha Franklin, Chrissie Hynde's Pretenders and Annie Lennox's Eurythmics. "Women with rock foundations and pop sensibilities were doing quite well on the charts."
**PMRC Filthy Fifteen** : The Parents Music Resource Center's list of objectionable songs included Lauper's "She Bop," Prince's "Darling Nikki" and Judas Priest's "Eat Me Alive." "The Parents Music Resource Center's Filthy 15 list of objectionable pop songs."
**Tacit Limits on Female Artists at Rock Radio** : Per Chris O'Leary, many rock stations had strict if unspoken limits on how many female artists got airplay. "Many rock radio stations had strict if tacit limits on how many female artists would get airplay."
**Women Pitted Against Each Other** : Lauper, The Bangles and Aimee Mann fought for autonomy in a business that often belittled women or pitted them against each other, and each tried to define her own image. "They fought for their autonomy in a business that often belittled women performers or pitted them against each other."
**Time After Time (1979 Film)** : The sci-fi time-machine film starring Malcolm McDowell and Mary Steenburgen gave Lauper's song its title via a TV Guide listing. "A 1979 sci-fi film about a time machine starring Malcolm McDowell."
**INOJ's Time After Time (1998)** : The 90s pop and R&B singer took the song back onto the Hot 100, peaking at No. 6 in 1998. "Took the song back onto the hot 100 in 1998, where it peaked at number six."
**Tuck & Patti's Time After Time (1988)** : The acoustic jazz duo's 1988 recording became popular on quiet storm and smooth jazz stations. "Became popular on Quiet Storm and Smooth Jazz stations."
