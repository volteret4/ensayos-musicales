## albums
**Unnamed Australian Albums (Pre-1967)** : Prior to their international debut, the Bee Gees had previously issued a pair of albums in Australia, which Robert Stigwood, their new manager, chose to treat as if they were by a brand new group. "Although the Bee Gees had previously issued a pair of albums in Australia, Stigwood treated them as a brand new group."
**Bee Gees First (1967) - International Debut** : This album served as the Bee Gees' international debut, despite them having released two prior albums in Australia. The tactic worked, generating three immediate hits, all of which were top 20 singles on Billboard's Hot 100 during a period when British acts dominated American charts. "Stigwood treated them as a brand new group, and their international debut was titled Bee Gees First. The tactic worked, amazingly the album generated three immediate hits."
**Odessa (1969) - Double LP Concept Album** : This was the Bee Gees' first ever double LP set and a concept album. It caused the group's first crisis, as a dispute over which track should be the single led to Robin Gibb's temporary departure. "It was spawned by their first ever double LP set, the 1969 Concept Album Odessa."
**Cucumber Castle (1970) - Two-Person Album** : Recorded by Barry and Maurice Gibb after Robin Gibb pulled away from the group to record a solo album. Their older sister Leslie contributed harmony vocals to this album, but it did not sell well in either the UK or the US. "Barry and Morris, meanwhile, kept the group name alive and recorded a two-person Bee Gees album, Cucumber Castle, with their older sister Leslie contributing harmony vocals."
**Trafalgar (1971)** : This album featured "How Can You Mend a Broken Heart," which became the lead single and the softest, most pillowy hit the Bee Gees had ever recorded, marking their first real slow jam. "The lead single off the 1971 album, Trafalgar was the softest, most pillowy hit the Bee Gees had ever recorded."
**Bee Gees Greatest Hits (1973) - Commercial Irrelevance** : This greatest hits album, composed largely of ballads, barely scraped the Billboard Top 100, signaling a period of commercial irrelevance for the band and prompting their need for a new musical direction. "When even their late 1973 greatest hits album, a wash and ballads barely scraped the Billboard Top 100, the Bee Gees knew they needed a new direction."
**Mr. Natural (1974) - Pivot Point Album** : Produced by Arif Mardin in a New York studio, this album was a "noble failure" commercially, only reaching number 178 on the album chart and spawning no hits. However, it served as a crucial pivot point in the Bee Gees' career, as Mardin encouraged them to experiment with new sounds and vocal ranges. "Mr. Natural was a noble failure, only reaching number 178 on the album chart in 1974, and spawning no hits. But with hindsight, Mr. Natural was the pivot point of the Bee Gees career."
**Main Course (1975) - Breakthrough Album** : This album, recorded at Criteria Studios in Miami with producer Arif Mardin, marked a radical shift for the Bee Gees and for the sound of the male voice in popular music. It established the template for their sound in the second half of the 1970s and became their first studio album certified gold by the RIAA by Christmas 1975. "And it's where they recorded their 1975 breakthrough, main course. It's worth pausing a moment here to consider just how radical a shift this album would be, not just for the Bee Gees, but for the sound of the male voice in popular music."
**Children of the World (1976) - First Platinum Album** : This album marked the first time the Gibb Brothers, Alby Galuten, and Karl Richardson were credited as producers. It featured the piercing falsetto sound perfected on "Nights on Broadway" and became the Bee Gees' first platinum album by the end of 1976. "Starting with their 1976 album, Children of the World, the producers of record on Bee Gees albums would be the three Gibb Brothers, Galuton, and Richardson."
**Bee Gees Live Album (1977)** : Released at the start of 1977, this live album was one of the projects the Gibbs branched out into, alongside bringing their younger brother Andy Gibb into the hit-making fold. "The Gibbs began branching out into other projects, such as releasing a Bee Gees live album at the start of 1977."
**Saturday Night Fever (1977) - Movie Soundtrack** : This soundtrack album became number one on the album chart in January 1978, four weeks after its lead single reached number one. It would go on to command the Billboard album chart for 24 weeks, sell 10 million copies in the US alone, and become the top-selling album of all time until Michael Jackson's *Thriller* five years later. "In January 1978, on the album chart, the Saturday Night Fever LP was also number one."

## songs
**Spicks and Specks (1966)** : This song became the Bee Gees' first major hit single, reaching number 4 on Australia's National Go Set chart in November 1966. Its piano hook, picked out by Maurice Gibb, sounded very much like Revolver-era Paul McCartney, though the song took on its own character after Barry Gibb completed it. "That's Spix and Specs... The Bee Gees first major hit single, which reached the top five in Australia in 1966."
**Monday's Rain (1966)** : In 1966, Australian radio DJs refused to play this single, arguing that it sounded too derivative of the Beatles' *Rubber Soul* era. "At one point in 1966, Australian radio DJs refused to play their new single Monday's reign on the premise that it sounded too derivative of rubber-soil era Beatles."
**New York Mining Disaster 1941 (Have You Seen My Wife, Mr. Jones) (1967)** : A lyrical flight of fancy by co-writers Barry and Robin Gibb, who had never been to New York and knew of no mining disaster there in 1941. This song reached number 14 on Billboard's Hot 100, becoming one of three immediate hits from their international debut album, *Bee Gees First*. "Like the Battle of the Blue and the Grey, the song New York Mining Disaster 1941, parentheses, have you seen my wife, Mr. Jones, was a lyrical flight of fancy for co-writers Barry and Robin Gibb."
**Holiday (1967)** : A minor-key song that reached number 60 on Billboard's Hot 100, contributing to the immediate success of the Bee Gees' international debut album, *Bee Gees First*. "New York Mining Disaster reached number 14, and the similarly minor key holiday reached number 60."
**To Love Somebody (1967)** : From *Bee Gees First*, this soulful, stately ballad was inspired by Otis Redding and is considered by many critics to be the best song the Bee Gees ever wrote. It became a standard, later recorded by more than two dozen artists, including Nina Simone, Gram Parsons, and Billy Corgan. "Inspired by Otis Redding, the Gibb's penned a soulful, stately ballad, to love somebody became a standard."
**Massachusetts (The Lights Went Out in Massachusetts) (1967)** : This lyrically fanciful but musically sturdy story song became the Bee Gees' first British number one before the end of 1967. It imagined a Massachusetts where the lights had dimmed because all the hippies had left for San Francisco. "Massachusetts, subtitled The Lights Went Out in Massachusetts, was yet another lyrically fanciful, but musically sturdy story song."
**I've Gotta Get a Message to You (1968)** : Led by Robin Gibb's vocals, this song became the Bee Gees' top 10 breakthrough in the United States in the summer of 1968. It tells the story of a prisoner writing from death row. "In the summer of 68, Robin led the Bee Gees top 10 breakthrough in the United States, a song about a prisoner writing from death row titled, I've got to get a message to you."
**I Started a Joke (1968)** : A mournful, number six single led by Robin Gibb, this track was practically a Robin Gibb solo single, following his success with "I've Gotta Get a Message to You." "The Malkish number six single, I started a joke, practically a Robin Gibb solo single."
**First of May (1969)** : A Barry Gibb song from the *Odessa* album, this single barely scraped the American top 40, peaking at number 37. Its performance was a point of contention that contributed to Robin Gibb's temporary departure from the group. "A Barry song, first of May, barely scraped the American top 40, peaking at number 37."
**Lamplight (1969)** : A track from the *Odessa* album that Robin Gibb argued should have been released as a single, leading to a major dispute with Barry Gibb who ultimately overruled him. "Robin Gibb was furious, arguing that an Odessa track of his called Lamplight should have been the single."
**Lonely Days (1970)** : This single, a hybrid of Beatles-esque orchestral pop and a strutting rave-up with hand claps and brass, introduced the Bee Gees' reunion album. It became their highest-charting hit in America to date by January 1971, peaking at number three. "Lonely Gees was a hybrid. On the verse, the brothers offered more of the movie, Beatles-esque orchestral pop that had been their stocking trade."
**How Can You Mend a Broken Heart (1971)** : The lead single off the 1971 album *Trafalgar*, this was the softest, most pillowy hit the Bee Gees had ever recorded and their first real slow jam. It became the top song in America and placed fifth on Billboard's Year-end Chart for 1971. "The lead single off the 1971 album, Trafalgar was the softest, most pillowy hit the Bee Gees had ever recorded."
**Nights on Broadway (1975)** : The very first track on the *Main Course* album, this song is where the Bee Gees' new, superstar-making falsetto register materialized. It is built on a hard funk riff, beginning with a low Barry vocal, shifting to a dreamy bridge with Barry and Robin harmonizing, and culminating in Barry's aggressive, impossibly high male voice ad-lib during the final chorus, after producer Arif Mardin asked if any of them could "scream." "What is remarkable about main course... not only how they found a new register... materialized within the space of one song. That would be the album's very first track, Nights on Broadway."
**Jive Talkin' (1975)** : The innovative and irresistible lead single from *Main Course*, RSO Records initially sent it to radio stations as an unmarked white label to prevent DJs from prejudging it based on the Bee Gees' "commercially poisonous name." It became the breakthrough disco smash of 1975, topping the Hot 100 in August 1975. The song's distinctive "Chica Chica" rhythm was inspired by the sound of car wheels on the Miami causeway to Criteria Studios, which Barry Gibb's wife, Linda, dubbed "drive talk." Welsh keyboardist Blue Weaver added a synthesizer hook reminiscent of Stevie Wonder's Synclavier. "Jive Talking was serendipitous in more ways than one. Every day when the Gibbs drove to criteria studios crossing the causeway that connected Biscayne Bay to Miami, the wheels of their car made a distinctive Chica Chica rhythm."
**Fanny (Be Tender with My Love) (1976)** : A romantic reverie and love song, this track applied the Bee Gees' new R&B falsetto sound and became a number 12 hit by the start of 1976. "By the start of 1976, the Bee Gees even applied this new R&B falsetto sound to a love song, the romantic reverie, fanning, be tender with my love, a number 12 hit."
**You Should Be Dancing (1976)** : The lead-off single from the *Children of the World* album, this four-on-the-floor dance record topped the Hot 100 in September 1976. It earned a showcase spot in the *Saturday Night Fever* film, featuring John Travolta's immortal solo dance to the song. "You should be dancing, the lead off single of Children of the World, topped the Hot 100 in September 1976."
**Love So Right (1976)** : A mid-tempo track that showcased the versatility of the Bee Gees' new, piercing falsetto sound, this song became a number three hit in November 1976, demonstrating its effectiveness beyond pure dance records. "It worked on a four on the floor dance record like you should be dancing, and it worked equally well on mid-tempo tracks like Love So Right, a number three hit in November of 1976."
**Stayin' Alive (1977)** : One of the Bee Gees' most famous hits, its immortal backing track was created by producer Alby Galuten by borrowing and looping a drum segment from the already recorded "Night Fever," a technique that would later be used on hip-hop records. In March 1978, it ejected the band Player from the number one spot on the Hot 100. "producer Albe Galutin actually created a backing track from a drum segment he borrowed and looped from the already recorded Night Fever."
**How Deep Is Your Love (1977)** : A lush and sumptuous slow dance track, this song perfected the disco ballad during the *Saturday Night Fever* sessions, with keyboardist Blue Weaver contributing. It served as the lead-off single for the *Saturday Night Fever* soundtrack and became number one on the Hot 100 in December 1977, just one week after the film's release. "The Gibbs, working again with keyboardist Blue Weaver who'd come up with the irresistible hook of Give Talkin, perfected the disco ballad with the lush, sumptuous slow dance How Deep Is Your Love?"
**If I Can't Have You (1977)** : Written by the Bee Gees, this promising song was played for Robert Stigwood during the *Saturday Night Fever* sessions but was later given away and became a future number one hit for Yvonne Elliman in early May 1978. "They played Stigwood versions of songs they would later give away if I can't have you, the future Ivan element hit."
**More Than a Woman (1977)** : A plush yet danceable love song written by the Bee Gees, played for Robert Stigwood during the *Saturday Night Fever* sessions. "And the plush yet danceable love song more than a woman."
**Night Fever (1978)** : This song gave John Travolta's movie its title and became a number one hit, remaining at the top of the Hot 100 for an eight-week run that ended in early May. "At number one, the song that gave John Travolta's movie its title, the Bee Gees' Night Feaver."

## curiosities
**British Origins and Australian Formation** : Barry Gibb (born 1946) and fraternal twins Robin and Maurice Gibb (born 1949) were all born on the Isle of Man, a British crown dependency, making them British nationals. Little brother Andy Gibb (born 1958) was also British, born in Manchester. The family moved to Redcliffe, Queensland, near Brisbane, Australia, in 1958 and lived there until 1966, during which time the vocal group, initially called The Rattlesnakes, was formed. "The three eldest Gibb brothers were born on the Isle of Man, a British crown dependency adjacent to the United Kingdom... That 1958 is also when the Gibbs moved to the Australian seaside town of Redcliffe in Queensland near Brisbane."
**The Rattlesnakes - Early Band Name** : The Bee Gees initially performed under the name The Rattlesnakes before adopting their more famous moniker. "So, the former rattlesnakes became the Bee Gees."
**Name Origin - Bill Gates, Bill Good, Barry Gibb** : The name "Bee Gees" coincidentally arose from the initials of two men who discovered the brothers singing for change at a Redcliffe Speedway in 1960: DJ Bill Gates and promoter Bill Good. The initials also happened to match eldest brother and group leader Barry Gibb's name. "Bee Gee happened to be the initials of the two men who discovered the brothers singing for change at a local Redcliffe Speedway in 1960. DJ Bill Gates and promoter Bill Good. Bee Gee also happened to be the initials of eldest brother and de facto group leader, Barry Gibb."
**Everly Brothers Influence** : The Bee Gees drew inspiration from the harmonizing American sibling band, the Everly Brothers, for their vocal group. "Their inspiration was an American sibling band, the harmonizing Everly Brothers."
**Versatility and Early Genre Exploration** : Over a half-decade, the Gibbs demonstrated significant versatility, or what a cynic might call a lack of a distinct sound. They experimented with a range of pop modes, including Country Pop Jamboree with lyrics about the American Civil War, as heard in "The Battle of the Blue and the Grey," and the British Invasion sound in "I Was a Lover, a Leader of Men." "Over a half decade, the Gibbs showed their versatility... They would try anything, a range of pop modes, from Country Pop Jamboree with lyrics about the American Civil War on the Battle of the Blue and the Grey."
**Beatles Comparisons** : For much of their first decade, the Bee Gees were dogged by accusations of sounding similar to The Beatles. In 1966, Australian radio DJs even refused to play their single "Monday's Rain" due to its perceived derivativeness of the *Rubber Soul*-era Beatles. "Accusations of similarity to the Beatles, dogged the Bee Gees for much of their first decade."
**Move to England (1966)** : Despite finally achieving an Australian hit with "Spicks and Specks" in November 1966, the Bee Gees and their family had already booked passage on an ocean liner back to England, convinced that "swinging London" would be more beneficial for their career after years in the Australian music business. "When Spix and Specks finally reached number 4 on Australia's National Go Set chart in November 1966, the Bee Gees and their parents and siblings already had tickets for an ocean liner back to England."
**Robert Stigwood Discovery and Signing (1966-1967)** : Australian entertainment impresario Robert Stigwood, then working at NEMS Enterprises, intercepted a copy of the Bee Gees' single "Spicks and Specks" sent by their father, Hugh Gibb. Stigwood, already managing Cream, signed the Bee Gees to a transatlantic recording contract by February 1967, shortly after their arrival in London. "When I signed the Bee Gees, it didn't take a genius to know that one had an incredible new exciting talent. Robert Stigwood was a boundlessly energetic, fiercely independent, and amazingly successful Australian entertainment in Prasaria."
**Carnaby Street Image (1967)** : Bankrolled by Robert Stigwood and outfitted in Carnaby Street jackets and slacks, the Bee Gees became the top new British group of 1967, quickly achieving sensation in England. "Outfitted in Carnaby Street, jackets and slacks, bankrolled by Robert Stigwood, they became the top new British group of 1967."
**First Crisis and Hiatus (1969-1970)** : A tension between Barry as the primary songwriter and Robin as an emerging vocal star boiled over with the 1969 *Odessa* album. Robin was furious that his song "Lamplight" was passed over for the single in favor of Barry's "First of May," leading Robin to leave the group and record a solo album. Barry and Maurice continued briefly as a duo, but by December 1969, the group was on a full hiatus, with Barry himself declaring he was done. "This tension between Barry as the group's leader and primary songwriter and Robin as its emerging star would eventually boil over into the Bee Gees first crisis."
**Reunion (1970)** : In the summer of 1970, out of boredom, twins Robin and Maurice began recording together. By late summer, Barry was informed of a potential rapprochement, joined them in the studio, and Robert Stigwood announced their reunion. "In the summer of 1970, partially out of boredom, twins Robin and Morris began recording again, quietly for an unnamed project."
**Commercial Irrelevance (1972-1974)** : After the success of "How Can You Mend a Broken Heart," the Bee Gees struggled for nearly three years, recording a string of ballads that failed to chart high. Their late 1973 greatest hits album barely scraped the Billboard Top 100, indicating they had made themselves commercially irrelevant. "From 1972 to 1974, their career was once again in the wilderness, and this time it wasn't due to bickering or a breakup. They had made themselves commercially irrelevant."
**Arif Mardin Collaboration (1974-1975)** : Atlantic Records connected the Bee Gees with Turkish-American producer Arif Mardin, who had worked with diverse artists like Aretha Franklin and Bette Midler. Mardin was instrumental in reinventing their career, encouraging them to pick up the tempo and explore new vocal parts, especially on the albums *Mr. Natural* and *Main Course*. "That's when Atlantic Records, the distributor for their American label, connected them with the man who would reinvent their career. Arif Martin, a Turkish American producer who had worked with everyone from Aretha Franklin to Steven Stills to Bet Middler."
**Development of Falsetto Vocal Style** : While the Bee Gees had used falsetto before, it was typically a whisper or "head voice." On *Main Course*, inspired by the Philly Soul sound of The Stylistics' Russell Tompkins Jr., Barry Gibb developed a new, impossibly high, loud, and aggressive falsetto that became their 1970s trademark. "In later interviews, Barry Gibb specifically cited the Philly Soul sound of the stylistics whose lead singer Russell Tompkins Jr sang in an especially high falsetto, a key model for Barry's new vocal approach."
**RSO Records Distributor Switch (1976)** : At the start of 1976, Robert Stigwood's RSO Records switched distributors from Atlantic to Polygram. This change prevented Arif Mardin, Atlantic's house producer, from continuing to work with the Bee Gees, although they were able to retain musical supervisor Alby Galuten and engineer Karl Richardson. "At the start of 1976, Robert Stigwood's RSO Records switched distributors from Atlantic to Polygram, and Martin was no longer permitted to work with the Bee Gees."
**Progenitors of the Disco Ballad** : The Bee Gees' versatile falsetto sound allowed them to create both four-on-the-floor dance records and mid-tempo tracks, and they are recognized as progenitors of what became known as the disco ballad, which they perfected with "How Deep Is Your Love." "The Bee Gees were progenitors of what became known as the disco ballad."
**Idolization of The Beatles** : Since the 1960s, the Bee Gees had idolized The Beatles, an influence that was evident in their music and chart aspirations, even achieving chart feats comparable to The Beatles in 1978. "Since the 60s, the Bee Gees had idolized the Beatles."
**Sergeant Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band Film (1978)** : The Bee Gees starred in this 1978 movie musical, based on The Beatles' 1967 album, playing the fictional band "The Hendersons." The film is routinely listed among the biggest pop culture flops of all time. "The Gibbs and Frampton star in this 70s artifact as the members of a fictional Sergeant Pepper's band, the Bee Gees playing The Henderson's and Frampton playing Billy Shears."
**Chart Dominance (March 18, 1978)** : On the week ending March 18, 1978, Gibb compositions held four out of the top five songs on Billboard's Hot 100. Furthermore, the Bee Gees held two top positions in the Top 40, a feat not matched by any other recording act since The Beatles 14 years prior, and were responsible for six of the songs in that week's Top 40. "the week ending March 18, 1978, when Gibb compositions held down four out of the top five songs on the Hot 100."
**Saturday Night Fever Context** : By 1977, the Bee Gees were already a revived, hit act with back-to-back gold and platinum albums and a string of disco hits, including two number ones, before their involvement with the *Saturday Night Fever* soundtrack. The movie did not revive them, but rather built on their existing success. "The movie did not revive the Bee Gees. By 1977, they were already revived, a hit act with back-to-back gold-and-platinum albums and a string of disco hits, including two number ones."
