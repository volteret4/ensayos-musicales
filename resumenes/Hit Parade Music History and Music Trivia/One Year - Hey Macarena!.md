**Macarena (1993) - Original Version** : Antonio Romero and Rafael Ruiz, performing as Los Del Rio, met in 1962 in Dos Hermanas, Spain, and started playing music together at age 14. In 1992, while on tour in Venezuela, they improvised a song inspired by a flamenco dancer. They initially named the girl in the song "Madalena" and wrote lyrics about her moving her body and having a good time, fantasizing about shopping and moving to New York, and having multiple boyfriends while her main boyfriend, Vitorino, was away. The name was quickly changed to "Macarena" because it was the name of the Virgin Mary celebrated in Seville and also one of the group members' daughters. The song was honed during rehearsal the next day with musicians and recorded back home in Seville at a famous Flamenco studio. This version features hand clapping, percussion, and acoustic flamenco guitar strumming, with the "hey, Macarena" part appearing at the end. They did not consider the song remarkable during its creation. "Madalena initially was the name of the girl. Dalia tu cuerpo alegría madalena, que tu cuerpo esfadarle alegría y cosa buena."
**Macarena (1993) - Debut and Spanish Success** : Los Del Rio debuted the Macarena at Seville's Big Spring Fair in 1993. The fair, which includes tents for parties, food, and music performances, saw the song rapidly gain popularity, with attendees talking and playing it everywhere by the fair's end, signaling to the band that they had their biggest hit to date. Although inspired by a dancer, no choreography existed for this original version. It became Spain's song of the summer, an infectious, up-tempo anthem, a massive and unexpected accomplishment for the duo, who had traditionally appealed to an older, regional Spanish audience. "Everybody was talking and playing Macarena. So I think that they realized that they had a big hit, probably the biggest hit they'd ever had up until that moment."
**Macarena (1996) - Billboard Number One Hit** : The "Macarena" topped the charts for months, eventually becoming Billboard's Number One Hit of 1996, surpassing popular songs by artists like Mariah Carey, Boys to Men, and Celine Dion. This achievement marked the song's widespread success and its defining presence in mid-90s pop music, despite its eventual status as a "cultural pariah." "It topped the charts for months, winding up Billboard's Number One Hit of 1996, oversmashes by Mariah Carey, Boys to Men, and Celine Dion."
## curiosities
**Aging Nostalgic Fanbase** : In the first three decades of their career, Los Del Rio primarily appealed to "aging nostalgic Spaniards," while younger audiences in metropolitan centers found their music outdated. Their global success with "Macarena" was a significant departure from this established pattern. "Their biggest fans were in the group's own words, aging nostalgic spaniards, younger people, in the country's metropolitan centers found their music outdated."
**Appearance and Regional Identity** : Antonio Romero and Rafael Ruiz were described as genial men in their 40s who looked like businessmen, reinforcing their image as traditional artists. Their music was deeply regional, specifically "of Sevilla," which made their international breakout with "Macarena" even more surprising. "You know, they were in their 40s. They look like businessmen as the best way I can describe it."
**Loss of Control over Creation** : Despite writing the original song, Los Del Rio ultimately "lost control of their creation" as the "Macarena" became known for a dance they didn't invent, a backing track they didn't play on, and English verses they didn't write. However, they didn't seem to mind, as the song elevated them from obscurity and carried them around the world. "The song they'd improvised in Venezuela had lifted them from obscurity. Now, it was carrying them around the world, including to the United States."
**Virgin Mary as Inspiration** : Rafael Ruiz suggested that the song's immense success might be a "gift from its namesake," the Virgin of Macarena, who is the patron saint of bullfighting in Seville. This connection provided a spiritual explanation for the unexpected global phenomenon. "In an interview, Los Alreos Rafael Ruiz said that the song's success might just be a gift from its namesake, the Virgin of Macarena, the patron saint of bullfighting in Seville."
**Observation of 18-wheeler in Miami** : Upon their first visit to the United States, after being greeted at the airport in Miami by BMG executive Rojaleo Mascin, Antonio Romero and Rafael Ruiz requested their car to stop so they could take a picture with an 18-wheeler truck, as they had never seen one before. This highlights their unfamiliarity with certain aspects of American culture, contrasting with the song's rapid American assimilation. "And going from the airport to the hotel, they saw an 18-wheeler parked in the street somewhere in Miami. And they asked me to stop the car because they wanted to take a picture by the 18-wheeler because they have never seen an 18-wheeler."

**Macarena (1995) - Basside Boys Remix Origin** : In the spring of 1995, Power 96 FM DJ Jam and Johnny Carre Day approached record producers Carlos De Arza and Mike Treyi (who operated under the name The Basside Boys, derived from their studio Basside Music) to create an English remix of Los Del Rio's "Macarena." The radio station, which primarily played English songs, stipulated that the track needed English elements to be considered for airplay. Carlos De Arza spent approximately 90 minutes researching existing versions, listening to the original, and then creatively adding English lyrics from Macarena's perspective, along with various samples and a guide track. "The ask was, they want you to do what you do to these things and put something in English on it. Put a wrap on it."
**Macarena (1995) - Basside Boys Remix Creation** : Carlos De Arza did not alter the existing instrumentation of the "River Famix" (Los Del Rio's electronic remix) but instead rearranged its elements and inserted new English verses from Macarena's point of view. He composed the lyrics in roughly 90 minutes, describing them as a "nursery rhyme." He then enlisted his friend Patty Alfarro to record the female vocals, mirroring his guide track. Additionally, he incorporated an isolated laugh sample from the B-side of a Situation 12-inch record, which he felt encapsulated Macarena's sound, and a spoken word sample ("I am not trying to seduce you") from a remix of George Michael's "Too Funky," unknowingly sourced from "The Graduate" movie. "If I were to add all the time that I worked on this project, I would say maybe 90 minutes from research to edit to writing to recording vocals."
**Macarena (1995) - Basside Boys Remix Success** : The Basside Boys' remix was submitted to Power 96 FM at the end of June 1995 and immediately entered rotation. The station's request lines "exploded" with calls, and the song quickly became a local sensation in Miami, despite Carlos De Arza's initial belief that listeners would "get tired of it." By early October, the record had sold 150,000 copies, with people dancing to it in diverse settings from gay bars to Girl Scout meetings and operating rooms, solidifying its status as a "perfect mutation" and "ultimate Macarena." "By the beginning of October, the record that Carlos had worked on for 90 minutes had sold 150,000 copies."
## curiosities
**Studio Name Origin** : The name "Basside Boys" for Carlos De Arza and Mike Treyi's remixing duo came directly from their studio, "Basside Music." When their "Macarena" remix began to gain traction, Power 96 FM would announce, "Oh, here's a new one. The Basside Boys did this," effectively establishing their moniker. "My studio is called Basside Music. So when the record starts playing, oh, here's a new one. The Basside Boys did this. And so that's where that name comes from."
**Copyright Infringement and Resolution** : After the Basside Boys' remix achieved national popularity, Marcia Records, Los Del Rio's label, contacted Carlos De Arza regarding copyright infringement. The label's lawyer initially threatened cease-and-desist orders to halt radio play. However, due to the remix's immense popularity, they instead struck a deal: the record company retained the rights, released the remix on CD, and gave the Basside Boys "a small cut of the proceeds," acknowledging the remix's cultural impact and the impracticality of removing it from the airwaves. "The label did not shut down the bass-eyed boys. Given how popular the remix was, it would have been crazy to pull it off the air. So they struck a deal."

# artist - Lostel Mar
## curiosities
**Canadian Cover Version Controversy** : A Canadian producer assembled a group called Lostel Mar ("those from the sea") to create a cover version of "Macarena" that was almost identical, or "zero-oxed," to the original. This "shrewd business move" exploited Canadian media rules requiring a certain percentage of radio songs to be Canadian-made, giving Lostel Mar's version a significant advantage in airplay over Los Del Rio's Spanish original. "There's a law in Canada that says a certain percentage of songs played on the radio have to be Canadian made. While Lostel Rio came from Spain, Lostel Mar counted as Canadian."
**Canadian Chart Success** : The Lostel Mar version of "Macarena" achieved considerable success in Canada, hitting number one on the Canadian singles chart in July 1995 and holding that position for nine weeks. This success was a source of frustration for BMG Canada, who were simultaneously promoting the original Los Del Rio version, perceiving the cover as a "windfall" for a group that copied the "heart and soul" of the original composition. "It hit number one on the Canadian singles chart in July 1995 and stayed there for nine weeks."

# artist - Makarena Luz Vianqui
## curiosities
**Name Origin and Nicknames** : Makarena Luz Vianqui was born in Chile, but her name originates from Spain, specifically a district in Seville known for the Basilica de la Makarena and its statue of the Weeping Virgin Mary, La Vieta de la Makarena, the patron saint of bullfighting. Her parents chose the name because they knew someone independent and classy with it, and to avoid the family tradition of using "Mariah" (e.g., Mariah Carey'sa, Mariah Angelica, Mariah de la Luz). As a child in Chile, she went by "Maka," but after moving to Miami in 1982, people often mispronounced it as "Maserina," leading her to default to "Mac" or endure schoolyard taunts like "Macaroni" and "Big Mac." "Makarena was born in Chile, but her name comes from Spain. It's a district in the city of Seville, which is home to the Basilica de la Makarena."
**Love for Unique Name** : Despite schoolyard taunts and mispronunciations, Makarena Luz Vianqui never considered changing her name, loving its rarity in the United States. She cherished the uniqueness until a Spanish soap opera briefly threatened its exclusivity, and then the song "Macarena" ultimately made it ubiquitous. "Nobody here had it, which I thought was fantastic. So I liked that it was rare."
**Nightlife Scene in Miami Beach (1995)** : In 1995, in her early 20s, Makarena Luz Vianqui was a prominent figure in the Miami Beach nightlife scene. She worked as a door person at exclusive clubs, controlling who gained entry. She was dressed by designers, recalls being the first to wear "plethora pants," and connected with a vibrant community through house music, disliking "cheesy pop music." "I worked the door at like the cool places, the cool clubs. I would decide who got in."
**Horrified Reaction to the Song** : Makarena Luz Vianqui's first encounter with the "Macarena" song was through a DJ friend in Miami, and her reaction was immediate horror and tears. She felt the song was "ripping something from me," taking away her precious, rare name and potentially destroying its uniqueness. She actively tried to avoid hearing it, describing the experience as "mortified" and the song itself as "disgusting" and "gross," especially finding the lyrics about a "freaking fluzy" upsetting and a "hijack" of her name. "It was 100% like, no. And I think that's when I started crying. Like you're ripping something from me. You're taking something from me that's precious."

# curiosity
**"Macarena" as a Cultural Pariah** : After dominating the charts and becoming Billboard's Number One Hit of 1996, the "Macarena" quickly transformed into a "cultural pariah." This phenomenon of widespread popularity followed by rapid disdain marked its unique trajectory in popular music. "And then, just as quickly, Makarena became a cultural pariah."
**Pre-Internet Virality of "Macarena"** : The "Macarena" became a monumental hit through "pre-internet virality," mutating in ways its creators never imagined across continents and cultures before the advent of widespread digital communication. Its spread was an organic evolution driven by local adoption and remixes. "It's a tale of pre-internet virality. And how a song can mutate in ways its creators never imagined."
**"Macarena" Dance Origin** : While the "Macarena" song was inspired by a dancer in Venezuela, the famous line dance choreography seemed to have originated independently in Mexico. Rojaleo Mascin first observed this dance, resembling the electric slide but with "more hip-swiveling and butt grabbing," in clubs in Acapulco at the tail end of 1993. "While we might never know who invented the line dance, it seemed to have originated in Mexico."
**Mexican Dance Spread (1993-1994)** : The "Macarena" dance rapidly spread through Mexican beach towns with vacationers and cruise ship passengers in late 1993 and 1994. These travelers then brought the dance back to their hometowns, contributing to its early virality and establishing it as an all-night party starter. "The dance looked a bit like the electric slide, but with more hip-swiveling and butt grabbing. Everyone in Acapulco seemed to know it."
**South Texas Line Dance Tradition (1994)** : In 1994, the "Macarena" surged across the border into Texas, particularly the Rio Grande Valley. This region had a long-standing line dance tradition, which facilitated the song's rapid adoption, with locals finding it "more energetic" than previous crazes like Billy Ray Cyrus's "Achy Breaky Heart." "When that song hit the valley, the Rio Grande Valley, it was just like a phenomenon in the clubs."
**Seattle Radio Discovery (1995)** : In June 1995, Mike Tierney, program director at Cube 93 FM in Seattle, learned about the "Macarena" from a co-worker returning from Cabo San Lucas, Mexico, who described it as being played everywhere with an accompanying dance. Unable to find the record easily, they eventually located a 45-inch record at a stall in Pike Place Market called "Lossarina Rosa," which also sold masks and piñatas. "His story was just, yeah, there was this song and everywhere we went, they were playing it."
**Seattle Radio Experiment Success (1995)** : Cube 93 FM played the "Macarena" at a barbecue restaurant event in Seattle, where the dance floor immediately became packed as attendees already knew the dance. The record had to be played "basically until last call," leading to the station airing it continuously, and subsequently, their request lines "exploded." "Oh my god, you're not going to believe what happened last night. The dance horse packed, everybody already knew the dance. I've never seen anything like it."
**Canadian Content Regulations (90s)** : Canadian media rules, specifically a law mandating a certain percentage of radio songs be Canadian-made, were exploited by a Canadian producer to create a cover version of "Macarena" by Lostel Mar. This allowed the cover to gain significant airplay advantage over the Spanish original, leading to a "music industry war" between the two versions. "There's a law in Canada that says a certain percentage of songs played on the radio have to be Canadian made."
**Miami Cheerleader Promotion (1995)** : Rojaleo Mascin, a BMG executive, orchestrated a marketing strategy in Miami in 1995 to promote "Macarena." He gathered 12 cheerleaders, including one from Spain, taught them the dance, dressed them in mini skirts, and had them perform the "Macarena" every 15 minutes during "happy hour" on Fridays and Sundays at Miami's top clubs. This grassroots effort quickly spread the dance across the city. "We came up with the idea of promoting a happy hour, Fridays and Sundays. And the girls would have to dance the Macarena every 15 minutes for two hours."
**Miami Radio Station's English-Only Policy (1995)** : Power 96 FM, a significant radio station in Miami known for breaking new music, maintained an "entire playlist was songs in English" policy in 1995. This policy necessitated Carlos De Arza's Basside Boys remix to incorporate English elements for "Macarena" to gain airplay on the station, even though Miami was a Spanish-speaking city with a strong Latin music heritage. "Power, I don't know why, would not play songs in Spanish. Their entire playlist was songs in English."
**"Macarena" Ubiquity (1995)** : By late 1995, the "Macarena" had achieved widespread ubiquity across America, being danced at diverse events such as gay bars, Girl Scout meetings, and even inside operating rooms. This underscored its universal appeal and status as a massive cultural phenomenon. "People were dancing the Macarena at gay bars and Girl Scout meetings, and inside operating rooms."

One episode specifically delves into the phenomenon of "Macarena," going "much, much deeper into just how improbable this song's very existence was." **Macarena (Original)** : Originally written by an old-fashioned flamenco group, this tune is described as inherently "really, really catchy" in its original recording, even before the addition of the dance, electronic beats, English verses, or a music video. Its simple nature also contributed to its widespread appeal, allowing "everybody to be part of it." : "At the same time, when you listen to that original recording, the Macarena feels inevitable, even without the dance or the electronic beat or the English verses or the music video, it's just really, really catchy."
**Macarena (Christmas Remix) (1996)** : Released later in November 1996, this festive rendition further intensified the song's presence in popular culture, leading to its identification as "the most overexposed cultural product of the late 20th century." : "Later that month, Lostel Rio released a Christmas remix, cementing the Macarena's place as the most overexposed cultural product of the late 20th century."

## albums
**Bayside Boys Mix (1996)** : This version of "Macarena" achieved significant chart success, hitting number one on Billboard's Hot 100 in August 1996. By the close of 1996, it had sold over 4 million copies. Its reign at number one lasted for 14 weeks before being replaced by Blackstreet's "No Diggity" in November 1996, marking "still one of the longest runs in Billboard history." : "In August 1996, the bass-eyed boys mix hit number one on Billboard's Hot 100."

## curiosities

**Anti-Grunge Artistic Vision** : The group consciously aimed to be "the anti-grudge," setting out to create music and a stage presence that was "as glam and glitter and ridiculously fun as we possibly can be." This involved wearing "very hot pleather and throw glitter around" as a direct contrast to the "darker rock stuff" prevalent at the time. : "I said, we are going to be the anti-grudge."
**Financial Success Motivation** : The extensive touring and performances were driven by a clear financial objective, viewed as a means "to just kill it and come back to Miami with buckets of money." : "This is a way for us to just kill it and come back to Miami with buckets of money."
**Democratic National Convention Performance** : The Bayside Boys Mix of "Macarena" was featured at the Democratic National Convention, where it "inspired some awkward gyrations" among attendees, illustrating the song's wide-reaching cultural impact. : "And the Bayside Boys Mix inspired some awkward gyrations at the Democratic National Convention."
**Lack of Contact with Los del Río** : Despite their shared musical success, Carlos De Arza maintains that the Bayside Boys and Los del Río have "never spoken," expressing a humorous sentiment that Los del Río, having profited from the Bayside Boys' success, had not even sent "a chorizo from Spain" or a gift basket. : "But Carlos says the two groups have never spoken."
