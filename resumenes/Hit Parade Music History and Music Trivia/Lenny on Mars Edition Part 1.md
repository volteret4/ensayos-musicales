## albums
**Let Love Rule (1989) - Debut Album** : Released in September 1989, this debut album took several months to break on the charts. Virgin Records promoted it primarily to rock radio stations, where its neo-hippy rock sound found success on both alternative/modern rock (peaking at No. 5) and album rock charts (No. 23). Critics noted its Beatles-esque qualities, with Rolling Stone categorizing it as a "childlike musical collage of no particular point," but radio listeners embraced its neo-Hendrix style. It was a sleeper hit on the album chart, reaching No. 61 by February 1990 and eventually certified gold five years later. "Lenny called the album Let Love Rule."
**Mama Said (1991) - Sophomore Album** : Released in 1991, this album kicked off with a vintage-style funk rock sound, further solidifying Kravitz's brand. It generated several hits and achieved gold certification by the fall of 1991, later reaching platinum status four years later. "Kravitz built on that brand on his 1991 sophomore album, titled Mama Said, which kicked off with a blast of uncut vintage style funk rock."
**Are You Gonna Go My Way (1993) - Breakthrough Album** : This album marked a significant commercial success for Kravitz, climbing to No. 12 on the album chart and becoming his first LP to go platinum immediately. It featured numerous riff rockers and demonstrated Kravitz's ability to cross over at multiple radio formats. "The are you gonna go my way? Album climbed to number 12 and became the first Kravitz LP to go platinum right away."
**Circus (1995) - Post-Divorce Album** : Released in 1995, this album's singles struggled to reach the top 40, and the album itself only went gold, suggesting a decline in Kravitz's chart success at the time. The album was written during a period of personal difficulty for Kravitz, following the end of his marriage and his mother's battle with cancer. "On the charts, neither single from Lenny's Circus album came close to the top 40, and the album only went gold, suggesting that Kravitz's hitmaking days were on the Wayne."
**Five (1998) - Experimental Resurgence** : Kravitz's fifth album, released in 1998, saw him experimenting with sequencers and samplers, techniques he hadn't used extensively since his work on "Justify My Love." Initially, the album performed weakly, muddling around the middle of the album chart for months and taking half a year to go gold. Critical reviews were also unkind, with All Music calling his pastiches "a bore." However, the late inclusion and promotion of the track "Fly Away" revitalized the album, leading it to a new peak of No. 28 and double platinum certification. A 1999 reissue with the bonus track "American Woman" extended its chart life significantly, making it his longest-running album on the Billboard 200 for 110 weeks. "The Five Album, opened weekly, muddled around the middle of the album chart for months, and took half a year to even go gold."
**Greatest Hits (2000) - Chart Topping Compilation** : Released in the fall of 2000, this compilation debuted at No. 2, becoming Kravitz's highest-charting album ever. It included a new romantic song, "Again," which demonstrated Kravitz's developed sound, combining Beatlesque classic rock with neo-soul. The success of "Again" propelled the album to triple platinum sales by February 2001. "Greatest Hits debuted all the way up at number two, Lenny's highest charting album ever, and like Madonna on the Immaculate Collection a decade earlier, Lenny anchored his Hits compilation with a romantic new song."
**Lenny (2001) - Platinum Album** : Released in 2001, this album continued Kravitz's success in the new millennium. It featured the Top 40 hit "Dig In" and achieved platinum certification. "Dig in from 2001's Lenny album Cracked the Top 40 at No. 31 and the album went platinum."

## songs
**In My Life (Mid-60s)** : This Beatles song was Leonard Albert Kravitz's favorite as a toddler and would later be played at his wedding. "this song in particular, in my life, would later play at Lenny's wedding."
**Give Peace a Chance (1969)** : John Lennon's anti-war anthem, which Lenny Kravitz's mother took him to hippie rallies where protesters harmonized on the song. "Kravitz recalls his mother taking him to hippie rallies where throngs of protesters harmonized on Lenny's anti-war anthem give peace a chance."
**Black Dog (1971)** : A Led Zeppelin track that caused Lenny Kravitz's "head exploded" when he first heard it in junior high school, indicating a significant influence on his rock music taste. "Kravitz said his, quote, head exploded in junior high school the first time he heard Zeppelin's black dog."
**I Build This Garden For Us (Late 80s)** : An introspective, groovy, and trippy song inspired by Kravitz's bohemian existence with Lisa Bonet. It later peaked at No. 25 on the modern rock chart. "Their bohemian existence was inspiring Lenny Kravitz to write more introspective, groovy, hippie adjacent music like the trippy I build this garden for us."
**Flower Child (Late 80s)** : A smitten ode written by Lenny Kravitz to Lisa Bonet during their early relationship, reflecting their bohemian lifestyle. "Or his smitten ode to Bonay flower child."
**Let Love Rule (1989)** : The title track from his debut album, it reached No. 5 on Billboard's modern rock chart and No. 23 on the album rock chart by December 1989. Critics immediately noted its Beatles-esque sound, particularly its chord progression, which was similar to "Hey Jude." It only reached a lowly No. 89 on the Hot 100. "Beatles fans noticed right away that Let Love Rule, the song, was built out of the same chord progression as He Jude."
**Mr. Cab Driver (1989)** : Featured on "Let Love Rule," this track was an "Urbane Hendrix reboot," showcasing Kravitz's admiration for Jimi Hendrix's guitar work. It became a No. 50 mainstream rock hit. "Into the Urbane Hendrix reboot, Mr. Cab Driver, a number 50 mainstream rock hit."
**Funky Drummer (1970)** : A James Brown track featuring Clyde Stubblefield's drum break, which became a monster sample in early hip hop, notably by Public Enemy in 1988 for their track "Security of the First World." This beat inspired Kravitz's production work for Madonna. "The monster drum break by Clyde Stubblefield on James Brown's 1970 track, Funky Drummer."
**Security of the First World (1988)** : An instrumental track by militant rap group Public Enemy, which slowed down the "Funky Drummer" beat, influencing Lenny Kravitz's later production work. "In 1988, militant rap group public enemy slowed the Funky Drummer beat way down for their loathing instrumental track, Security of the First World."
**Justify My Love (1990)** : A number one smash hit for Madonna, co-written and produced by Lenny Kravitz with Andre Betz and Ingrid Chavez. The song originated from a poem by Chavez, paired with a James Brown-influenced hip-hop beat and evocative synthesizers. Famous for its MTV-banned, sex-positive, queer-friendly video, it topped the Hot 100 in January 1991 and was sold as a video single on VHS. It remains Kravitz's only number one pop hit as a writer and producer. "Perhaps you didn't realize Lenny Kravitz of all people, co-wrote and produced Madonna's number one smash, Justify My Love."
**Give Peace a Chance (1991) - The Peace Choir Remake** : Kravitz was the brainchild behind this 1991 remake of John Lennon's anti-war anthem, created as a benefit for Gulf War victims and a protest against the war. He teamed with Yoko Ono and Sean Ono Lennon, with Kravitz and Sean opening the video playing guitar and Kravitz being one of the first voices heard on the recording. Credited to The Peace Choir, it featured over 40 eclectic acts and peaked at No. 54 on the Hot 100. "1991's remake of John Lenny's Give Peace a Chance, a benefit for Gulf War victims and and implied protest against that war, was the brainchild of Lenny Kravitz."
**Always on the Run (1991)** : The lead single from "Mama Said," this track was a savvy blend of funk rock, recalling Sly and the Family Stone, with a primary riff played by Guns N' Roses' Slash. Slash, who is biracial like Kravitz, spoke highly of playing more funk on this track than in his usual work. The song peaked at No. 8 on the modern rock chart and No. 40 on the album rock chart in spring 1991. "Always on the run, was perhaps Lenny Kravitz's savviest single yet, rebooting and blending multiple strains of rock history."
**It Ain't Over Till It's Over (1991)** : A 70s fluttery slow jam written by Kravitz as a plea to his then-foundering marriage with Lisa Bonet. Its string arrangement, sitar solo, and falsetto vocal evoked peak Philly Soul (Spinners, Stylistics) and Earth Wind & Fire's "That's the Way of the World." This song was a pop and R&B breakthrough, climbing to No. 2 on the Hot 100 in August 1991 and No. 10 on the R&B chart a month later. It was ahead of its time, initiating a 20-year nostalgia cycle for 70s culture. "Written as a plea by Kravitz to wife Lisa Bonet as their marriage was foundering, it ain't over till it's over was Lenny in 70's Fluttery Slow Jam mode."
**Stand by My Woman (1991)** : A Lennon-esque minor pop follow-up hit from the "Mama Said" album. "The Mama said album generated a minor pop follow-up hit with the Lennon-esque stand by my woman."
**Are You Gonna Go My Way (1993)** : This galvanizing riff rocker became Kravitz's bid for "air guitar immortality," built on a riff worthy of Jimi Hendrix. Critics were overwhelmingly positive, with one calling it a "rollicking fast-paced rock masterpiece." Its music video, directed by Mark Romanoch, presented Kravitz as a guitar icon and won an MTV Video Music Award. It topped the album rock chart (No. 1) and reached No. 2 on the modern rock chart in early summer 1993, a rare feat for a Black artist on mainstream rock radio. "Are you gonna go my way? Was Lennie Kravitz's bid for air guitar immortality built out of a riff worthy of Jimmy Hendrix?"
**Is There Any Love In Your Heart? (1993)** : A riff rocker from the "Are You Gonna Go My Way" album, which became a Top 20 album rock hit. "Kravitz filled the album with riff rockers like is there any love in your heart? Top 20 album rock hit."
**Believe (1993)** : An anthemic track from the "Are You Gonna Go My Way" album, whose melody was compared to The Beatles and its string arrangement to Derek and the Dominos' "Layla." It returned Kravitz to the modern rock top 10 and reached No. 60 on the Hot 100. "And the anthemic belief whose melody was once again compared to the Beatles and its string Hoda to Derek and the Domino's Laila."
**Heaven Help (1993)** : A melancholy soul ballad that cracked the lower rungs of both the Hot 100 and the R&B chart. Six years later, it was covered by Neo Soul singer Angie Stone. "His melancholy soul ballad heaven help. Kravitz cracked the lower rungs of both the hot 100 and the R&B chart."
**Rock and Roll is Dead (1995)** : The lead single from the "Circus" album, despite its snarky, tongue-in-cheek title, it cracked the top 10 on both mainstream and modern rock charts. Ironicially, its riff was borrowed from Led Zeppelin's "Heartbreaker." "He kicked off his 1995 album Circus with the tongue-in-cheek Rock and Roll is dead, which despite its snarky title cracked the top 10 at both mainstream and modern rock stage."
**Can't Get You Off My Mind (1996)** : A country rock track that became a No. 62 pop hit, combining Kravitz's riffage with a "lonely highway" lyric. He wrote it while grieving the 1993 end of his marriage to Lisa Bonet and his mother, Roxy Roker's, fight with breast cancer (she died in December 1995). "Kravitz was seriously wounded when he wrote the song, as he was still grieving the 1993 end of his marriage to Lisa Bonay and watching his mother, Roxy Roker, lose her fight with breast cancer."
**If You Can't Say No (1998)** : The lead single from the "Five" album, echoing the Trip Hop sound of Madonna's "Justify My Love." It only managed to reach No. 39 on the modern rock chart. "The lead single, if you can't say no, echoed that Madonna hits, trip hop sound, but only managed to reach number 39 on the modern rock chart."
**Fly Away (1998)** : A funk rocker added to the "Five" album at the last minute, fusing the sound of Parliament Funkadelic with classic rock riffage and a simple pop chorus. It reintroduced Kravitz to both rock and pop audiences, taking several months to climb the charts before simultaneously hitting No. 1 on both mainstream and modern rock charts by November 1998, his only single to do so. It crossed over to top 40 radio, featured on the first "Now That's What I Call Music" CD, and peaked at No. 12 on the Hot 100 in May 1999, becoming his first major pop hit since 1991. "Buried on the disc's back half, it was a funk rocker, fusing the sound of Prime Parliament Funkadelic with classic rock riffage and the simple Sing Along pop chorus."
**American Woman (1999) - Cover Version** : A straight-up cover of The Guess Who's 1970 number one hit, recorded as a bonus track for the soundtrack to the second Austin Powers movie and re-issued on the "Five" CD. Kravitz retained its crunchy riff while adding a late 90s sheen. This cover hit the top 10 on both rock charts and significantly boosted "Five" to 110 weeks on the Billboard 200. "American Woman was a number one hit in 1970 for Canadian band The Guess Who. Lenny did a straight-up cover of the track, retaining its crunchy riff and adding the same late 90s sheen he'd added to Fly Away."
**Again (2000)** : A new romantic song included on Kravitz's "Greatest Hits" compilation, showcasing his evolved sound as its own genre, blending Beatlesque classic rock and neo-soul without being indebted to one specific style. By February 2001, it rose to No. 4 on the Hot 100, driving the compilation to triple platinum sales. "The heart felt again, was the single that showed Lenny Kravitz' music had finally become its own genre."
**Dig In (2001)** : A track from the 2001 "Lenny" album that cracked the Top 40, peaking at No. 31, contributing to the album's platinum certification. "Dig in from 2001's Lenny album Cracked the Top 40 at No. 31 and the album went platinum."
**Storm (2004)** : A hip-hop collaboration with rapper Jay-Z, which appeared in the top half of Billboard's R&B Hip Hop chart. "In 2004, Kravitz tried his hand again at Hip Hop, teeming with rapper Jay-Z on Storm, which made the top half of Billboard's R&B Hip Hop chart."
**Lady (2005)** : A riff rock song that gave Kravitz one more Top 40 pop hit, peaking at No. 27. "And in 2005, a return to riff rock on Lady gave Kravitz one more top 40 pop hit, peaking at No. 27."
**I'll Be Waiting (2008)** : A No. 73 hit on the Hot 100, which would turn out to be Lenny Kravitz's last appearance on that chart. "In 2008, Justice Kravitz was making the Hot 100 for what would turn out to be the last time with the No. 73 hit, I'll be waiting."

**Musical Emulator and Flower-Child Hippie** : From the start of his career in the early 90s, Kravitz was known for openly emulating his musical heroes from prior generations. He was seen as a "flower-child hippie" for Generation X, a vibe that predated its mainstream coolness. "From the start of his career at the turn of the 90s, Crabbit's was an open emulator of his musical heroes from a prior generation."
**"Rip-Off Artist" Criticism** : Kravitz frequently faced criticism, often being "tarred with the insult rip off artist more than once" due to his overt musical borrowings, a criticism that stuck to him more than to artists like Billy Joel. "Kravitz in particular was tarred with the insult rip off artist more than once."
**Biracial Background and Racial Essentialism** : Kravitz is biracial, born to a white NBC TV news producer father, Sy Kravitz, and a Black theater actress mother, Roxy Roker. The criticism he and Bruno Mars received for stylistic borrowing, compared to Billy Joel, suggested issues of "racism, or at the very least racial essentialism," implying artists of color were expected to "stick to their musical lane." "The product of a biracial couple Lenny was born to sigh Kravitz, a white NBC TV news producer, and Roxy Roker, a black theater actress who won a claim on the show."
**Deep Familial Showbiz Foundation** : Kravitz had a profound family background in showbiz. His mother, Roxy Roker, was an acclaimed theater actress known for her role as Helen Willis on CBS sitcom "The Jeffersons." His father, Sy Kravitz, was an NBC TV news producer with an extensive network of contacts, befriending jazz greats like Ella Fitzgerald, Bobby Short, and Duke Ellington. "Speaking of his mom, Kravitz was steeped in showbiz from a very young age."
**Duke Ellington Birthday Serenade** : At the request of his parents, Sy and Roxy, legendary bandleader Duke Ellington led the Duke Ellington band in a "rousing happy birthday" for young Lenny one night at the Rainbow Room. "Who, at sigh and Roxy's request, led the Duke Ellington band through a rousing happy birthday for young Lenny one night at the Rainbow Room."
**Jackson Five Concert - Formative Experience** : In his memoir, "Let Love Rule," Kravitz recounts his father surprising him with tickets to a Jackson Five concert, an experience he called "formative" for both their music and "big afrode style." "In his memoir, Kravitz talks rapturously about the time his father surprised him with tickets to a concert by the Jackson Five, whom Kravitz called, quote, formative, both for their music and for their big afrode style."
**Inspired by Prince's Self-Sufficiency** : By high school, Kravitz was deeply inspired by Prince, whom he called "the me I wanted to be," particularly impressed that Prince handled all his own writing, most of the playing, and even his own production, a model Kravitz would emulate. "Kravitz was especially impressed that prince did all of his own writing, most of the playing, and even his own production. A model Lenny would later emulate."
**Early Sound Comparison to Prince and John Lennon** : Friends in the 80s told Kravitz that his early compositions sounded like Prince crossed with John Lennon, specifically the contemplative, lo-fi Lennon from his post-Beatles "Plastic Ono Band" period. "friends told him he sounded like prince crossed with John Lennon, specifically the contemplative lo file Lenin of his post Beatles period on the plastic Ono band LP."
**Romeo Blue - Early Stage Name** : In the 1980s, while trying to break into music in LA, Kravitz adopted the stage name "Romeo Blue" and even wore blue contact lenses for an exotic look. His mother, Roxy Roker, expressed bemusement about his chosen path on "The Mike Douglas Show." "Speaking of name changes when Lenny Kravitz began hustling his music in LA in the 80s he renamed himself Romeo Blue."
**Rejected Major Label Deals** : As Romeo Blue and later as Lenny Kravitz, he turned down multiple record deals in the 80s, including one from A&M Records that wanted to turn him and other musicians into a "Black Durand Durand," and another from Capital Records for an electro-pop band. He found these offers uninspiring due to the major labels' unwillingness to invest in his preferred music styles. "In fact Lenny Kravitz turned down several deals in the 80s. Uninspired by the kinds of music the major labels were willing to invest in."
**Lisa Bonet's Influence - Return to True Self** : In 1987, his new girlfriend, actress Lisa Bonet (known for "The Cosby Show" and "A Different World"), encouraged him to revert to "Lenny Kravitz" and hold out for a solo record deal that would allow him to write, produce, and play all instruments himself, much like Prince. They eloped in 1987 and had their daughter, Zoe Kravitz. "By 1987 the would be Romeo Blue had changed his name back to Lenny Kravitz at the encouragement of his new girlfriend actress Lisa Bonay."
**Record Label Rejections - "Not Black Enough, Not White Enough"** : His self-produced demos were rejected by numerous labels, with some criticizing his music for "wasn't black enough" while others said it was "not white enough." Virgin America eventually signed him, appreciating his "Prince meets John Lennon" sound. "Some said Kravitz's music wasn't black enough, others not white enough."
**Ingrid Chavez Lawsuit for "Justify My Love" Credit** : Several years after its release, Ingrid Chavez sued and won songwriting credit for Madonna's "Justify My Love." She claimed Kravitz initially hid her contributions, fearing his wife Lisa Bonet would suspect an affair between them. "By the way, several years later, Ingrid Shavez sued for and won songwriting credit on Justify My Love."
**MTV Video Music Award for "Are You Gonna Go My Way" (1993)** : The music video for "Are You Gonna Go My Way," directed by Mark Romanoch, which featured Kravitz as a guitar icon in a multi-tiered rotunda, won an MTV Video Music Award later that year. "Later that year, it would take home an MTV Video Music Award."
**Racial Context of "Are You Gonna Go My Way" Chart Success** : The song's climb to No. 1 on the album rock chart in 1993 was improbable at a time when songs by Black artists rarely topped mainstream rock radio playlists. "At a time when songs by black artists rarely topped mainstream rock radio playlists, Kravitz's, are you gonna go my way? Did the improbable?"
**Mother's Death and Songwriting Inspiration** : Kravitz was "seriously wounded" when he wrote "Can't Get You Off My Mind" (1996), as he was grieving the 1993 end of his marriage to Lisa Bonet and watching his mother, Roxy Roker, lose her fight with breast cancer. Roker died in December 1995. "Kravitz was seriously wounded when he wrote the song, as he was still grieving the 1993 end of his marriage to Lisa Bonay and watching his mother, Roxy Roker, lose her fight with breast cancer."
