## albums
**Touch (1989) - Debut LP** : Released in 1989, Sarah McLachlan's debut album only reached number 132 on the Billboard album chart. It eventually went gold, but only about ten years after release. Her first five years were a kind of beta test, a series of experiments toward finding her eventual sound. "It would eventually go gold, but not for another decade."
**Solace (1992) - Sophomore Album and Canadian Breakthrough** : Her second album blended the driving beats and folk songwriting of her debut with bass-driven guitar rock, bringing her closer to her eventual sound. In Canada it made her a star, reaching the Canadian top 20 and going double platinum. In America, despite a sizeable alt-rock hit with "Into the Fire," it charted even lower than Touch. "In her native Canada, Solis made Sarah a star."
**Fumbling Towards Ecstasy (1994) - Third Album and Sleeper Hit** : Made with producer Pierre Marchand, this was the album where McLachlan found the sound that made her a US star. It never peaked higher than number 50 on the Billboard 200. It still went platinum in early 1995, nearly a year after release, and double platinum by summer 1996, carried by the slow-building "Possession" and further modern rock hits "Good Enough" and "Hold On." "Fumbling towards ecstasy just kept quietly selling."

## songs
**Steaming (1989) - First US Chart Appearance** : McLachlan's first appearance on an American singles chart was not on the Hot 100 or Modern Rock Tracks. A remix of "Steaming" reached number 37 on Billboard's Club Play chart. "a remix of her song, Steaming, reached number 37."
**Into the Fire (1992) - Modern Rock Top Five** : The lead single from Solace brought McLachlan into the modern rock top five, peaking at number four in March 1992. "where it peaked at number four in March of 92."
**Possession (1994) - Song Inspired by a Stalker** : The first track and lead single from Fumbling Towards Ecstasy. McLachlan wrote it around the ravings of a real-life stalker who had been sending her deranged letters, which made it darker than the average love song despite its passionate intensity. Its pull came from the aching lyrics and its "high-lonesome," cavernous sound. It peaked at number four on modern rock in 1994 but had unusual staying power: pop stations picked it up slowly and some were still playing it in 1995, even as she released new singles. "McLaughlin wrote possession around the ravings of a real-life stalker who'd been sending her deranged letters."
**Good Enough and Hold On (1994-1995) - Mid-Charting Follow-Ups** : These were further singles from Fumbling Towards Ecstasy that became mid-charting modern rock hits while "Possession" was still being played on pop radio. "Even as McLaughlin issued new singles, like the mid-sharding modern rock hits Good Enough and Hold On."
**Building a Mystery (1997) - Debut the Week Lilith Fair Opened** : "Building a Mystery" debuted on Billboard's Modern Rock chart in the week ending July 5, 1997. That same week, Lilith Fair played its first show at the Gorge Amphitheatre in Washington State. One month later McLachlan had the top single-artist album in the US while Lilith sold out venues nationwide, which was the culmination of a decade of female success in pop. "when building a mystery by Sarah McLaughlin made its debut on Billboard's Modern Rock Chart."

## curiosities
**A Stealth Rock Star, Not a Megastar** : When McLachlan conceived an all-female festival, she was not a megastar. Asked in 1995 or 1996 to guess which woman would mount such a tour, most people would have named one of several multi-platinum chart dominators instead. "she was a kind of stealth rock star."
**Lilith Born From Writer's Block** : In mid-1996 McLachlan was struggling to write the follow-up to Fumbling Towards Ecstasy, and the idea for the shows was more a distraction than a brainstorm. She later told Billboard that she liked the idea of doing a few shows but did not want a whole tour, and her manager Terry McBride suggested playing with artists she had shared bills with before. By McBride's account, McLachlan then challenged him and the promoters to book only female acts for this short run of gigs in summer 1996. "I was having writer's block."
**1996 Prototype Shows** : McLachlan's team booked her with Paula Cole, whom she already knew and had played alongside, plus veteran Suzanne Vega, who was touring her own latest album, and Lisa Loeb. This four-woman lineup debuted in Clarkston, Michigan, just outside Detroit, on June 14, 1996. They played to a packed house. After that, the same lineup played in August at LA's Starlight Bowl and the Bay Area's Greek Theater. "they played to a packed house of more than 10,000 fans."
**Coining the Name Lilith in Vancouver** : At a September 1996 gig in Vancouver, British Columbia, McLachlan coined the name "Lilith" for the multi-artist show, after the female figure in Jewish and Babylonian mythology. That Vancouver gig was the first to carry the name. Within a year the name was applied to a much larger festival that beat everyone's expectations, including McLachlan's. "named for the female figure in Jewish and Babylonian mythology who supposedly spurned Adam the first man."
**Producer Pierre Marchand** : McLachlan worked with producer Pierre Marchand. After two more years and a sleeper hit, the two of them arrived at the sound that made her a US star. "It would take two more years and a sleeper hit, for McLaughlin and her producer Pierre Marte Shond, to find the sound that would make her a US star."

## songs
**Woodstock (1970) - Song About a Festival She Missed** : Joni Mitchell wrote and first recorded "Woodstock," released a few months after the festival in early 1970, about a concert she never went to. Her boyfriend at the time, Graham Nash, attended and described the experience to her, which inspired the song. The hit version belonged to Nash's band, Crosby, Stills, Nash & Young, not to her. "Mitchell was writing about a concert she herself never attended."

## curiosities
**Skipping Woodstock on David Geffen's Advice** : Mitchell had been invited to play Woodstock but did not even travel to Upstate New York, on the advice of her manager David Geffen; the decision is now infamous. In her own words, "the boys" (CSNY) were taken in, but she was told she could not go because of a television show the next day, since there was a chance they could get in but no clear way to get out. She felt the festival was an amazing event and "as good as it was going to get." "I was told I couldn't go because I had to do a television show the next day."
**Voice of a Generation, Kept From Its Defining Event** : The episode presents this as a metaphor: a woman who would become the voice of her generation was pressured into missing its signature live music event. Then her song about it became a hit for a group of men who were never discouraged from attending. "A woman who would become the voice of her generation was pressured into missing the signature live music event of her generation."
**Influence on Later Artists** : Mitchell scored gold and platinum albums throughout the rest of the 1970s and inspired dozens of male and female performers. Emmylou Harris, Chrissie Hynde and Prince all spoke of her influence. "Everyone from Emileu Harris to Chrissy Hind to Prince would testify to Joni's influence in the decades to come."
**Spiritual Godmother of Lilith Fair** : Mitchell never played Lilith Fair, but she served as a kind of spiritual godmother to the festival. Her sound was also all over college radio and MTV in the second half of the 1980s through the neo-folk wave. "she served as a kind of spiritual godmother to the festival."

# concert - Lilith Fair

## curiosities
**First All-Women Touring Festival (1997)** : In early summer 1997, a team of women launched a traveling festival of all women and women-fronted bands. It went against the conventional wisdom that no tour should have more than one or two women on the bill. Founded by Canadian singer-songwriter Sarah McLachlan, it was the highest-grossing touring festival of the year on its very first run. Its main stage acts were all certified gold, and the shows sold out. "no concert tour should have more than one or two women on the bill."
**First Show at the Gorge** : Lilith Fair played its first show at the Gorge Amphitheatre in Washington State in the week ending July 5, 1997, the same week "Building a Mystery" debuted on the Modern Rock chart. "The same week her Lilith Fair festival played its first show at the Gorge Amphitheater in Washington State."
**Expansion to R&B and Hip-Hop** : When Lilith expanded its roster to include artists at the forefront of R&B and hip-hop, those women were platinum sellers too. "those women were platinum sellers too."
**The Irony of Lilith's Timing** : At the moment Lilith was selling out venues nationwide, radio, especially rock radio, was turning away from women performers. The festival was the culmination of a decade of women's success in 90s rock, but it came closer to the end of that era than the beginning. "Rock Radio in particular was pivoting away from women performers."
**Origin of the Name** : The name comes from Lilith, the female figure in Jewish and Babylonian mythology who supposedly rejected Adam, the first man. McLachlan coined it at a September 1996 gig in Vancouver. "named for the female figure in Jewish and Babylonian mythology who supposedly spurned Adam the first man."

# genre - Neo-Folk

## curiosities
**Late-80s Folk Revival** : In the second half of the 1980s, more than a decade after the folk and singer-songwriter movements peaked, a new wave of folk-inspired performers emerged on college radio and MTV. The songs were written on traditional guitars, but the recordings drew on new wave rock and sometimes used synthesizers. There were male performers too (Billy Bragg, Peter Case, Freedy Johnston), but the strongest and most commercially successful voices tended to be women. "the music was acoustic but not acetic."

# genre - Alternative Rock

## curiosities
**An Unusually Egalitarian Era** : The first five to ten years of alt-rock's reign were unusually even between men and women, and women competed effectively on radio and on the charts. The rise of commercial alternative stations alongside college radio gave women room to experiment. "an exceptionally egalitarian time, gender-wise."
**Grunge Reasserts the Male Rock Star** : Alternative music moved from post-punk and folk-rock toward the heavier sound of grunge. Even when the first wave of Seattle bands openly supported feminist ideals, those bands restored the male rock star to the top. "these bands reasserted the primacy of male rockstar."
**Men Still Outsold Women** : Through the early-to-mid-90s peak, men still sold more albums and had more radio hits. Women's smashes like Tuesday Night Music Club or Yes I Am sold six or seven million copies, while blockbusters like Pearl Jam's Ten or Green Day's Dookie were selling 8 to 10 million by the mid-90s. "men were still selling more albums and scoring more radio hits."
**Crossovers Push Women Off Alt Radio** : Once alt-rock-leaning women could score huge Hot 100 hits, alternative programmers no longer felt obliged to play them next to Pearl Jam, Bush or the Red Hot Chili Peppers. By 1996-97, women-led acts were more likely to break through with pop smashes. "alternative radio programmers no longer felt as obligated to spin them."

**Launch of Billboard's Modern Rock Tracks** : Until mid-1988 Billboard had no chart for the college rock scene. Two weeks after Tracy Chapman and "Fast Car" peaked, the magazine launched Modern Rock Tracks in September 1988. The week it launched, nearly half the chart was women-fronted, and neo-folk was all over it alongside post-punk. "The week the chart launched, nearly half the chart was women-fronted."
**Album Rock Tracks' Few Female Number Ones** : In the decade before 1988, Billboard's Album Rock Tracks chart had only been topped by a small handful of women-led acts, including Joan Jett, Heart and the Pretenders. "had only been topped by a small handful of women-led acts."
**Village Voice Pazz & Jop - Three Straight Female Winners** : For three straight years, the Village Voice's influential Pazz & Jop critics' poll was led by an iconoclastic female rocker: Liz Phair in 1993, Hole in 1994 and PJ Harvey in 1995. All three albums took apart and reinvented male-dominated tropes, and all three acts had modern rock top 10 hits. "all three of these women's albums deconstructed and reinvented previously male-dominated troops."
