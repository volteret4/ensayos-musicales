# curiosity

**Early Music Exposure in Brooklyn (Late 1970s)** : In the late 1970s, in a two-family house in Brooklyn, New York, the narrator and his sister were exposed to a lot of music by his three teenage cousins (Rosemary, Josephine, and LaRetta) who lived downstairs and played records loudly, including artists like KC and the Sunshine Band, the Grease Soundtrack, the Bee Gees, and Donna Summer. "I grew up in a two-family house in Brooklyn, New York. In the late 70s, my three teenage cousins, who lived with my aunt and uncle in the downstairs apartment, played records loud enough to be heard upstairs, meckoning my little sister and me to come downstairs and dance."
**Solid Gold TV Show (1980-1988)** : "Solid Gold," a syndicated musical variety show, premiered in early 1980 as a one-off special named "Solid Gold 79," counting down the top songs of the prior year (Rod Stewart's "Do You Think I'm Sexy?" was #1). It became a weekly show later in 1980, featuring a countdown accompanied by "The Solid Gold Dancers." The show's countdown was based on "Radio and Records" magazine charts, but producers sometimes "fudged" positions based on artist availability for lip-syncing, like Laura Branigan. It ran through 1988. "In early 1980, a syndicated musical variety show called Solid Gold, premiered on television."
**Historical Countdown Shows** : Long before Solid Gold, countdowns existed on radio (like "Your Hit Parade" from the 1930s) and TV (like "Your Hit Parade" in the 1950s), as well as on music and dance shows like "American Bandstand" and "Soul Train," though these didn't make the countdown the sole focus as Solid Gold did. "Little did I know that countdowns had been on the radio and television for decades."
**America's Top 10 TV Show (1981)** : "America's Top 10," hosted by Casey Kasem, was a television program providing "exclusive reviews of the Top 10 on Billboard's pop, country and album charts" for the week ending October 4, 1981. "From Hollywood, it's America's number one pop music show, America's Top 10 with Casey Caso."
**American Top 40 Radio Show (1981-1984)** : This radio show, hosted by Casey Kasem (sometimes Charlie Van Dyke), provided countdowns of the "official Billboard survey." The narrator became hooked on it in June 1981 at age 9, understanding it was a weekly feature by Christmas Day 1983. By February 1984, it became a weekly ritual, influencing his schedule. "On the radio, it was American Top 40."
**Year-End Chart Listening Ritual (1983)** : The narrator, at age 12, stumbled upon the year-end countdown of the top 100 songs of 1983 on Christmas Day while gathered with relatives at his Aunt Rose's basement. Despite holiday distractions, he was glued to the radio, observing family favorites like Laura Branigan's "Gloria" and Donna Summer's "She Works Hard for the Money" being counted down. "It wasn't until two years later, when at age 12, I stumbled upon the year-end countdown of the top 100 songs of 1983 on Christmas Day, that I understood this countdown was a regular feature on the airwaves."
**Cassette Taping from Radio (1984)** : In 1984, the narrator started keeping a cassette tape by his living room radio to record his favorite songs as they were counted down on American Top 40, including hits by Genesis, Cyndi Lauper, Shannon, John Lennon, and Duran Duran. "I started keeping a cassette tape by the radio in my living room to tape my favorite songs as they were counted down."
**The Billboard Book of Top 40 Hits (1987)** : While listening to classic rock on AOR radio, the narrator would try to remember song titles and then consult this reference volume at home to see if they had ever charted, as these classic rock tracks weren't current hits featured in Billboard magazine. "I would instead try to remember the song titles, go home after work and check my reference volume, the Billboard book of Top 40 hits, to see if they'd ever charted."
**Leola's Musical Influence (1990)** : While working at the Dürfee Sweet Shop at Yale in the fall of 1989, the narrator befriended his night shift boss, Leola, who by 1990 introduced him to country music, a genre he had not deeply explored before. "I befriended my night shift boss, Leola, who by 1990 turned me onto the one major genre of music I hadn't explored in depth, country music."
**Music and Charts at Yale (Early 1990s)** : During college at Yale in the early 1990s, the narrator DJ'd dances, co-edited an arts magazine and a music magazine, and became a "charts reference" for his schoolmates. He observed that while his schoolmates pretended not to care, they would gravitate towards Billboard magazine when he brought it to music magazine meetings. "College was a melting pot of music. I DJed several dances, co-edited an arts magazine and a music magazine, and served as a charts reference for my schoolmates."
**Generation X Cultural Takeover (1992)** : The moment Nirvana's "Nevermind" replaced Michael Jackson's "Dangerous" at number one on the Billboard 200 album chart in early 1992 was perceived as a significant cultural shift, with Generation X finally "taking over the culture" after a "long boomer hangover." "It felt like our generation after a long boomer hangover was finally taking over the culture."
**Gen X's Moment Ends (Late 1990s)** : A couple of years after 1995, the period where Generation X was "the sun around which popular culture orbited" concluded, as Alt Rock faded from pop airwaves and the larger millennial generation began to dominate the charts. "A couple of years after my Charlotte'sville adventure, Gen X's moment as the sun around which popular culture orbited was over. As Altrock departed the pop airwaves and the much larger millennial generation took over the charts."
**Casey Kasem's Legacy** : Casey Kasem is a legendary figure in chart history, described as a "radio savant," "trivia junkie," "cartoon stoner," and a "god to chart nerds everywhere," credited with originally bringing the hits "from coast to coast."
