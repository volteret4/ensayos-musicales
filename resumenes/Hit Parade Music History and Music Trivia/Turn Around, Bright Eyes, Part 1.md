## songs
**Total Eclipse of the Heart (1983)** : This song was America's number one hit in October 1983, marking Bonnie Tyler's biggest hit and only time topping the Billboard Hot 100. It was a master class in melodrama, sung by the raspy-voiced Welsh woman, who had only scored one prior American hit and would never top the charts again. Written and produced by Jim Steinman, it sounded uncannily similar to Air Supply's contemporaneous hit. "America's number one song was a master class in Melodrama."
**Holding Out For A Hero (1984)** : A 1984 hit for Bonnie Tyler, written by Jim Steinman. Despite a relatively pithy title, it was structured like a pop aria and produced to deliver maximum drama and minimal subtlety, characteristic of Steinman's style. "Even when his titles are relatively pithy, like say Bonnie Tyler's 1984 hit Holding Out For A Hero, they are structured like pop arias, produced to deliver maximum drama and minimal subtlety."

## curiosities
**Early Life and Writing Prodigy** : Born in New York City and raised in the Five Towns area of Long Island, Jim Steinman was considered a prodigy, winning a prize for his writing while still in high school. "Born in New York City, and raised in the 5 towns area of Long Island, Steinman was something of a prodigy, winning a prize for his writing while still in high school."
**Theater Focus at Amherst College** : While attending Amherst College in Massachusetts, Steinman devoted his attention to the theater, writing music for Bertolt Brecht productions and directing a production by Beat Poet and Playwright Michael McCleur. His senior project was a rock musical he wrote and starred in, *The Dream Engine*. "Entering Amherst College in Massachusetts, he devoted his attentions to the theater."
**Joseph Papp's Early Recognition** : His 1969 college production of *The Dream Engine* drew unusual acclaim, leading legendary New York theater producer Joseph Papp to purchase the rights to the show for his Public Theater by intermission. Although the play was never produced there, it brought Steinman back to New York after college to work with Papp. "Legendary New York theater producer Joseph Papp came to Massachusetts to see it, and by intermission, he had purchased the rights to the show for his public theater."
**Artistic Vision and Influences** : After college, Steinman secured a publishing deal and a small office near Broadway, where he wrote songs blending opera and rock and roll, aiming for music that was "very heightened and larger than life." He cited influences ranging from Richard Wagner to Little Richard. "Steinman later told Music writer Paul Myers that he was after music that was, quote, very heightened and larger than life."
**Rejected by Clive Davis** : Along with Meat Loaf, Steinman was rejected by legendary executive Clive Davis while pitching their *Neverland* song cycle. Davis, who had just launched his new Arista label, famously told Steinman he couldn't write and Meat Loaf couldn't sing, a memory Steinman still relishes. "Steinman, to this day, still relishes the memory of his rejection by legendary executive Clive Davis, who had just launched his new Ares the label, but told Steinman he couldn't write and that Meatloaf couldn't sing."
**Lord of Excess and Over-the-Top Style** : Known as the "Lord of Excess," Jim Steinman never met a ten-word song title he didn't love, bringing camp theatricality and grand melodrama to pop music. His style is characterized as operatic, bombastic, Wagnerian, even tragic, with lyrically purple and knowingly absurd titles. "Jim Steinman, the self-declared Lord of Access, and the guy who never met a ten-word song title he didn't love."
**Comparison to Phil Spector** : Jim Steinman has often been compared to Phil Spector, with some arguing that Steinman's sound and songwriting style are even more overpowering. While Spector was known for cinematic depth, Steinman's songs are instantly recognizable regardless of the performer. "Jim Steinman has often been compared to Phil Specter, but arguably Steinman has a sound and songwriting style even more overpowering than Specter's."
**Headstrong Artistic Vision** : Steinman was creatively self-assured and headstrong, seeing himself as an artistic visionary, a perception that was validated by his early and sustained success. Unlike Spector, he was not known for pulling guns on artists. "But Steinman certainly was headstrong, he saw himself as an artistic visionary, and in the most basic sense he was right."
**Work with Phantom of the Opera** : Jim Steinman, described as pop's "Phantom of the Opera," actually worked with *The Phantom of the Opera*, indicating a connection to the musical or related artistic endeavors. "And by the way, he even worked with the Phantom of the Opera."
**Inspiration from Bruce Springsteen's *Born to Run*** : After seeing Bruce Springsteen play New York Club the Bottom Line in 1975, just before *Born to Run*'s release, Steinman called Meat Loaf, telling him he had to see Springsteen's next show because it was exactly what they'd been trying to create with their *Neverland* songs, expressing a desire to make an album that sounded like a movie. "I was so impressed with what Bruce was doing, but I could also see how it related to what I was writing. I wanted to make an album that sounded like a movie."
**Praise for Roy Bittan's Piano Playing** : Steinman highly praised Roy Bittan, the keyboard player hired for *Bat Out of Hell* from Bruce Springsteen's E Street Band, calling him "the best pianist I had ever seen in my life" and stating that for his type of music, Bittan was "without parallel." "I thought Roy was the best pianist I had ever seen in my life. Steinman told Todd Rungren biographer Paul Myers. For the kind of music I do, he's without parallel."
**Admiration for Max Weinberg's Drumming** : Jim Steinman was very impressed with Max Weinberg, the drummer from Bruce Springsteen's E Street Band who played on *Bat Out of Hell*, appreciating his "emotionalism" and the way his playing would "speed up," comparing it to a "classical rubata where the conductor controls the flow." "I was very impressed with Max too. I loved the fact that even when Max was rehearsing Bad Out of Hell, it would speed up. Max's emotionalism was my favorite thing about his playing."
**Dialogue from Neverland in Song** : The theatrical and overheated dialogue at the start of "You Took the Words Right Out of My Mouth," where a man asks a woman if she would "offer her throat to the wolf with the red roses," was written by Jim Steinman for his stage show *Neverland*. "That's Jim Steinman, reciting dialogue he wrote for the stage show Neverland."
**Theatrical Direction for Ellen Foley** : Jim Steinman specifically directed singer Ellen Foley to perform her part on the "Randy Teenage Epic," "Paradise by the Dashboard Light," as if she were acting in *West Side Story*, aiming for maximum drama. "Steinman also directed Singer Ellen Foley to sing her part on the Randy Teenage Epic, paradise by the dashboard light, as if she were acting in West Side Story."
**Suits Against Meat Loaf** : Jim Steinman and Meat Loaf eventually sued each other over profits from *Bat Out of Hell* in the wake of the failed *Dead Ringer* album, which led to a cessation of their direct collaboration for nearly a decade. "In the wake of the failed follow-up, Jim Steinman and Meatloaf would wind up suing each other, over profits from Bad Out of Hell, and they stopped collaborating directly for nearly a decade."

## albums
**Bat Out of Hell (1977) - Magnum Opus** : Recording took a few weeks by early 1976 at Bearsville Studios in Woodstock, New York. Todd Rundgren produced, fronted costs, arranged vocals, played guitar, and emulated a revving motorcycle sound with his guitar and amplifier. Jim Steinman wrote all songs, asked Rundgren to emulate vintage Phil Spector sound on "You Took the Words Right Out of My Mouth," and directed Ellen Foley to sing "Paradise by the Dashboard Light" like *West Side Story*. It features E Street Band members Max Weinberg (drums) and Roy Bittan (keyboards). Finding a label took a year; Warner Brothers rejected it. Steve Popovich of Cleveland International Records signed them, distributed by Epic Records (who had originally rejected it). Released in October 1977, it was a slow-grower, debuting at number 185 and charting for half a year. Television appearances, including Meat Loaf's *Saturday Night Live* performance in March 1978 and a 9-minute video on UK's *The Old Grey Whistle Test*, propelled its success. It became one of the longest-charting albums in UK history (522 weeks), trailing only Fleetwood Mac's *Rumours* and Pink Floyd's *Dark Side of the Moon* among studio albums. In the US, it peaked at number 14, was certified platinum in 1978, quadruple platinum in 1984, and diamond by 1994, with 14 million copies sold in America and reportedly over 40 million worldwide. "Bat Out of Hell made stars out of both Meatloaf and Jim Steinman."
**Dead Ringer (1981) - Sophomore Album** : Meat Loaf's ill-fated sophomore album, produced by Jim Steinman (not Todd Rundgren), with all songs penned by Steinman, some dating back years, including the title track from Steinman's 1973 musical *More Than You Deserve*. Released in September 1981, nearly four years after *Bat Out of Hell*, it landed in a new pop era dominated by MTV and new wave. It sported an "outrageously lavish and freaky" album cover and was a near total misfire, peaking at number 45 and being off the album chart before Christmas 1981. "Meat's sophomore album was a near total misfire."
**Midnight at the Lost and Found (1983)** : Meat Loaf's first LP recorded without Jim Steinman, released after the financial and collaborative rupture between the two artists. This album followed a period of legal disputes and Meat Loaf's contractual obligations to Epic and Cleveland International. "Meatloaf's next LP, 1983's Midnight at the Lost and Found, was his first without Steinman."

## curiosities
**Early Life & Nickname** : Born Marvin Lee Aday in 1947 in Dallas, Texas. He reportedly weighed 240 pounds by seventh grade. His stage name, Meatloaf, has various origin stories: either given by his family at four months old or as a nickname on his high school football team. "Born Marvin Lee a day in 1947, he was already going by the name."
**Early Career in Theater and Music** : Prior to meeting Jim Steinman, he had tried his hand at both recording and acting. His stage breakthrough came in the 1968 Los Angeles production of *Hair*. He was also briefly a Motown recording artist, signing to Rare Earth, a subsidiary, as part of the duo Stoney and Meatloaf with his *Hair* cast-mate Shawn Stoney Murphy. "By the early 70s, Meat, that's what most people call him, had already tried his hand at both recording and acting."
**Theatrical Rise with Rocky Horror** : After his stint in Jim Steinman's musical *More Than You Deserve*, Meat Loaf was cast in the first-ever US production of Richard O'Brien's stage musical *The Rocky Horror Show* in Los Angeles, where he originated the role of the undead greaser Eddie. He reprised this role in the 1975 film adaptation, *The Rocky Horror Picture Show*. "Not long after his stint in Steinman's show, he went back to Los Angeles after being cast in the first-ever US production of Richard O'Brien's satire of B-movie sci-fi, the stage musical The Rocky Horror Show."
**Vocal Contribution to Ted Nugent's Album** : During the long delay before *Bat Out of Hell* was finally released, Meat Loaf kept himself busy by recording vocals for Ted Nugent's 1976 album *Free For All*. "Meatloaf kept himself busy by recording vocals for Ted Nugens 1976 album Free For All."
**Nervous Breakdown and Vocal Loss** : Around 1979, exhausted and overwhelmed by his newfound fame following the success of *Bat Out of Hell*, Meat Loaf suffered a nervous breakdown and lost his singing voice for about a year. During this time, he visited psychiatrists to overcome his emotional block, leading Jim Steinman to release "Bad for Good" under his own name. "Exhausted and overwhelmed by his newfound fame, the singer had a nervous breakdown around 1979. And he lost his singing voice for about a year."
**Acting Career Post-Bat Out of Hell** : Following the success of *Bat Out of Hell*, Meat Loaf expanded his acting career, playing a menacing biker in 1979's *Scavenger* and taking the lead role as Raven in 1980's *Roadie*, where he appeared opposite a dozen other music stars, including Alice Cooper. "Meat began acting in more movies, such as playing a menacing biker in 1979's Scavenger. And taking the lead role in 1980's Roading opposite a dozen music stars, including Alice Cooper."
**Legal Disputes with Jim Steinman** : In the wake of the failed follow-up album *Dead Ringer*, Meat Loaf and Jim Steinman wound up suing each other over profits from *Bat Out of Hell*, leading to them stopping direct collaboration for nearly a decade. "In the wake of the failed follow-up, Jim Steinman and Meatloaf would wind up suing each other, over profits from Bad Out of Hell, and they stopped collaborating directly for nearly a decade."

## curiosities
**Wall of Sound Architect** : A legendary and infamous producer and songwriter, Phil Spector is famed for his "Wall of Sound" production technique. He had an incalculable impact on the sound of the artists he produced, making his brand as significant as, or even more significant than, the artists themselves, acting as an "auteur" or director of his material. "That of course is the legendary and infamous producer and songwriter Phil Spector, famed for his wall of sound."
**Conflicts with Artists** : Spector was known for difficult working relationships with artists; for instance, he angered Paul McCartney by adding strings to The Beatles' final number one hit, "The Long and Winding Road," and The Righteous Brothers "heated" working with him on "You've Lost That Lovin' Feelin'," avoiding him for the rest of their career despite him giving them their breakthrough. "He angered Paul McCartney by adding strings to the Beatles final number one hit, The Long and Winding Road."
**Versatility in Production** : Despite his distinctive "Wall of Sound," Spector displayed versatility across genres and artists. While his touches were recognizable, he could reinvent his sound, such as giving The Ramones' *End of the Century* album a "girl group punch" that reinvented his wall of sound for the punk age, demonstrating that his productions didn't always sound like The Ronettes or The Righteous Brothers. "But even Phil Specter came in multiple guises. He angered Paul McCartney by adding strings to the Beatles final number one hit, The Long and Winding Road."
