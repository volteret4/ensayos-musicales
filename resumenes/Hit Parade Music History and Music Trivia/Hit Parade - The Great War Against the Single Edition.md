## curiosities
**Oakland A's Batboy to Rapper** : Prior to his rap career, MC Hammer started as a batboy for the Oakland A's. He also became known for making baggy pants famous. "And it all kicked off with an energetic, boastful, fun-loving single by a guy who started as an Oakland A's Batboy before trying his hand at rap and making baggy pants famous."

## albums
**Please Hammer Don't Hurt 'Em (1990)** : This album was a "chart monster" for MC Hammer, reaching number 1 on the Billboard album chart and staying there for 21 weeks. It was the fastest album to be certified 10 times Platinum (Diamond) by April 1991, achieving this milestone faster than *Born in the U.S.A.*, *Purple Rain*, or *Hysteria*. Its sales in its first year surpassed those of any album by Madonna, Whitney Houston, or Janet Jackson to that point. It achieved its massive success despite "U Can't Touch This" being hampered by a unique single release strategy and having no number 1 singles on the album, with only three of its four singles reaching the top 40. The album's success marked it as the first hip-hop album to reach this level, and it remains the third best-selling single-disc rap album of all time. "Please Hammer Don't Hurt Him was the first hip-hop album of any kind to see this level of success."

## songs
**U Can't Touch This (1990)** : This song was MC Hammer's first pop crossover hit and became ubiquitous, regarded by many as the "song of the summer" in 1990. It heavily sampled Rick James' 1981 R&B hit "Super Freak" and entered the "jock jam and party starter pantheon" instantly upon its spring 1990 release. Despite its widespread popularity and ubiquity, it only peaked at number 8 on the Billboard Hot 100 on June 16, 1990, and didn't even make the top five, an underperformance due to Capital Records' deliberate decision to release it only on a niche 12-inch vinyl format. This strategy aimed to compel consumers to buy the full *Please Hammer Don't Hurt 'Em* album. "You can't touch this, not only didn't top the chart, it didn't even make the top five."
**Have You Seen Her (1990)** : This song was one of four singles issued from the album *Please Hammer Don't Hurt 'Em*, and one of only three to reach the top 40. "Capital did issue 4 singles from Please Hammer Don't Hurt Him, but only 3 were top 40 hits, including Have You Seen Her and Pray."
**Pray (1990)** : This song was one of four singles issued from the album *Please Hammer Don't Hurt 'Em*, and one of only three to reach the top 40. "Capital did issue 4 singles from Please Hammer Don't Hurt Him, but only 3 were top 40 hits, including Have You Seen Her and Pray."

# artist - The Beatles

## genres
Pop
Rock

## labels
Capital Records

## albums
**Rubber Soul (1965)** : This album showed the differing music business practices between the UK and America; in the UK, it was released with "Nowhere Man" as an album track, while in the US, "Nowhere Man" was released as a separate single and omitted from the LP. It was the first major pop album to have no contemporaneous singles pulled from it in either the US or the UK. "Rubber Soul was the first major pop album to have no contemporaneous singles pulled from it in either the US or the UK."
**Yesterday and Today (US-only compilation)** : This US-only compilation album by The Beatles, released by Capitol Records in America, collected tracks including "Nowhere Man." "Capital records in America later collected no-where man on the US only compilation yesterday and today."
**Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967)** : Recorded two years after *Rubber Soul*, this album was considered the "most heralded album of the rock era" and a smash success, spending 15 weeks atop the US album chart (their longest stay in America) and 27 weeks atop the UK chart, becoming England's best-selling studio album ever. It was presented as a coherent artistic piece, with radio stations playing entire sides in one go, and no singles were officially issued from it on either side of the Atlantic. Its importance to the recording industry was immense, as it helped establish the album, rather than the single, as the standard unit of measure for popular music. "Pepper inaugurated the modern era of album length artistic statement being now take for granted today."

## songs
**Nowhere Man (1965)** : This track appeared on The Beatles' 1965 album *Rubber Soul* in the UK. In America, it was released as a single on 45 RPM vinyl and excluded from the US version of the *Rubber Soul* LP, later collected on the US-only compilation *Yesterday and Today*. In the UK, it was not issued as a single at all. "Here in America, no-where man was a single, sold on 45 RPM vinyl, and it was left off the US version of the Rubber Soul LP in 1965."
**Strawberry Fields Forever (1967)** : This song was recorded during the sessions for *Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band* but was pulled and issued as a double-sided single in February 1967, paired with "Penny Lane." The single performed well, topping the Hot 100 in the US. "Recorded for the sessions that would ultimately become Sergeant Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club band, Strawberry Fields Forever was pulled from the sessions and issued as a single in February 1967, paired with another Beatles classic on the flip side, Penny Lane."
**Penny Lane (1967)** : This song was paired with "Strawberry Fields Forever" on a double-sided single released in February 1967. This single performed well on both sides of the Atlantic, reaching number two on the UK singles chart and topping the Hot 100 in the US. "This double-sided single performed well on both sides of the Atlantic, topping the Hot 100 in the US and reaching number two on the UK singles chart."

# curiosity

**The Recording Industry's War on the Single (1990s)** : This was a decade-long campaign by the recording industry, initiated in 1990 by Capitol Records with MC Hammer's "U Can't Touch This," to "bury the single as a retail medium" and compel consumers to purchase entire albums. The strategy involved withholding hit songs from common single formats (like cassette or CD singles) or pulling them from stores once they peaked. This effort went through several phases, including pop-leaning rap (MC Hammer, Vanilla Ice), Grunge/Nouveau Punk bands (Pearl Jam, Soundgarden), and later, more mainstream pop acts (The Rembrandts, Alanis Morissette, Fugees, Tupac Shakur). While it initially led to massive album sales and profits, it ultimately "backfired by decades end" and confused chart followers due to many ubiquitous songs not appearing or underperforming on the Hot 100. "The one-two punch of You Can't Touch This and Ice Ice Baby kicked off a decade-long campaign to bury the single as a retail medium."
**Evolution of Singles vs. Albums in Music Business** : The relationship between singles and albums in the music business underwent significant shifts from the late 1950s to the 1990s. In the late 50s and early 60s, 45 RPM singles were king, outselling albums 2-to-1 and viewed as essential for teenagers. The perception began to change around 1965 with The Beatles' *Rubber Soul*, which was the first major pop album with no contemporaneous singles. By 1967, *Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band* solidified the album as the standard unit of measure, convincing the industry that long-playing albums could sell better than singles. In the 1970s, Album Oriented Rock (AOR) acts sometimes deliberately avoided singles for "badge of honor," while late 70s blockbusters like *Rumours* and *Saturday Night Fever* showed the power of multi-single albums. The 1980s saw the "Thriller model" of milking albums for numerous hits, but by the 1990s, the industry began deliberately withholding singles to force album purchases, a strategy that ultimately backfired. "For the music business, Sergeant Pepper was the killer app. It helped make the album, not the single, the standard unit of measure for popular music."
**Billboard Hot 100 Eligibility Rules** : Since 1958, Billboard rules stated that a song had to be issued as a retail single to be eligible to compete on the Hot 100 chart. This rule was critical during the 1990s when record labels deliberately withheld retail singles (or pulled them from stores) to boost album sales, resulting in many highly popular radio hits not appearing or significantly underperforming on the Hot 100. "Remember, since 1958, Billboard rules stated that a song had to be a retail single to appear on the chart."
**Music Industry Nomenclature for "Single"** : The definition of a "single" has been surprisingly malleable over time. In general industry parlance, it means a song promoted to radio, video channels, clubs, and the public as a potential hit. However, in the pre-digital era (especially in Billboard magazine), a single traditionally meant a "retail single"—a song consumers could buy on its own without purchasing an entire album. In the 21st century, with digital purchasing and streaming, "single" typically refers simply to a song promoted to radio and destined for the charts. "In industry parlance, a single is generally understood to mean a song promoted to radio stations, video channels, clubs, and the public as an emphasis track and a potential hit."
**Retail Single Formats and Chart Annotations (1990)** : In mid-June 1990, Billboard charts used specific annotations for retail single formats. A "C" in parentheses indicated a Cassette Single (Casingle), a popular and affordable format. A "CD" indicated a CD Single, which was slightly more expensive. A "T" indicated a 12-inch vinyl single, a niche product primarily for club DJs since the 1970s disco era, used for extended mixes. By 1990, 12-inch singles were not a major retail product for mainstream consumers. "That billboard footnote was an abbreviation for Cassette Single or Cassingle."

**The Great War Against the Single (1995-1999)** : Starting around 1995, the recording industry deliberately withheld most radio-friendly songs from retailers to force album purchases, a tactic that became known as the "Great War Against the Single." This strategy peaked in 1999 and 2000, coinciding with the compact disc's dominance and maximum industry control over music releases. The war led to consumer resentment, dubbed the "One Good Song period." "This phase of the Great War against the single stoked the greatest consumer resentment."
**The One Good Song Period (Late 1990s)** : This era refers to the late 1990s when, due to labels withholding singles, consumers were compelled to buy full-length CDs for $15-$18 to acquire a single radio hit. This tactic, fueled by a booming US economy, successfully generated multi-platinum albums for acts, including one-hit wonders. "Millions of consumers paid just shy of a Hamilton for full-length CDs by acts with a single radio hit."
**Billboard Hot 100 Rules Change (December 1998)** : At the end of 1998, Billboard finally changed the Hot 100's rules to allow Airplay Only hits to appear on the chart. This move, which Billboard had resisted for years to appease music retailers, acknowledged the business reality that many popular songs were not available as retail singles. The change had a radical effect, allowing previously invisible smashes like Goo Goo Dolls' "Iris" to chart. "At the end of 1998, they finally changed the Hot 100's rules to allow Airplay Only hits to appear on the chart."

Napster initiated the decimation of the music business as it was previously known, acting as a rebellion against the erratic quality, high prices of 90s CDs, and the industry's album-centric model.
