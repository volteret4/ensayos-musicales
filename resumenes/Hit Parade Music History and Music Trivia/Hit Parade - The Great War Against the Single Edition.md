# artist - MC Hammer

## labels

## albums
**Please Hammer Don't Hurt Him (1990) - First Diamond Hip-Hop Blockbuster** : Because Capitol issued "U Can't Touch This" only on niche 12-inch vinyl, most fans who wanted the song had to buy the album on cassette or CD. The album reached No. 1 on the Billboard album chart and stayed there for 21 weeks, nearly half the year, even though it had no No. 1 hits and few hits at all. Capitol issued four singles from it, and only three were Top 40 hits, including "Have You Seen Her" and "Pray." Its 21-week run was longer than any album since Prince's Purple Rain, and longer than any 1980s album except Purple Rain and Thriller. By April 1991, barely a year after release, it was certified 10 times platinum (later called Diamond). That was the fastest any album had reached the 10-million level since multi-platinum awards began in 1984, faster than Born in the USA, Purple Rain and Hysteria. Its first-year sales were higher than any album by Madonna, Whitney Houston or Janet Jackson to that point. It was the first hip-hop album of any kind to reach this level of success. In fall 1990 Vanilla Ice's To the Extreme knocked it out of the No. 1 spot. It remains the third best-selling single-disc rap album ever, behind only two Eminem albums. "Among rap albums, it remains the third best-selling single-disc of all time, behind only a pair of Eminem albums."
**Post-1990 Albums - Steep Commercial Decline** : None of Hammer's albums after 1990 sold more than a fraction of Please Hammer Don't Hurt Him. "None of MC Hammer's albums after 1990 sold much more than a fraction as well as please Hammer don't hurt him."

## songs
**U Can't Touch This (1990) - The Single Released Only on 12-Inch** : This was Hammer's first pop crossover hit. It is built on a prominent sample of Rick James's 1981 R&B hit "Super Freak" and became a jock jam and party starter almost as soon as it arrived in spring 1990. It peaked in June and many considered it the song of the summer, yet it peaked at only No. 8 on the Hot 100 on June 16, 1990. That week Roxette's "It Must Have Been Love" was No. 1. Of that week's Top 40, it was the only song not available on cassingle or CD single. Billboard showed only a "T" (12-inch vinyl) under Hammer's name. Capitol deliberately held it back from popular formats to force album purchases, a move that started a decade-long industry experiment. At least 10 million Americans ended up buying the album to own the song. "Believe it or not, the single underperformed because Hammer's record label wanted it that way."
**Have You Seen Her (1990)** : One of the three Top 40 hits among the four singles Capitol issued from Please Hammer Don't Hurt Him. "Capital did issue 4 singles from Please Hammer Don't Hurt Him, but only 3 were top 40 hits, including Have You Seen Her and Pray."
**Pray (1990)** : Another Top 40 hit from Please Hammer Don't Hurt Him. The album produced few hits overall, compared with 1980s blockbusters that had far more. "Virtually all of the Blockbuster 80s albums I just mentioned had far more hits."

## curiosities
**From Oakland A's Batboy to Rap Star** : Before rapping, Hammer was a batboy for the Oakland A's. He went on to make baggy pants famous. "a guy who started as an Oakland A's Batboy before trying his hand at rap and making baggy pants famous."
**Chapter 11 Bankruptcy - Mid-1990s** : In the mid-1990s Hammer declared Chapter 11 bankruptcy after years of profligate spending. "in the mid 90s, Hammer declared Chapter 11 bankruptcy after years of profligate spending."
**Staples Center Comeback Concert - September 2017** : In early September 2017, more than a quarter century after his peak, Hammer surprised the industry by headlining a concert at the Staples Center in Los Angeles. When the LA Times asked how he could draw such a crowd, he said: "they already know the music. You're not doing a story about a guy trying to make an audience." Few of his vintage hits get much radio play today besides "U Can't Touch This." "My music's already been heard by billions."
**Hip-Hop's First Diamond Pop Blockbuster** : Hip hop dates to the 1970s and rap emerged as a recorded medium around 1980. It took another decade, until Hammer in 1990, for the genre to produce its first diamond-level pop blockbuster. "But it took another decade for hip hop to generate its first diamond level pop blockbuster."

# artist - The Beatles

## members
George Harrison

## labels
Capitol Records

## albums
**Rubber Soul (1965) - No Contemporaneous Singles** : This was the first major pop album with no contemporaneous singles pulled from it in either the US or the UK. "Nowhere Man" was on the UK version but was left off the US LP. In early 1966 Billboard reported that more teenagers were buying albums, and that 1.2 million people bought Rubber Soul in its first nine days, numbers labels like Capitol used to see only for singles. "Rubber Soul was the first major pop album to have no contemporaneous singles pulled from it in either the US or the UK."
**Yesterday and Today - US-Only Compilation** : Capitol in America later collected "Nowhere Man" on this US-only compilation. "Capital records in America later collected no-where man on the US only compilation yesterday and today."
**Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967) - The Industry's Killer App** : It spent 15 weeks atop the US album chart, the longest of any Beatles album in America. It spent 27 weeks atop the UK chart, making it England's best-selling studio album ever. It was presented as a coherent piece: radio stations played whole sides at once, and no singles were issued on either side of the Atlantic. Removing "Penny Lane" and "Strawberry Fields Forever" beforehand made it easier to present the album as an indivisible statement. It made the album, rather than the single, the standard unit of popular music, and labels rebuilt their economics around bundles of songs. "For the music business, Sergeant Pepper was the killer app."

## songs
**Nowhere Man (1965)** : On the UK Rubber Soul it was an album track and was never issued as a single there. In America it was a 45 RPM single, left off the US Rubber Soul and later collected on Yesterday and Today. "Here in America, no-where man was a single, sold on 45 RPM vinyl, and it was left off the US version of the Rubber Soul LP in 1965."
**Strawberry Fields Forever / Penny Lane (1967) - Double-Sided Single** : Recorded during the sessions that became Sgt. Pepper, both songs were pulled and issued as a single in February 1967. It topped the US Hot 100 and reached No. 2 on the UK singles chart. "Strawberry Fields Forever was pulled from the sessions and issued as a single in February 1967, paired with another Beatles classic on the flip side, Penny Lane."
**Here Comes the Sun (1969) - Best-Selling Digital Song** : This George Harrison album cut was never issued as a single, even in the 1960s. When the Beatles put their catalog on iTunes in 2010, it became their best-selling digital song, ahead of "Yesterday" and "Hey Jude." "If the band had released here comes the Sun as a 45 in 1969, it likely would have been a hot 100-topping hit."

## curiosities
**Late Arrival on iTunes - 2010** : The Beatles were among the digital holdouts and did not put their catalog on iTunes until 2010. "when The Beatles put their catalog on iTunes in 2010, their best-selling digital song wasn't one of their big-charts singles like Yesterday or Hey Jude"

# genre - Album-Oriented Rock

## curiosities
**Non-Singles as Respectability** : For some 1970s rock acts it was a mark of respectability not to release 45s or chase Top 40 airplay. Jethro Tull, Pink Floyd and Yes skipped singles for famous songs, and Billy Joel and Elton John did so for songs that were bigger on rock radio. "So for AOR acts like Zeppelin, not issuing certain songs as singles was a badge of honor."
**The Misconception** : The leading AOR acts still released a single every album or two, and Casey Kasem counted down the ones that made the Top 40. "However, one misconception of album oriented rock in the 70s was that the dominant acts of the day stopped issuing singles entirely."

# genre - Grunge

## curiosities
**Album Rock for a New Generation** : Started in 1991 by Seattle bands, grunge gave labels bands they could credibly sell as album rock. "It also provided the record industry with a new generation of bands that could credibly be sold as album rock."
**Singles-Free Era, 1991–1994** : Leading grunge and new-punk acts released no singles, including Soundgarden, Stone Temple Pilots, Counting Crows, Smashing Pumpkins, Green Day and the Offspring. "From 1991 to 1994, every leading Grunge and Nouveau punk act released no singles, from Soundgarden to Stone Temple Pilots."
**End of Singles-Free Grunge** : By the mid-1990s frontmen pushed for singles, and the labels shifted their single-withholding strategy to pop acts. "This era of singles-free Grunge didn't last."

# curiosity

**Defining "Single"** : In industry terms, a single is a song promoted as a potential hit. Before the digital era, and especially in Billboard, it meant a retail single you could buy without the album. The 1990s "war on the single" targeted the retail single. "What is a single? This is a surprisingly malleable word"
**The Cassingle "C"** : Billboard marked cassette singles with "C" and CD singles, usually $5 or less, with "CD." "That billboard footnote was an abbreviation for Cassette Single or Cassingle."
**June 16, 1990 Chart** : 39 of the Top 40 songs, including hits by Bell Biv DeVoe, Depeche Mode and Mariah Carey, were on cassingle or CD single. The exception was Hammer. "In fact, on that week's chart, 39 of the top 40 songs were available on cassette single or CD single."
**12-Inch Vinyl** : Starting in 1970s disco, labels released extended dance mixes on LP-sized vinyl singles, mainly for club DJs. By 1990 these were essential to DJs for mixing but a niche retail product. "Since the 1970s at the height of disco, record labels had made extended mixes of dance-oriented songs available on vinyl singles The Size of LPs, primarily for club disc jockeys."
**Vinyl's Decline** : Cassettes and the Walkman cut into vinyl, and labels sped the shift to CDs by changing vinyl return policies. "By 1990, some labels weren't even issuing their new albums on vinyl LP at all."
**Early-1960s Singles Supremacy** : Singles cost under a dollar and suited teenagers' cheap record players, while LPs were seen as products for adults with hi-fi systems. Labels titled albums after the latest hit and sometimes retitled them while in stores. "According to a 1960 Billboard article, singles were outselling albums that year by roughly a 2-to-1 ratio."
**Album Becomes the Standard Unit** : After 1967, labels built their economics around bundles. Major 1970s albums such as Tapestry, Songs in the Key of Life and Hotel California sold 10 million. "Labels built their economic foundation around people's willingness to buy bundles of songs more often than they paid for individual ones."
**Billboard Retail-Only Rule (1958–1998)** : A song had to be a retail single to appear on the Hot 100. "Remember, since 1958, Billboard rules stated that a song had to be a retail single to appear on the chart."
**Industry's Codependent Relationship with Singles** : Labels wanted people to buy more profitable LPs, but needed Top 40 hits to build stars. "The business hated retail singles but needed them."
**Three-Single Ceiling Before 1977** : Before 1977, labels usually stopped after a third single. "Prior to 1977, no album had ever generated more than three top 10 singles."
**The Thriller Model** : From 1984 to 1990, more than a dozen chart-topping albums produced at least five Top 40 singles, including Purple Rain, Bryan Adams's Reckless, True Blue, Whitney Houston's Whitney, George Michael's Faith and Paula Abdul's Forever Your Girl. Most sold best on cassette. "For the rest of the 1980s, the major record labels pursued what could be called the Thriller model, emphasizing albums that could be milked dry for hits."
**Cost of Hit Singles** : Hit singles are expensive to promote and less profitable than albums. "Labels spend big money promoting each one to radio and at retail, they're not as profitable as albums."
**The Great War Against the Single Begins** : The Hammer and Vanilla Ice hits started the campaign. "The one-two punch of You Can't Touch This and Ice Ice Baby kicked off a decade-long campaign to bury the single as a retail medium."
**Pop Acts Targeted from 1995** : Labels moved the strategy from rock bands to more profitable pop acts. "Starting in 1995, more obviously top 40 bound acts with insanely catchy songs were withheld from the singles market."
**CD Pricing** : CDs cost far more than LPs or cassettes. "And the fact that by the mid-90s album usually meant a $15-$18 compact disc, up to $10 higher than the LP or Cassette, the industry was minting money."
**The "One Good Song" Period** : In the late 1990s, helped by a booming economy, one-hit acts sold millions of full-price CDs, causing the strongest consumer resentment of the era. "Millions of consumers paid just shy of a Hamilton for full-length CDs by acts with a single radio hit."
**Late-1990s Withholders** : Janet Jackson, Will Smith, Foo Fighters, Jewel, Smash Mouth and Sugar Ray withheld singles case by case. "as well as repeat hitmakers like Jewel, Smash Mouth and Sugar Ray."
**Billboard's 1998 Rule Change** : Billboard had resisted the change because retailers, a major part of its readership, feared it would mean even fewer singles. They worried young people with $5 or $10 had little to buy. Ultimately Billboard decided excluding airplay hits was damaging the chart. "At the end of 1998, they finally changed the Hot 100's rules to allow Airplay Only hits to appear on the chart."
**Industry Peak** : The music business peaked in 1999–2000. "The music business peaked in 1999 and 2000 when the compact disc was king"
**Napster (1999)** : Peer-to-peer MP3 file sharing that devastated the industry's old model. "made its debut in the summer of 1999, a few months after 18-year-old computer programmer Sean Fanning dropped out of Northeastern University."
**Suing Consumers** : As sales collapsed, the industry sued some of its own customers. "As music sales began to crater in 2001, the recording industry tried everything to put the file-sharing genie back in the bottle, up to and including suing some of its own consumers."
**iTunes 99-Cent Songs (2003)** : Steve Jobs insisted every song be sold for about a dollar, which effectively made every song a single. "In that one detail, Steve Jobs upended four decades of music industry gospel, and he revived the singles market."
**Digital Sales on the Hot 100** : Changed how songs became hits. "Billboard added digital sales to the Hot 100 in 2005."
**Streaming Era** : Labels still pick the songs to promote, but listeners choose with their clicks. "In the 2010s, with streaming music taking over the industry, albums today behave like playlists, and the consumer is more empowered than ever."
**Hot 100 Longevity Record** : "One Sweet Day" and "Despacito" share the 16-week record, which "Don't Speak" matched on airplay alone. "No song in Hot 100 history has ever spent more than 16 weeks at number one."
