## albums
**Garth Brooks (1989) - Self-Titled Debut Album** : His debut album, made after he came up through Nashville during the new traditionalist era. Brooks was no purist, and the songs carried his mass-appeal sensibilities. Pop was built in, but the album was not courting Top 40 listeners the way early-80s country pop had. Its last single and biggest hit was "The Dance." "Pop was baked in, but it wasn't courting top 40 listeners like the stuff from a decade earlier."
**No Fences (1990) - Sophomore Album and All-Time Best Seller** : Brooks had his second album finished when the summer of 1990 was barely over. He released it in the middle of the chart run of "Friends in Low Places." It reached No. 1 on the Country Albums chart in just three weeks, knocking off Clint Black's "Killin' Time," and gave Brooks the top country single and the top country album in America at the same time for the first time. In the week ending October 27, 1990, it instantly went gold and platinum, confirming him as country's biggest star. It remains his best-selling album, certified for 18 million in sales in the US alone. On it he perfected his blend of tradition-minded pedal steel and fiddle country with 70s-style soft rock melodies. "No Fences remains Garth Brooks' all-time best-selling album, Certified for Sales of 18 Million in the US alone."
**No Fences (1990) - Chart Disadvantage Before SoundScan** : It debuted on the Billboard album chart in September 1990, the same week as Queensrÿche's "Empire." By spring 1991, "Empire" was certified for a million in sales and sat in the pop albums Top 10. In the same span, "No Fences" was triple platinum yet had never charted higher than No. 15, and by April it had fallen back into the mid-30s. This was happening even as it produced its third No. 1 country hit, "Two of a Kind, Workin' on a Full House." "But Garth's No Fences, again, an album with the same number of weeks on the chart, was triple platinum."
**No Fences (1990) - SoundScan Surge** : In the first week SoundScan came online in May 1991, "No Fences" jumped into the Top 10 at a new peak of No. 4, and it later reached No. 3. It spent the rest of summer 1991 in and around the Top 10 and was certified quadruple and then quintuple platinum. Accurate counting is widely seen as what let the industry recognize, for the first time, how popular country and Brooks really were, and promote him accordingly. "The very first week, Sound Scan came online, Garth's No Fences album, hurtled into the top 10, reaching a new peak of number 4."
**Ropin' the Wind (1991) - First Country Album to Debut at No. 1** : His third album arrived at the top of the Billboard 200 in its first week, the week before Guns N' Roses' heavily hyped "Use Your Illusion" releases in mid-September 1991. It was the first country album ever to debut at No. 1, and the first country title to top Billboard's flagship album chart at all since Kenny Rogers' "Greatest Hits" more than ten years earlier. The next week "Use Your Illusion II" debuted at No. 1 and "Use Your Illusion I" at No. 2. Guns N' Roses held the top for only two weeks before "Ropin' the Wind" returned to No. 1 for most of the fall. Brooks was releasing it to test whether his chart success had been a SoundScan fluke. "It was not only the first country album ever to debut at number one."
**Ropin' the Wind (1991) - Sales and Chart Dominance** : It was certified quadruple platinum just two months after release. It spent 18 weeks at No. 1 on the all-genre Billboard 200 between fall 1991 and spring 1992. At country radio it produced five hits: the lead single "Rodeo" (No. 3), "Papa Loved Mama" (No. 3), and the No. 1s "Shameless," "The River," and "What She's Doing Now." It was eventually certified for 14 million copies, as much as the two Guns N' Roses albums sold combined. Billboard ranked it the top seller among both country and pop albums for all of 1992. "Ropened the wind was eventually certified for sales of 14 million copies, which by the way was as much as those two guns and roses albums sold combined."
**The Chase (1992) - Fourth Album** : Its lead single was "We Shall Be Free." By 1992 Brooks was simply expected to debut at No. 1 on the Billboard 200, and he did, as the second single, "Somewhere Other Than the Night," headed to No. 1. Its first two months of sales kept R.E.M.'s "Automatic for the People" (the follow-up to 1991's "Out of Time," eventually quadruple platinum) from reaching No. 1, leaving it at a No. 2 peak. A couple of weeks later it also kept Madonna's highly anticipated "Erotica" from the top, and that album too peaked at No. 2. "The chase sailed in at number one."
**Beyond the Season (1992) - Christmas Album** : Brooks released his Christmas album two years before Mariah Carey recorded her holiday album in 1994. It reached No. 2 on the Billboard 200, higher than Carey's "Merry Christmas," which topped out at No. 3. "Brooks issued his Christmas disc Beyond the Season two years early."
**In Pieces (1993) - Fifth Studio Album** : It debuted at No. 1 in late 1993 as his fifth studio album and third chart-topper. It produced five Top 10 country hits, including the No. 1s "Ain't Goin' Down ('Til the Sun Comes Up)" and "American Honky-Tonk Bar Association," and featured "Standing Outside the Fire." It held No. 1 for more than a month, which pushed Mariah Carey's "Music Box" back until Christmas. Even with some retailers shut out over used CDs, it opened to the best numbers of his career to that point. "In late 1993, Brooks debuted on top with in pieces, his fifth studio album and third chart topper."
**The Hits (1994) - First Greatest Hits, Limited Edition** : For years Brooks resisted requests for a compilation from fans and from Capitol Nashville, fearing it would cannibalize his still-strong studio album sales. He agreed to the plainly titled "The Hits" only on the condition that it be a limited-edition release, and the CD was printed with "limited time only" on the cover. It arrived just days before Christmas 1994, sold in the millions, and topped the Billboard 200 for eight weeks in early 1995. It outsold Pearl Jam's third album, "Vitalogy," and the Beatles' "Live at the BBC," charting higher and selling more than the quadruple-platinum Beatles set. "The CD was even printed with the phrase limited time only on the cover."

## songs
**Much Too Young (To Feel This Damn Old) (1989) - First Hit** : His very first hit was entering the country Top 10 in summer 1989. At that point he was getting much less attention than Clint Black, but he would prove the true harbinger of 90s country. "This newcomer's very first hit, Much Too Young, to feel this damn old, was just breaking into the country top 10 in the summer of 1989."
**If Tomorrow Never Comes (1989) - First No. 1** : His first No. 1 hit, co-written with Kent Blazy, one of the songwriters he met at the Bluebird Cafe. "Kent Blaisey, who teamed with Brooks to co-write his first number one hit, If Tomorrow Never Comes."
**The Dance (1990) - Signature Ballad** : Written by fellow Bluebird alumnus Tony Arata, it was the last single and biggest hit from his debut, a song you could easily picture Dan Fogelberg singing. It spent three weeks at No. 1 on Hot Country Singles in summer 1990. That was impressive for a new artist, since in 1989 no country single had spent more than two weeks at No. 1. "The Dance became Brooks' signature of ballet."
**Friends in Low Places (1990) - Most Iconic Song** : A barn-busting, hard-drinking, sing-along honky-tonk anthem about a man letting beer chase away his blues while only pretending to apologize for being déclassé. It was released less than a month after "The Dance" peaked, reached No. 1 in October 1990, and stayed there four weeks. The week ending October 27, 1990 was its fourth week at No. 1. Critic Chuck Klosterman wrote that singing along with it "was like drunkenly laughing at a rich person and knowing that you were right," and that Garth told stories about blue-collar people who felt good about what their bad life symbolized. "Friends in low places is a barn-busting, hard-drinking, sing-along, honky-tongued anthem."
**Unanswered Prayers (1990)** : A No. 1 country hit from "No Fences." It shows how Brooks, his band, and co-writers turned the sound of old James Taylor records into wistful bromides about small-town life. "Into wistful bromides about small-town life, like the number one country hit, unanswered prayers."
**The Thunder Rolls (1991)** : The rock-inflected opener of "No Fences," channeling the sound and spirit of vintage Eagles or Aerosmith. It is a cinematic story song about a marriage gone wrong. It reached No. 1 on Hot Country Singles, helped by a controversial video in which Brooks played an abusive husband wearing a fake beard, glasses, and a wig. "Fueled by a controversial music video in which Brooks played an abusive husband in a fake beard, glasses, and a wig."
**Two of a Kind, Workin' on a Full House (1991)** : The third No. 1 country hit from "No Fences." It peaked in spring 1991, while the album sat in the mid-30s on the pre-SoundScan album chart. "Brooks was racking up his third number one country hit from the album."
**Rodeo (1991)** : Lead single from "Ropin' the Wind." It was already in the Hot Country Singles Top 5 when the album debuted, on its way to No. 3. "Ropin The Wind's lead single, Rodeo, was already in the hot country singles top five on its way to number three."
**Shameless (1991) - Billy Joel Cover** : Billy Joel wrote and first recorded it as a deep cut on his 1989 album "Storm Front," never issued as a single. Brooks heard its potential and country-friendliness, played it live for months, saw it go over with crowds, and convinced producer Allen Reynolds to let him record it. It reached No. 1 on Hot Country Singles in November 1991. More than a dozen pop stations started playing it alongside Bryan Adams, Boyz II Men, and Paula Abdul, a genre crossover on a smaller scale comparable to Run-DMC's 1986 cover of Aerosmith's "Walk This Way." His label was not promoting it to Top 40 and was wary of reaching beyond country ten years after the Urban Cowboy boom and bust. His co-manager and publisher told Billboard: "if it happens, it happens. It's not something we're trying to generate. Let him cross to us this time." "Garth Brooks heard its potential and the country friendliness of the song."
**Papa Loved Mama (1991)** : A No. 3 country hit from "Ropin' the Wind." "The album generated five hits including the number three pop-a-love mama."
**The River (1992)** : A No. 1 country hit from "Ropin' the Wind." "The number ones the river and what she's doing now."
**What She's Doing Now (1992)** : A No. 1 country hit from "Ropin' the Wind." "The number ones the river and what she's doing now."
**We Shall Be Free (1992) - Social Protest Single** : A gospel-flavored country song and the lead single from "The Chase," which was the No. 1 album in the country. Brooks was its singer and main songwriter. It expresses tolerance for freedom of religion and gay equality, condemns racism, hunger, and homelessness, and gestures toward condemning economic inequality. It peaked at No. 12 on Hot Country in October 1992 and was still in the Top 20 that November. That made it the only lead single from one of his original studio albums to miss the country Top 10, and his first major single to get pushback. Country stations were leery of a pro-equality, anti-racist, anti-homophobia song, at least partly to avoid taking sides in a presidential election year. "The only way a song like Re-Shalby Free could be any kind of country hit in 1992 was if it came from an absolute megastar."
**Somewhere Other Than the Night (1992)** : The second single from "The Chase," a more conventional country ballad. It was heading to No. 1 as the album debuted at the top. "The more conventional country ballad, somewhere other than the night, was on its way to number one."
**Ain't Goin' Down ('Til the Sun Comes Up) (1993)** : A No. 1 country hit from "In Pieces." "The number one Rae Vups, Inc. Goin Down, Till the Sun comes up."
**American Honky-Tonk Bar Association (1993)** : A No. 1 country hit from "In Pieces." "It's called the American Boky Tonk Ball Association."

## curiosities
**Best-Selling Musician of the 1990s** : Brooks was the best-selling musician of the 1990s in any genre, not just country, with nearly 100 million albums sold that decade. That put him ahead of Mariah Carey, Nirvana, Pearl Jam, and Tupac. Two decades later he had reached 157 million in certifications, second in America only to the Beatles. "Garth is up to 157 million in certifications, second only in America to the Beatles."
**All 18 Country No. 1s Absent from the Hot 100** : None of his hit songs, including all 18 of his 90s country chart-toppers, appeared on Billboard's Hot 100. This was his choice: he was a savvy Billboard watcher who understood how the charts worked and played to win. He was the music megastar Top 40 pop fans barely knew. "Garth Brooks wasn't on the Hot 100 because Brooks wanted it that way."
**Strategic Refusal to Release Retail Singles** : Through the mid-90s the Hot 100 required a retail single, while Hot Country Singles & Tracks did not. Brooks could dominate the country chart and sell huge numbers of albums without singles. Releasing singles would have made his songs eligible for the Hot 100, where as country songs they would have been at a disadvantage against big pop hits and might have peaked below No. 1, the Top 10, or even the Top 40. "Why allow his hits to peak on the Hot 100 somewhere below the number one spot, the top ten, or even the top four?"
**Dancing With the One That Brung Him** : Brooks came up in a Nashville that remembered how early-80s country pop became a bull-riding, denim-and-rhinestones fad and even a punchline. He was determined not to let that happen to him. In multiple interviews he took pride in setting sales records while staying close to country fans and inviting pop fans to come to his side of the dial. "He was determined to dominate the hit parade while, as the country saying goes, dancing with the one that brung him."
**Rock Spectacle, Pop Sales, Country Sound** : Brooks wanted to prove country could have the flash and arena-packing spectacle of rock and the blockbuster sales of mainstream pop. He blended an earlier generation's adult contemporary balladry and some hard rock elements into songs that kept steel guitars, fiddles, and yearning high-lonesome vocals. "He fused a prior generation of adult contemporary balladry and even certain hard rock elements into songs that still had steel guitars, fiddles."
**Rock-Loving Oklahoma Childhood** : He grew up in Yukon, Oklahoma (and is also called the guy from Tulsa), and by his own account listened more to rock than country. He was especially inspired by the soft rock of singer-songwriters James Taylor and Dan Fogelberg. Only after hearing George Strait around 1981 did he become convinced he should pursue country. "It was only after young Garth heard George Strait circa 1981 that Brooks became convinced he should pursue country music."
**Bluebird Cafe Break (1987)** : After several years in Nashville looking for a break, he got it in 1987 after playing the Bluebird Cafe. Several songwriters he met there, including Kent Blazy and Tony Arata, went on to supply him with songs, a vital connection in a songwriting-driven town. "It came in 1987 after he played the legendary music city venue, The Bluebird Cafe."
**Archetypal Artist of the SoundScan Era** : He became the archetypal artist of the SoundScan era, more than any rock star, rapper, or pop diva. No single artist benefited more from the 1991 chart revolution. "No single artist benefited more from this chart revolution than Garth Brooks."
**Annual Fall Release Pattern** : Each fall he released a new album that debuted at No. 1 and outperformed a more hotly anticipated pop or rock album: Guns N' Roses, R.E.M., Madonna, Mariah Carey, Pearl Jam, and the Beatles. "Every fall, he would release a new album. And it would not only debut at number one, but outperform a more hotly anticipated pop or rock album."
**Used-CD Boycott (1993)** : In 1993, CDs cost $5–$7 more than LPs or cassettes, and retailers found shoppers happy to sell back old discs and buy used ones cheaply. The record industry earned nothing from those sales. When a coalition of labels planned to end cooperative advertising with retailers selling used CDs, Brooks went further. A few months before "In Pieces," he announced he would not sell it on CD at any retailer dealing in used product. He framed it as a fight for musicians, songwriters, and producers, while the media portrayed it as greed. It was comparable to Metallica taking on Napster or Taylor Swift taking on Spotify years later. Used CDs also hurt his chart positions and platinum certifications, which may have been his ultimate motive, since he was a fierce competitor who avoided arenas where he could not win. "In essence, Garth's move was like Metallica taking on Napster or Taylor Swift taking on Spotify years before those fights happened."
**Outsold the Beatles at Christmas 1994** : "The Hits" outsold the Beatles' "Live at the BBC" during the 1994 holiday season. Perhaps that made him feel invincible, because the next year he tried something similar and it did not go well, as he finally met his match on the album chart and in the record books. "Perhaps outselling the Beatles during the holiday season of 1994 made Garth Brooks feel fairly invincible."

**I'll Still Be Loving You (1987)** : A stately ballad that crossed to the Hot 100. It was the only record of its kind on pop radio at the time, and Nashville was stunned it crossed over at all. It peaked at a modest No. 33. "The industry in Nashville was stunned that restless heart crossed over at all."

# artist - Clint Black

## albums
**Killin' Time (1989)** : Went double platinum, rare for a country LP then by anyone but Randy Travis. It produced four No. 1 country hits right away, remarkable for a newcomer. Black was widely seen as country's great mass-market hope for the 90s and bridged traditionalists and arena-friendly country. Garth Brooks's "No Fences" knocked it off the top of the Country Albums chart. "It generated a stunning four number one country hits out of the box, remarkable for a newcomer."

# artist - Guns N' Roses

## albums
**Use Your Illusion I & II (1991)** : More than four years after "Appetite for Destruction," they released two separate CDs on the same day in mid-September 1991. Media hype hit fever pitch and stores held midnight sales. "II" debuted at No. 1 and "I" at No. 2, but they held the top for only two weeks before "Ropin' the Wind" returned. Combined, they sold about as much as "Ropin' the Wind" (14 million). "Music stores announced midnight sales for the two user Illusion CDs."

# artist - Billy Ray Cyrus

## albums
**Some Gave All (1992)** : Spent 17 consecutive weeks at No. 1 on the Billboard 200 and eventually went nine times platinum. "It spent 17 consecutive weeks on top and eventually went nine times platinum."

## songs
**Achy Breaky Heart (1992)** : A line-dancing earworm that, unlike Brooks's songs, deliberately courted pop fans. It topped Hot Country Singles and reached No. 4 on the Hot 100. It started an Urban Cowboy-style line-dance craze with specific steps. By some measures Cyrus was a bigger crossover success than Garth Brooks in 1992. "Unlike Garth, Billy Ray actually did court pop fans with his line dancing earworm."

# artist - Alan Jackson

## albums
**A Lot About Livin' (And a Little 'Bout Love) (1992)** : Went triple platinum in 1993 and was sextuple platinum two years later. "Two years later, Jackson's album was Sex Tuffle Platinum."

## songs
**Chattahoochee (1993)** : A wistful look back at summers on a river near Jackson's Georgia hometown and the No. 1 country song of 1993. He released it as a retail single, which went gold, but it peaked just below the Hot 100 Top 40 because of limited airplay outside country. That outcome confirmed Brooks's decision to avoid pop competition. "Chata Hootsie peaked just below the top 40 given its limited airplay beyond country stations."

# genre - Country Crossover

## curiosities
**Policing the Border Since the 1920s** : Ever since "hillbilly music" became a category in the 1920s, country performers, programmers, and fans have guarded the line between what is and isn't country. Late-50s and 60s pop chart-toppers by country stars included Conway Twitty, Brenda Lee, Jimmy D., Bobby Goldsboro, and Jeannie C. Riley. "Country performers, radio programmers and fans have pridefully policed the border of what is and isn't an ain't country."
**70s Dual-Chart No. 1s** : Charlie Rich, Glen Campbell (a veteran guitarist turned country pop megastar), and B.J. Thomas all had No. 1 hits on both the Hot 100 and Hot Country Singles. "All of these were number one hits on both the hot 100 and hot country singles."
**Urban Cowboy Era, 1980–1983** : From 1980 to 1982, almost any country act that played down the twang could score a pop hit. These were middle-of-the-road records that served radio during the disco backlash. The wave faded by 1983. "From 1980 to 82, it seemed any country act able to record a crossover track and play down the twang could score a pop hit."
**Mid-80s Wilderness** : By 1984 pop had become more new wave, funk, and synth-driven (Duran Duran), and heartland rockers like Bruce Springsteen and John Mellencamp ("Pink Houses") covered middle-American authenticity. Country entered a long wilderness period on the pop charts, much like disco after 1980, while staying as popular as ever with its core fans. "It was as if pop listeners had overdosed on Nashville product and sent the entire genre packing."
**Parallel Platinum Worlds in the 90s** : Unlike the Urban Cowboy era, in the 90s country and pop each succeeded in their own arenas, and crossover by the same recording was no longer required. "This only reinforced that country and pop were parallel platinum worlds."

# genre - New Traditionalism

## curiosities
**Term Coined Against Country Pop** : A mid-80s term coined to set this music apart from the by-then disdained Urban Cowboy-era country pop. Stung by the pop mainstream's rejection, artists returned to country's roots. It thrived in the late 80s because blending country with pop had little upside, and it culminated in Clint Black's success in 1989. "A mid 80s term coined specifically to differentiate it from the now disdained country pop sound of the early 80s, circa urban cowboy."

# concert - The Bluebird Cafe

## curiosities
**Garth Brooks's 1987 Break** : Brooks's 1987 performance at the Nashville venue led to his break, and songwriters he met there, including Kent Blazy and Tony Arata, supplied his songs. "Several songwriters Brooks met at The Bluebird, would wind up supplying him with songs."

# curiosity

**Urban Cowboy (1980) - The Country Saturday Night Fever** : Released in summer 1980 and starring John Travolta (who traded his Brooklyn accent for a Southern one as Bud Davis) and Debra Winger, it was called "the country Saturday Night Fever" by more than one critic. Both films were about blue-collar strivers showing off at a buzzy nightclub. It grossed $47 million, about half of "Saturday Night Fever," but its musical impact was enormous. Honky-tonks with mechanical bulls spread across America, with designer jeans and cowboy boots as the dress code. "Urban Cowboy was more than a movie. It was a cultural signing."
**Urban Cowboy Soundtrack** : Reached the Top 3 on the pop album chart, went platinum, and produced half a dozen Top 40 hits. Its knock-on effect on country acts unconnected to the film showed the trend had legs. "The urban cowboy soundtrack was itself a smash, reaching the top three on the pop album chart, going platinum and spawning a half dozen top forty hits."
**Grease and Travolta's Opposite Paths** : After "Saturday Night Fever," Travolta co-starred with Olivia Newton-John in "Grease." She then went fully pop, while he moved toward country on screen. "He was going to do for country what he did for disco with Saturday night fever bring it fully into the mainstream."
**SoundScan Launch (May 1991)** : Barcode-scanning retail technology that accurately counted record-store sales for Billboard. It launched on the Billboard 200 in May 1991 and permanently ended the coast-skewed industry's overstatement of rock and pop and understatement of country and rap. "Sound Scan, which launched on the Billboard 200 album chart in May of 1991, eliminated that bias against rap and country for good."
**Charts as Feedback Loops** : Charts tell the music industry what is popular so it can make it more popular. "Charts are feedback loops."
**The War Against the Single** : The biggest chart story of the 90s was the industry's quiet campaign to kill retail singles and push full-length CD sales. Because the Hot 100 required a retail single through the mid-90s, huge radio hits never released as singles were invisible on it, including the Rembrandts' "Friends" theme and No Doubt's "Don't Speak." "The biggest story on the charts in the 90s was the music business's quiet campaign to kill off retail singles."
**Hot Country Singles & Tracks** : The chart's full title signaled that many of its hits were album-only tracks, since it did not require a retail release. "That chart's full title was Hot Country singles and tracks, indicating that many of its hits were tracks available only on albums."
