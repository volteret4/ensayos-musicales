## albums
**Garth Brooks (Debut Album)** : This self-titled debut album featured songs infused with mass appeal sensibilities, where pop elements were "baked in" but without overtly courting Top 40 listeners in the same way country-pop had a decade earlier. Its last single, "The Dance," became its biggest hit and Brooks' signature ballad. "Pop was baked in, but it wasn't courting top 40 listeners like the stuff from a decade earlier."
**No Fences (1990) - Best-Selling Album** : This album instantly went gold and platinum in the week ending October 27, 1990, affirming Brooks as country music's biggest star, and soon after, the archetypal artist of the SoundScan era. It shot to number one on the Country Albums Chart in three weeks, knocking out Clint Black's "Killin' Time," and remains Brooks' all-time best-selling album, certified for 18 million sales in the U.S. alone. "No Fences remains Garth Brooks' all-time best-selling album, Certified for Sales of 18 Million in the US alone."
**No Fences (1990) - SoundScan Impact** : Despite being triple platinum, "No Fences" had never gone higher than number 15 on the Billboard album chart by April 1991, while albums of similar or lesser stature in pop/rock charted much higher. However, the very first week SoundScan came online in May 1991, the album hurtled into the top 10, reaching a new peak of number 4 and later number 3. "The very first week, Sound Scan came online, Garth's No Fences album, hurtled into the top 10, reaching a new peak of number 4."
**Ropin' the Wind (1991) - Chart-Topping Debut** : Brooks' third album, released in mid-September 1991, arrived atop the Billboard 200 in its first week. It was not only the first country album ever to debut at number one, but also the first country title to top Billboard's flagship all-genre album chart at all since Kenny Rogers' "Greatest Hits" more than ten years earlier. "Ropin The Wind, Garth Brooks' third album, arrived atop the Billboard 200 in its first week."

## songs
**We Shall Be Free (1992)** : A gospel-flavored country song released in November 1992, which was in the top 20 on Billboard's Hot Country singles and Tracks chart and served as the lead single from a number one album. The song captured the mood of the times, espousing tolerance for freedom of religion and gay equality, decrying racism, hunger, and homelessness, and even condemning economic inequality. "We shall be free peaked at number 12 on the Hot Country Chart in October 1992."
**If Tomorrow Never Comes** : This was Garth Brooks' first number one hit, co-written with Kent Blazy. It exemplified Brooks' potent sound, fusing soft rock (à la Dan Fogelberg) and plaintive country (à la George Strait). "This fusion of soft rock, a la Dan Fogelberg and plaintive country, a la George Strait, was a potent sound for Brooks."
**The Dance (1990)** : Penned by Tony Arata, "The Dance" became the last single and biggest hit from Brooks' self-titled debut album and his signature ballad, a song that could easily be pictured as a Dan Fogelberg tune. It spent three impressive weeks at number one on the Hot Country Singles chart in the summer of 1990, a notable achievement for a new artist given no country single had spent more than two weeks at number one the prior year. "The dance spent three weeks at number one on the Hot Country Singles chart in the summer of 1990."
**Friends in Low Places (1990)** : This barn-busting, hard-drinking, sing-along honky-tonk anthem, a rowdy but witty song about a man letting beer chase his blues away, reached number one on the Hot Country Singles chart in October 1990 and stayed there for four weeks. Critic Chuck Klosterman described singing along to it as "drunkenly laughing at a rich person and knowing that you were right." "Released less than a month after the dance peaked on Hot Country Singles, friends in low places stormed up the chart, reaching number one in October 1990."
**Unanswered Prayers** : This number one country hit from the "No Fences" album is described as a wistful bromide about small-town life, embodying Brooks' ability to translate the sound of old James Taylor records into heartfelt narratives. "Brooks, his band and co-songwriters were able to translate the sound of, say, old James Taylor records. Into wistful bromides about small-town life, like the number one country hit, unanswered prayers."
**The Thunder Rolls** : The rock-inflected opener from "No Fences," this cinematic story song about a marriage gone wrong, also hit number one on the Hot Country singles chart. Its success was further fueled by a controversial music video in which Brooks played an abusive husband in a fake beard, glasses, and a wig. "While the Thunder Rolls hit number 1 on the Hot Country singles chart, fueled by a controversial music video in which Brooks played an abusive husband in a fake beard, glasses, and a wig, the No Fences album spent the rest of the summer in and around the top 10, as it was certified quadruple and then quintuple platinum."
**Two of a Kind, Workin' on a Full House** : This song became Garth Brooks' third number one country hit from his "No Fences" album. "Brooks was racking up his third number one country hit from the album, two of a kind working on a faux house."
**Rodeo** : The lead single from Brooks' third album, "Ropin' the Wind," this song was already climbing the Hot Country Singles chart, sitting in the top five and on its way to number three as the album was released. "Ropin The Wind's lead single, Rodeo, was already in the hot country singles top five on its way to number three."
**Shameless (1991)** : This song reached the top of the Hot Country Singles chart in November 1991. It was originally written and first recorded by Billy Joel for his 1989 album "Stormfront" as a deep cut, never released as a single by Joel himself. "Shameless reached the top of hot country singles in November 1991."
**Much Too Young, to Feel This Damn Old (1989)** : This was Garth Brooks' very first hit, breaking into the country top 10 in the summer of 1989. This song, more than Clint Black's contemporary successes, was the true harbinger of country music in the 1990s, making the divide between country-pop and new traditionalism moot. "This newcomer's very first hit, Much Too Young, to feel this damn old, was just breaking into the country top 10 in the summer of 1989."

## curiosities
**1990s Best-Selling Musician** : Garth Brooks was the best-selling musician of the 1990s across any genre, with total album sales in that decade reaching nearly 100 million. He topped other 90s titans such as Mariah Carey, Nirvana, Pearl Jam, and Tupac, and two decades later, his certifications totaled 157 million, placing him second only to The Beatles in America. "He was the best-selling musician of that decade. Not just country music's bestseller, the bestseller, period in any genre with total album sales in that decade of nearly 100 million, topping such 90s titans as Mariah Carey, Nirvana, Pearl Jam and Tupac."
**Hot 100 Exclusion Strategy** : All 18 of Garth Brooks' 90s country chart toppers did not appear on Billboard's Hot 100 Pop Chart, a deliberate strategy by Brooks. As a savvy Billboard observer, he understood how the charts worked and played the game to win, aiming to dominate the hit parade while remaining unapologetically country. "Garth Brooks wasn't on the Hot 100 because Brooks wanted it that way."
**Nashville's Lesson from Country Pop** : Brooks came up in a Nashville environment that remembered the last time country music had attempted to flirt with the pop charts, resulting in country pop becoming a "bull riding denim and rhinestones fad, even a punchline." He was determined not to let that happen to him, blending arena-packing spectacle with unapologetically country roots. "Moreover, Brooks came up in a Nashville that remembered the last time country music had tried to flirt with the pop charts. And how country pop then became a bull riding denim and rhinestones fat, even a punchline."
**Fusion of Styles** : Garth Brooks fused elements from a prior generation of adult contemporary balladry and certain hard rock influences into his songs, while still retaining traditional country instrumentation like steel guitars and fiddles, and the yearning, high-lonesome vocals that were a Nashville trademark. He wanted to prove country music could have the flash of rock and the blockbuster sales of pop while staying true to its roots. "He fused a prior generation of adult contemporary balladry and even certain hard rock elements into songs that still had steel guitars, fiddles, and the kind of yearning, high-loan, some vocals that were a Nashville trademark."
**SoundScan Era Archetype** : Brooks became the archetypal artist of the SoundScan era, a period marked by the accurate tallying of music sales through barcode scanning. His rise to multi-platinum status demonstrated the power of this new technology in revealing the true popularity of country music, which had previously been understated by the industry. "Soon enough, Garth Brooks would become more than any rock star, rapper, or pop diva, the archetypal artist of the sound scan era."
**Musical Upbringing and Influences** : Growing up in Yukon, Oklahoma, Garth Brooks primarily listened to rock music and was inspired by soft rock singer-songwriters such as James Taylor and Dan Fogelberg. His decision to pursue country music was spurred by hearing George Strait around 1981, guiding his unique fusion of soft rock and traditional country. "It was only after young Garth heard George Strait circa 1981 that Brooks became convinced he should pursue country music."
**Early Career in Nashville** : Brooks spent several years in Nashville searching for his break before achieving success. His opportunity arose in 1987 after performing at the legendary Bluebird Cafe, where he connected with several songwriters who would contribute to his hit catalog. "Brooks had already spent several prior years in Nashville looking for his break. It came in 1987 after he played the legendary music city venue, The Bluebird Cafe."
**Bluebird Cafe Songwriting Connections** : After playing at the Bluebird Cafe in 1987, Garth Brooks met several songwriters who became vital connections in Nashville's songwriting-fueled scene. These writers, including Kent Blazy and Tony Arata, provided him with key songs, such as his first number one hit, "If Tomorrow Never Comes," co-written with Blazy, and his signature ballad, "The Dance," penned by Arata. "Several songwriters Brooks met at The Bluebird, would wind up supplying him with songs, a vital connection in a town as fueled by songwriting as Nashville."
**SoundScan as a Catalyst** : Garth Brooks was the single greatest beneficiary of the chart revolution initiated by SoundScan, which accurately tallied music sales. Upon SoundScan's implementation, his "No Fences" album surged into the top 10, indicating that accurate data enabled the music industry to recognize and promote his true popularity for the first time. "And no single artist benefited more from this chart revolution than Garth Brooks."

## curiosities
**Industry Stance on Used CDs (1993)** : Garth Brooks took a unique and competitive stance against the sale of used CDs, a controversial topic in 1993 when CDs were significantly more expensive than LPs or cassettes. The record industry opposed used CDs because they generated no royalties. Brooks announced that his "In Pieces" album would not be sold on CD at retailers dealing in used products, framing it as a fight for musicians, songwriters, and producers, though the media often portrayed it as greed. This move was unprecedented for an artist of his stature, compared to later actions by Metallica against Napster or Taylor Swift against Spotify. His ultimate motivation was competitive, as used CD sales would not contribute to his chart positions or platinum certifications. "Brooks announced he wouldn't sell it on CD at any retailer that dealt in used product."
**Strategic Avoidance of Hot 100 Singles** : Brooks deliberately avoided releasing retail singles for the Hot 100 chart, a strategy enabled by the Hot Country chart's flexibility (which charted tracks as well as singles) and motivated by a desire to dominate without competing at a disadvantage against pop hits. He prioritized selling full-length albums and maintained pride in achieving sales records by sticking to country fans, remembering the fate of country stars who aggressively pursued pop listeners in the early 1980s Urban Cowboy fad. "Brooks could dominate this chart and still sell truckloads of albums by not issuing singles."
**Consistent Album Chart Dominance** : Starting in 1992 with "The Chase," Garth Brooks established a pattern of releasing a new album every fall that would not only debut at number one on the Billboard 200 but also consistently outperform other highly anticipated pop or rock albums released around the same time. This strategic timing allowed him to dominate the charts and feed his dedicated fan base. "Every fall, he would release a new album. And it would not only debut at number one, but outperform a more hotly anticipated pop or rock album."
