## albums
**Glass Houses (1980) - Chart-topping Album** : This album completed a six-week run on top of the Billboard album chart in the week ending July 19, 1980. Its release marked a significant departure from Joel's traditional piano-centric sound, featuring rock songs where the piano was an afterthought. This stylistic shift was rewarded with increased chart consistency, establishing Joel as a top pop star in the new decade. "his album Glass Houses was completing a six week run on top of the Billboard album chart."

## songs
**You May Be Right (1980)** : This song marked Billy Joel's comeback into the top 10 on the Billboard charts in April 1980. It was primarily a guitar rock song, a significant departure from his perceived "piano man" persona, with the piano heard only "way back in the mix." The song showcased a "Snottie, Snide, Snarkie" attitude, which was the first time Joel made such a style the lead single from a new album. "Only piano wasn't the most prominent instrument on his latest hit. It was basically a guitar rock song."
**It's Still Rock and Roll to Me (1980) - First Hot 100 Number One** : This song became Billy Joel's first number one song on the Hot 100 in the week ending July 19, 1980. Through its lyrics, Joel reportedly poked fun at his own stylistic insecurities, reflecting his embrace of new styles beyond his "piano man" image and his willingness to adapt to new musical trends. "He even poked fun at his own stylistic insecurities right in the lyrics of a hit."
**We Didn't Start the Fire (1989)** : This song is characterized as an "apocalyptic history lesson song with too many words in it." It is also described as a "strange, unkillable Hot 100 number one hit," and to a generation born after the 1980s, Billy Joel is primarily known for this particular track. "Yes, we will talk about that strange, unkillable Hot 100 number one hit."

## curiosities
**"The Piano Man" Nickname** : While widely known by this nickname, Billy Joel was never "entirely" the piano man. The narrative suggests that some of his most famous piano standards were not actually Billboard chart hits in their day, and even on his massive Grammy-winning smashes, keyboards were just one tool in his repertoire. "The truth is, Billy Joel never really was the piano man. Not entirely anyway."
**Stylistic Versatility** : By the 1980s, when he was at the peak of his hit-generating powers, Joel was known as "the pastiche man" due to his constant experimentation with genres, styles, and even voices, trying them on "like they were clothing." He sometimes didn't need any instruments besides the human voice, consistently seeking new approaches such as new wave or dance crazes to get on the "wave." "Forget piano man, Joel was the pastiche man."
**Post-1980s Recognition** : For a generation born after the 1980s, Billy Joel is mostly recognized as the artist behind "that apocalyptic history lesson song with too many words in it," referring to "We Didn't Start the Fire." This highlights how a particular hit can define an artist's legacy for newer audiences. "to a generation born after the 80s, he is now mostly known as the guy with that apocalyptic history lesson song with too many words in it."
**Career Transformation in 1980** : The year 1980 marked a pivotal moment in Joel's career where he attained "exit velocity" from his "piano man" persona. During this period, he won a top Grammy and released an album full of rock songs (Glass Houses) where the piano was an afterthought, leading to him topping the charts more consistently than ever before. "Today on Hit Parade, we are going to pinpoint the moment that Joel's career attained exit velocity from his so-called piano man persona."
