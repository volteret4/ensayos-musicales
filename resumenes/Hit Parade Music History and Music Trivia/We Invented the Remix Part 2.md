## albums
**On The Six (1999)** : This was Jennifer Lopez's very first album, released in 1999. From this album, singles were strategically aimed at multiple audiences by her team at Sony Music. "waiting for tonight" is cited as an example of this multi-audience targeting. "From her very first album, 1999's On The Six, Jennifer Lopez and her team at Sony Music aimed her singles at multiple audiences."
**J-Lo (2001)** : Released in 2001, this disc permanently established the well-known nickname "J-Lo" for Jennifer Lopez. Her management aimed to affirm her as a "queen of all media," so Sony Music released the CD the same week Sony Pictures released her romantic comedy *The Wedding Planner*. It debuted at number 1 on the Billboard 200 in early February, making Lopez the first artist to have the top album and top movie at the box office in the same week. The album's lead single, "Love Don't Cost the Thing," was already in the top 5 on the Hot 100. The album began to tumble on the charts after the second single "Play" failed to perform well, even briefly falling out of the top 100. "By the way, the disc that permanently established that well-known nickname."

## songs
**Waiting For Tonight (1999)** : This Latin house banger was released as a single from "On The Six" and was strategically serviced to multiple audiences. It went to top 40 radio stations, Spanish-language stations (as "Unanochemas"), and club DJs in a remix by Hex Hector. The Hex Hector remix topped Billboard's Club Play Chart over a month before the pop version peaked at number 8 on the Hot 100. Team Lopez even re-cut the music video with Hector's version to reinforce her club credentials and keep the track fresh. "For example, waiting for tonight, a Latin house banger was serviced not just to top 40 radio stations, but also Spanish language stations in a version called Unanochemas, and to club DJs in a remix by House Producer Pex Hector."
**Love Don't Cost a Thing (2001)** : This song was the lead single from Jennifer Lopez's 2001 album, "J-Lo." At the time of the album's debut at number 1 on the Billboard 200 in early February 2001, this single was already lodged in the top 5 on the Hot 100. "On the Hot 100, the album's lead single Love Don't Cost the Thing was already lodged in the top 5."
**Play (2001)** : This electro-pop track was the second single released from the "J-Lo" album in the spring of 2001. It performed poorly on the charts, peaking at number 18, which caused the album to begin tumbling on the charts. "A second single from the album, the electro pop track Play, whiffed on the charts in the spring of 2001, peaking at number 18."
**I'm Real (2001) - Original Version** : The original version of this song was an 80s-flavored dance-pop track, for which promotion began in early summer 2001. Lopez shot a glossy video, and the track was serviced to top 40 radio stations. However, producer Corey Rooney feared it might stiff on the hip-hop-centric radio of the early 2000s, similar to the "Play" single. "The original version of I'm Real was a percolating 80s flavored dance pop diddy."
**I'm Real (2001) - Murder Remix (feat. Ja Rule)** : Commissioned by producer Corey Rooney due to concerns about the original version's chart performance on hip-hop radio, this remix was a complete re-recording by Ja Rule and the Murder Inc. team. It shared almost nothing with the original beyond the titular refrain, with different tempo, melody, and lyrics. Billboard allowed Sony's strategy to merge airplay and sales of both versions under one chart entry. From July to early September 2001, it was listed as a solo single, but the week it reached number one, Billboard changed the artist credit to Jennifer Lopez featuring Ja Rule, as 85% of airplay was for the Murder Remix. It spent five weeks at number one, despite controversy over Lopez dropping the N-word and its association with September 11th. "The track Jarroul and the Murder Ink team came up with for Lopez sounded like this."
**Ain't It Funny (2001) - Murder Remix (feat. Ja Rule)** : Sensing Billboard's impending policy change on remixes, Lopez's team immediately repeated the "I'm Real" stunt in the fall of 2001. Lopez went back into the studio with Ja Rule to reimagine "Ain't It Funny" from her "J-Lo" album as a totally new song. Billboard, in a concession to Sony Music, "grandfathered in" this track, allowing the original and the unrelated Murder Remix version to be merged into a single Hot 100 slot. It reached number one in March 2002, becoming the last hit permitted to artificially combine separate compositions under the same song title. "Billboard in a concession to Sony Music, which did manage to get Lopez and Jarrule's latest onto the charts by mid-December 2001, Grandfathered in Ain't It Funny."
**All I Have (2003) (feat. LL Cool J)** : By 2003, Jennifer Lopez was back on top of the Hot 100 with this duet. With the chart rules having changed since her previous controversial remixes, there was only one version of this song. It demonstrated that J-Lo could top the charts successfully without an "artificial assist" from merged remix strategies. "By 2003, she was back on top of the hot 100 with another jam that leaned into her prevailing J-Lo Plus rapper style, a duet with hip hop legend LL Cool J called All I Have."

## curiosities
**First Artist with Top Album and Top Movie Simultaneously (2001)** : In early February 2001, when her album "J-Lo" debuted at number 1 on the Billboard 200, Jennifer Lopez also had her romantic comedy "The Wedding Planner" released by Sony Pictures. This made her the first artist to hold both the top album and top movie at the box office in the same week, a milestone that her team effectively "made a thing." "Lopez became the first artist to have the top album and the top movie at the box office in the same week."
**Controversy Over "I'm Real" Remix (2001)** : The Murder Remix of "I'm Real" generated controversy within the music industry because it was an entirely new song, not a traditional remix, yet Billboard allowed it to be merged with the original for chart tracking. Additionally, Jennifer Lopez was "roundly castigated in the media" for dropping the N-word in one of her verses on the remix. Despite this notoriety, the song spent five weeks at number one. "For another thing, in the murder ink teams' zeal to make J-Lo sound street, she drops the N word in one of her verses, for which she was roundly castigated in the media."
**Influence on Billboard Remix Policy Change (2001)** : The highly controversial nature of the "I'm Real" remix, which bore no resemblance to the original song but was merged for chart tracking, led Billboard magazine to change its policy. In a statement published December 1st, 2001, Billboard announced that effective January 2002, re-recorded songs with no lyrical or melodic similarity to the original would be treated as separate songs for chart purposes. "Across the industry, chart watchers and rival labels cried foul. This was not a remix. By the end of 2001, Billboard magazine agreed."
**Revived Romance with Ben Affleck** : Two decades after her initial controversies, Jennifer Lopez's revived romance with actor Ben Affleck and her latest wedding-themed movie were enjoying headlines, indicating her enduring presence in the public eye and her ability to navigate past career challenges. "Jennifer Lopez, as you can tell from our vantage point two decades later as she enjoys headlines about her latest wedding themed movie and her revived romance with actor Ben Affleck, she survived her various controversies just fine."

# curiosity

**Remix History (Beyond Dancefloor Extensions)** : Remix history has evolved significantly beyond simply extending tracks for dancefloors. DJs and producers transitioned from creating longer versions for clubs to radically reinventing songs from the ground up, as exemplified by Jennifer Lopez's "I'm Real" remix which changed the rules of the remix itself. "How DJs and producers went from extending tracks for Dancefloor X to C to reinventing them from the ground up."
**Billboard's Precedent for Separate Remix Tracking (1998)** : Prior to the "I'm Real" controversy, Billboard would generally track a remixed hit separately only if it had a different title. An example is Big Punisher's "I'm Not A Player" and the "completely separate hit" "Still Not A Player," demonstrating that all-new tracks with distinct titles were already charted independently. "Generally, Billboard would track a remixed hit separately only if it had a different title, such as, for example, when rapper Big Punisher had a number 19 R&B hit in 1998 called I'm not a player."
**Billboard's Precedent for Merged Remix Tracking (1999)** : Nashville Group Loanstar's "Amazed" demonstrated a different precedent: a re-recorded song with pop-leaning instrumentation that retained the original title. Because it was still called "Amazed," airplay for both versions contributed to the same chart position, helping it climb to number 1 on the Hot 100. This was the strategy Corey Rooney and Sony hoped to follow with Jennifer Lopez's "I'm Real." "But because this re-recording was still called Amazed, Airplay for both versions contributed to the same chart position, helping Loanstar climb all the way to number 1 on the Hot 100 in March of 2000."
**Billboard Policy Change on Remixes (2001)** : Due to the controversial "split personality" of Jennifer Lopez's "I'm Real" (where the "remix" was an entirely different composition), Billboard magazine changed its policy. Effective the first week of 2002 (published December 1st, 2001), Billboard announced that re-recorded songs bearing no lyrical or melodic resemblance to the original would be treated as separate and distinctive songs for chart tracking. This landmark decision was driven by industry outcry that such practices were "fraudulent and fairly unprecedented." "Effective with the first week of 2002, Billboard will treat re-recorded songs that bear no resemblance to the original recording as a separate and distinctive song for the purposes of chart tracking."
**The "J-Lo Rule" (Post-2002 Billboard Policy)** : The Billboard policy change, dubbed the "J-Lo rule," prevented the merging of chart positions for re-recorded songs that had no lyrical or melodic similarity to their original versions. This significantly altered remix strategies, forcing artists like P Diddy to chart distinct "remixes" separately, and impacting how collaborative remixes would be tracked. "The first major artist to feel the impact of the new J-Lole rule was Lopez's ex-paramore Sean Combs, who by the way now went by P Diddy."
**Late-Breaking Remix Strategy (2010s)** : The late-breaking remix strategy, perfected by Katy Perry, became a prevalent chart fad in the 2010s. It was highly effective at turning second, third, fourth, or fifth singles from "aging albums" into chart-topping smashes by enticing fans to repurchase or stream new versions of existing tracks. This tactic provided labels with a "fresher, hiper, new, and improved" story for radio programmers. "The Katie Perry late-breaking remix strategy became the chart fad of the 2010s."
**Intersectional Implications of Remix Strategy (2010s)** : A "depressing" aspect of the late-breaking remix maneuver in the 2010s was its "intersectional implications," specifically "a whole lot of white pop stars were glomming onto credibility enhancing black performers." This practice became a "recipe for crossover success in the streaming era," extending a historical trend of white artists collaborating with black artists for broader appeal. "What was a little depressing about the maneuver, however, were the intersectional implications."
**Digital Downloads and iTunes Music Store (Mid-2000s)** : By the mid-2000s, digital downloads, particularly those powered by Apple's iTunes music store, became a significant factor on the Hot 100. This created a strong incentive for labels and artists to keep rising songs "fresh by any means necessary," including adding rappers to pop songs, as downloads contributed directly to chart positions. "By the mid-Aughts, digital downloads, powered by Apple's iTunes music store, were a factor on the Hot 100."
**Billboard's Interpretation of Remix Credit (2010s)** : The Billboard remix rule, originally designed to prevent fraudulent merging of distinct songs, ironically compelled Billboard to weigh in on how to distribute credit for remixes that changed "very little at all." This meant that if a featured artist's version (even a minimal change) dominated sales and streams, they would receive full artist credit, even for what critics might call an "embellishment." "It is ironic to consider that Billboards remix rule, which was developed to police reboots like Jennifer Lopez's that were too different from the original song, was now compelling billboard to weigh in on how to dull out credit for remixes that change very little at all."
**The Object of the Remix Game (Modern Day)** : For most modern-day hitmakers and their labels, the primary goal of the remix game is to change "just enough" on a remix to make it "a little interesting," but "not so much that Billboard won't count it" towards the original song's chart position. This strategy, though sometimes resulting in remixes that don't travel far from their origins, paradoxically led to all-time chart records in the late 2010s. "However, for most modern day hitmakers and their labels, the object of the remix game is to ensure that just enough is changed on a remix to make it a little interesting, but not so much that Billboard won't count it."
**The Insta Remix (2020s)** : By the 2020s, the "Insta remix" became a "well-established chart gambit" and "almost unremarkable." This tactic consistently propelled songs to number one, exemplified by hits like Megan Thee Stallion's "Savage Remix," Doja Cat's "Say So remix," and The Weeknd's "Save Your Tears" duet with Ariana Grande, demonstrating its continued effectiveness in the digital era. "Now in the 2020s, the Insta remix is such a well-established chart gambit, it's become almost unremarkable."
