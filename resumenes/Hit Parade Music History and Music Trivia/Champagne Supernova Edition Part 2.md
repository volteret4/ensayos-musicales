## albums
**(What's the Story) Morning Glory? (1995) - Record-Breaking Sophomore Album** : Oasis's second album arrived weeks after Blur's The Great Escape and overshadowed it, turning out to be a "Wembley-sized commercial juggernaut." In its first week in the UK it sold a record-breaking 345,000 copies, roughly double Blur's first-week figure. Two advance singles preceded it, "Some Might Say" and "Roll With It," but Noel Gallagher had saved the best tracks for later. In the US it entered the top five and went platinum by March 1996, and by the end of 1996 it was certified quadruple platinum in America for four million copies, nearly as much as it sold in the UK. That success made Oasis the top Britpop band in the world. "Noel Gallagher had clearly saved the best tracks for later."
**Be Here Now (1997) - Fastest-Selling Album in British History** : Oasis's third album got a heroes' welcome in England, and the press noted that it landed just weeks after Tony Blair's victory as the new UK Prime Minister. It opened at number one, a month after its lead single did. It sold more than 400,000 copies on its first day and nearly 700,000 in its first week, making it the fastest-selling album in British history, a record it held until Adele's 25 in 2015. In America it debuted at number two on the Billboard 200, which was remarkable for any Britpop band and functioned as a referendum on the US popularity of Morning Glory. The press quickly soured on it, calling it a monument to cocaine-fuelled excess full of interminably long songs. It was out of the US top 10 in two weeks, never to return, though it still went platinum. Critic Jon Savage later identified Be Here Now as the moment the Britpop movement ended, because it fell short critically and commercially and showed that Britpop had grown indulgent and was no longer at the vanguard of British rock. "a monument to cocaine-fueled excess, filled with interminably long songs."

## songs
**Some Might Say (1995) - First UK Number One** : Released in April 1995, this was Oasis's first UK number one. It was a standalone single that would later appear on the second album, and it was part of Noel Gallagher's emergence as British rock royalty. According to legend, its success sparked the Blur–Oasis feud. "In April 1995, Oasis scored their first UK number one song."
**Roll With It (1995) - Loser of the Chart War** : This advance track from the second album was described as "a catchy trifle." It was scheduled for summer release, and Damon Albarn moved Blur's "Country House" to the same date, Monday, August 14, 1995. "Roll With It" sold 216,000 copies to Country House's 274,000 and lost the chart battle. Critics speculated that Blur benefited from pent-up demand, having been away from the charts a few months longer than Oasis. "Country House sold 274,000 copies to roll with its 216,000."
**Morning Glory (1995) - Title Track** : The title track echoed the sound of 1980s R.E.M. Its lyrics referenced cocaine and waking up with "morning wood." The song was "typically lyrically obtuse, yet bracing." "echoed the sound of 80s REM and included lyrical references to cocaine and waking up with Morning Wood."
**Wonderwall (1995) - Signature Song and US Breakthrough** : Noel Gallagher named the song after an obscure piece of Beatles arcana: George Harrison's 1968 instrumental solo album Wonderwall Music, which doubled as the soundtrack to the psychedelic film Wonderwall. In the film the word refers to a literal wall through which the protagonist peers at his glamorous neighbours, and the word held no particular meaning for Harrison. The song echoes the Beatles' Help! period, and video director Nigel Dick styled the video to resemble the 1965 Beatles movie. It contains Noel's most unabashedly heartfelt lyrics, about being saved by love, sung in a plaintive vocal by Liam Gallagher. It peaked at number two in the UK behind a number one by the pop crooner duo Robson & Jerome, but went on to sell more than three million UK copies, by far Oasis's best seller. In the US it hit number one on the Modern Rock chart in December 1995 and stayed there an unprecedented 10 weeks. It also reached number eight on the Hot 100 by March 1996, where no Britpop-associated single had previously cracked the top 40. "It remains the single for which Oasis are best known."
**Don't Look Back in Anger (1996) - Noel-Sung Number One** : This single featured a rare lead vocal by Noel rather than Liam Gallagher. It became Oasis's second-ever UK number one in March 1996. "with a rare lead vocal by no, not Liam Gallagher, became Oasis second ever number one hit in March 96."
**Champagne Supernova (1995) - US Modern Rock Chart-Topper** : The follow-up to "Wonderwall" in America was "anthemic and equally nonsensically titled." It reached number one on the Modern Rock chart for five weeks. "Champagne Supernova also reached number one at Modern Rock for five weeks."
**D'You Know What I Mean? (1997) - Seven-Minute Imperial Single** : This lead single from Be Here Now was Noel Gallagher's "Imperial move," a single "reveling in its own bigness" that ran more than seven minutes. Its chorus, "All my people right here right now, do you know what I mean," was described as a meaningless rally for popularity. Critic Tom Ewing later called it "the victory, the hangover, or both at once," saying the chorus translates as "vote Oasis." It opened at number one in the UK, but in America it reached only number 49 on the Hot 100 Airplay chart. "That chorus translates as vote Oasis."
**Don't Go Away (1997) - US Follow-Up Single** : This follow-up single from Be Here Now did better on the US charts than "D'You Know What I Mean?" Even so, American listeners "did just that. They went away." "Had Americans did just that. They went away."

Blur had never placed higher than number five on the British singles chart, which was where "Girls & Boys" had peaked the year before. **Acrimonious 2009 Breakup** : Oasis soldiered on for another decade to diminishing returns, putting out several more solid-selling UK albums through the mid-2000s. They broke up in acrimony in 2009 after "the umpteenth feud" between Noel and Liam Gallagher. "breaking up in Acrimony in 2009 after the umpteenth feud between Noel and Liam Gallagher."

## songs
**Girls & Boys (1994) - Previous Chart Peak** : Before the chart war, this song's number five placing was Blur's highest position on the British singles chart. "which was where girls and boys had peaked the year before."
**Country House (1995) - Winner of the Britpop Chart War** : The lead single from the Parklife follow-up was "a bit of class system satire." After Noel Gallagher's taunting at the Oasis party, Albarn told the label to move its release to Monday, August 14, 1995, the same day as Oasis's "Roll With It." The NME ran a cover story billing a "British heavyweight championship," and the BBC called it "the biggest chart war in 30 years." To win, Blur commissioned a video directed by the edgy British artist Damien Hirst and released two CD singles with alternate B-sides. It sold 274,000 copies to Roll With It's 216,000. Critics speculated that Blur benefited from pent-up demand after being away from the charts a few months longer. "Albarn instructed his label to move the release date for Country House to Monday, August 14th, 1995, the same day as Roll With It."
**The Universal (1995)** : One of the four UK top 10 hits from The Great Escape. "including the Universal and Charmless Man."
**Charmless Man (1995)** : Another of the four UK top 10 hits from The Great Escape. "including the Universal and Charmless Man."
**Beetlebum (1997) - Second UK Number One** : The lead-off track of the 1997 self-titled album was a more overt Beatles homage than anything Oasis had attempted lately. AllMusic critic Stephen Thomas Erlewine called the "druggy ditty" "a run through the White Album in the space of five minutes." It became Blur's second UK number one in February 1997. "a run through the white album in the space of five minutes."
**Song 2 (1997) - The "Woo-Hoo" Song and Biggest US Hit** : The song was track two on the CD and ran two minutes two seconds, so lacking a better title Blur called it "Song 2." Graham Coxon said they wrote it as a gag on their record company, "a piss take" showing how dopey a single could sound, but the label thought it sounded like a hit. It sounds more American than any other Blur single, and critics debate whether it parodies grunge, pop punk, or heavy metal, as Albarn sings in the chorus. Many listeners, especially in sports stadiums, know it as "the Woo Hoo song." It was never issued as a US single and was therefore ineligible for the Hot 100. It still reached number 55 on Billboard's Hot 100 Airplay chart, number six on Modern Rock (where it spent half a year), and number 25 on mainstream rock alongside Sammy Hagar and Aerosmith. "a piss take to demonstrate how dopey a single could sound but the record company wasn't laughing to them it sounded like a hit."

# artist - Radiohead

## genres

## albums
**The Bends (1995) - Most Anthemic Album** : Radiohead's 1995 second album was their most anthemic, "perfectly suited to UK Radio in 1995." It spun off five hit singles, the most of any Radiohead album, including the top 20 hits "High and Dry," "Street Spirit (Fade Out)," and "Just." Radiohead wanted nothing to do with Britpop, just as the 1993 Radiohead had nothing to do with grunge, but they were swept into the movement anyway. Pitchfork later ranked The Bends the third-greatest Britpop album ever, with its critic writing that Radiohead "were never a Britpop band" but "issued a doomed cry from the party's cellar." Its sound, blended with Britpop tunefulness, later shaped Travis and Coldplay. "radiohead were never a Britpop band, but as Britpop fever erupted into a Dionysian free for all, these Oxford Oddballs issued a doomed cry."
**OK Computer (1997) - Leaving Britpop in the Dust** : With this innovative album, Radiohead came into their own and represented the new vanguard of British rock. It left Britpop behind and eventually outsold Oasis's latest album in America. "Okay Computer left Brit pop in the dust, and even eventually outsold Oasis latest in America."

**Lifting Older Bands' Fortunes** : The movement improved the fortunes of Lush, The Boo Radleys and Black Grape, and made 1980s veterans like Morrissey and Paul Weller newly relevant. "And the movement improved several bands' fortunes."
**Ignored in America** : In the US, most Britpop bypassed the charts. A year after Kurt Cobain's death, alternative rock moved toward pop punk (Green Day), ska punk (Rancid) and post-grunge instead. "In America, most of Britpop was bypassing the charts altogether."
**A Misnomer** : To Britons, the term meant mid-1990s UK guitar rock with catchy songs, not pop boy bands or girl groups. "The very word Britpop is something of a misnomer."
**Synths Were Allowed** : Despite being guitar-centric, Britpop did not shy away from dance music or synth-pop; hits by Blur and Pulp used 1980s-style synthesizers. "Britpop as guitar centric as it was had not shied away from dance music or synth pop."
**Legacy** : Travis and Coldplay blended The Bends-era Radiohead with Britpop tunefulness. In the early 2000s English rock fans turned to American garage bands led by The Strokes, while Arctic Monkeys and Gorillaz later represented Britpop's descendants in the US. "A new generation of UK bands rushed in to fill the void."
**End of the Movement** : Critic Jon Savage pinpointed Oasis's Be Here Now as the moment Britpop ended. "identified Oasis Be Here Now as the moment where the movement ended."

# curiosity

**Clueless Soundtrack (1995)** : The summer 1995 American teen movie featured Supergrass's "Alright" and Radiohead's "Fake Plastic Trees." "The song even made the soundtrack of the summer 95 American team movie, Clueless."
**Trainspotting (1996) - Britpop's Cinematic Artifact** : Danny Boyle's adaptation of Irvine Welsh's novel about Scottish heroin addicts starred a young Ewan McGregor and was the top-grossing British film of 1996. It did for 1990s Cool Britannia what Saturday Night Fever did for 1970s disco. Boyle skipped a score in favour of needle drops, juxtaposing Iggy Pop's "Lust for Life" and New Order's "Temptation" with Britpop from Blur, Elastica, Sleeper and Pulp, and closing with Underworld's "Born Slippy." "its soundtrack was an instant classic."
**Britpop Second Tier** : By 1996 a constellation of bands below Oasis, Blur, Pulp and Elastica, including Menswear, Sleeper and Cast, were scoring plenty of hits. "Britpop had spawned a whole constellation of bands several tiers below Oasis, Blur, Pulp and Elastica in popularity."
**US Pop Landscape in 1997** : US pop was dominated by boy bands like Backstreet Boys, frilly alt-pop like Matchbox 20 and Third Eye Blind, and flossy hip hop from Puff Daddy. That left little room for Britpop. "There was little chance of Britpop breaking through that hit parade."
