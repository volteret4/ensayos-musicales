# curiosity

**TV Music Curation Post-Miami Vice (1980s)** : In the 1980s, following the success of *Miami Vice*, TV music supervisors began curating songs for maximum exposure, effectively turning under-appreciated gems into hits by strategically placing them in shows. "in the wake of Miami Vices success, TV music supervisors were curating songs for maximum exposure and turning under-appreciated gems into hits."
**Movies Leveraging Song Resurrections (Late 1980s - Early 1990s)** : Initially, movies, more so than TV, took advantage of the trend of bringing back older songs, exemplified by *Ferris Bueller's Day Off* featuring The Beatles' "Twist and Shout" and the 1986 film *Stand By Me* with Ben E. King's song, and *Wayne's World* resurrecting Queen's "Bohemian Rhapsody" in 1992. "at first movies more than TV took advantage of the trend bringing back such hits as The Beatles Twist and Shout from Ferris Bueller's Day Off, Ben E King's Stand By Me from the 1986 movie of the same name and of course Queen's Bohemian Rhapsody which was resurrected in 1992 by a TV sketch comedy routine turned hit movie Wingsworld."
**TV Breaking Artists and Songs in 90s** : In the 1990s, television still held the power to launch both artists and songs, as illustrated by the fictional band The Heights achieving a real-world number one hit, demonstrating TV's continued influence beyond just theme songs. "It was still possible for TV in the 90s to break artists as well as songs."
**TV Extending Hit Life** : TV shows possessed the ability to rebrand contemporary hits and significantly extend their chart life, even if the songs had already peaked independently, as demonstrated by Paula Cole's "I Don't Want to Wait" and *Dawson's Creek*. "Even when a TV show picked a proven contemporary hit for a sink, the TV association could rebrand that hit and extend its life."
**Millennium TV Music Strategy (Early 2000s)** : At the turn of the new millennium, TV showrunners and music supervisors actively sought "sinks"—songs their shows could, in essence, "own." This strategy prioritized previously undiscovered or underheralded songs that the show could help popularize. "Entering the new millennium, TV showrunners and music supervisors were looking further afield for sinks as the term is now known. Songs their TV shows could, in a sense, own."
**iTunes Launch Coincidence (2003)** : The premiere of *The O.C.* and its theme song "California" happened to coincide with the launch of Apple's iTunes music store, which played a significant role in the song becoming a best-selling digital download. "the premiere of The OC happened to coincide with the launch of Apple's iTunes music store."
**"Don't Stop Believin'" - Most Debated TV Sink** : Journey's "Don't Stop Believin'" was arguably the most celebrated and debated TV "sink" of the 2000s, though its revival was not solely due to television. "But probably the most celebrated and debated TV Sink of the Decade was this diddy."
**Billboard Re-entry Rules (2000s)** : Billboard's rules during the mid-2000s explicitly precluded older songs from re-entering the Hot 100 if they did not have current pop radio airplay, impacting the chart performance of TV-resurrected songs like Journey's "Don't Stop Believin'." "Billboards rules at this time precluded old songs without current pop radio airplay from re-entering the Hot 100."
