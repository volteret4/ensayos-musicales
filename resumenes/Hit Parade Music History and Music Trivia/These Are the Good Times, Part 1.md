## albums
**Sheik (1978) - Debut LP** : Sheik's self-titled debut album broke into the album charts top 30 in February 1978 and was certified gold by March 1978. True to Nile Rogers' vision, the album cover emulated Roxy Music's approach, featuring a pair of models with whistles in their mouths instead of band members or its female singers. It generated two top 40 hits: "Everybody Dance" and "Dance, Dance, Dance (Yowsah, Yowsah, Yowsah)". An album cut, "You Can Get By," also reached number one on the Disco Chart. "Sheep's self-titled debut LP broke into the album charts top 30."
**C'est Chic (1978) - Second Album** : While "Le Freak" was at number one, *C'est Chic* reached number four on the album chart the week of Christmas 1978 and was certified platinum virtually out of the box. More than their debut, this album represented the cutting edge of polished, high-octane dance music, particularly with its second single "I Want Your Love," which foreshadowed the sound of new wave dance music. "As for the Seisheak album, it was platinum virtually out of the box."

## songs
**Everybody Dance (1977)** : Nile Rogers wrote "Everybody Dance" as a showcase for Sheik's instrumental prowess, featuring Bernard Edwards' unique bass part, Tony Thompson's relentless four-on-the-floor beat, and Nile's signature "chicken scratch" guitar. Luther Vandross arranged the vocals, which included himself, Diva Gray, Robin Clark (on background), and Norma Jean Wright (on lead). Despite seemingly simple, decadent lyrics, the song had intricate vocal arrangements and instrumental chops. Before being signed to a label, a lacquer test pressing generated immense buzz at New York's The Night Owl club. It was eventually released as a single, reaching number 38 on the pop chart by June 1978 and number one on the Disco Chart. "Nile Rogers wrote everybody dance as essentially a flex, a showcase for Sheep."
**Dance, Dance, Dance (Yowsah, Yowsah, Yowsah) (1977)** : Co-written by Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards, this complex R&B track combined jazzy chords with seemingly frivolous lyrics and a parenthetical subtitle, "Yowsah, Yowsah, Yowsah," a jazz phrase from the Roaring Twenties. Its infectious quality led to Sheik being signed by Atlantic Records in 1977 after initially being passed over, especially after a smaller label, Buddha Records, released an early pressing that created strong DJ and dance floor buzz. Reissued by Atlantic in summer 1977, it first topped Billboard's Dance Chart by December, then broke into the pop top 40, and reached number six on the Hot 100 in February 1978. "It was the infectious dance, dance, dance, Yausa, Yausa, Yausa that finally got Sheep signed to a label in 1977."
**Le Freak (1978)** : The best-selling single in Atlantic Records history, this song originated from Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards being denied entry to the exclusive New York nightclub Studio 54. Initially conceived as "Awww, F*** Off! F*** Studio 54!", Edwards suggested changing the expletive to "freak," and Nile then proposed "freak out" in reference to a bad LSD trip. Sung by Alfa Anderson (who took over primary vocal duties after Norma Jean went solo) and Diva Gray, the "Frankophile duo" (Rogers and Edwards) created a track that was unusually wordy for Sheik, with verses that sounded like advertising copy and a chant-like chorus. Released in early fall 1978, it took only seven weeks to reach number one on the Hot 100, where it achieved a rare "hat trick" by returning to the top spot three times, accumulating six weeks at number one. Its chart run outperformed the combined weeks of Barbara Streisand & Neil Diamond's "You Don't Bring Me Flowers" and The Bee Gees' "Too Much Heaven." By the end of its run, it had sold six million copies in the US, making it unofficially the first "triple platinum" single. "Le Freak, as the Frankophile duo called their song, was unusually wordy for shick."
**You Can Get By (1978)** : An album cut from Sheik's self-titled debut LP, this shuffling track featured a rare lead vocal performance by Bernard Edwards himself. It achieved significant success in the clubs, reaching number one on the Disco Chart, following the path of "Dance, Dance, Dance" and "Everybody Dance." "A shuffling album cut called You Can Get By featuring a rare lead vocal by Bernard Edwards himself."
**I Want Your Love (1979)** : This song was the second single released from the *C'est Chic* album, becoming a number seven hit in the spring of 1979. Its distinctive chiming bell sounds were noted for forecasting the sound of new wave dance music that would emerge in the subsequent decade, drawing comparisons to Donna Summer's pioneering "I Feel Love." "With its chiming bell sounds, I Want Your Love forecasted the sound of new wave dance music in the decade to come."
**Good Times (1979)** : Released in 1979, "Good Times" became Sheik's final chart-topping hit and their last top 40 hit, reaching number one on the Billboard Hot 100 the week ending August 18, 1979. One week later, it was replaced by a new number one that rock fans at the time perceived as a "blow against bands like Sheik." The song's iconic bassline is recognized as its greatest legacy, later credited with helping to invent a genre. "Good Times. Not only their last number one hit, but their last top 40 hit."

## curiosities
**Inventing the Sound of the Next Decade** : Unwittingly, Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards, through Sheik, played a pivotal role in inventing the sound of the 1980s. Their style inspired the formation of other bands, their masterminds produced major hits for pop legends, and their sound was "pilfered" by chart-topping bands they had no direct involvement with. They are also credited with "accidentally launched the most important musical shift of the last 40 years," referencing hip-hop, particularly through the influence of the "Good Times" bassline. "unwittingly, Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards of Sheik were helping to invent the sound of the next decade."
**The Sheik Organization, Limited** : Upon signing with Atlantic Records, Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards established a corporation named The Sheik Organization, Limited. This entity was formed to manage their diverse array of projects, reflecting their self-perception as a collective rather than a conventional band, which often featured varying players and singers on individual tracks and albums. "In fact, when they signed with Atlantic, Rogers and Edwards formed a corporation, the Sheik Organization, limited, to manage their array of projects."
**Overlooked by Rock and Roll Hall of Fame** : Despite the profound influence Sheik had on the trajectory of rock music, the band was not a "shoe-in" for the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame. This suggests a disconnect in recognition from rock fans, potentially stemming from the "disco sucks" backlash of the late 1970s. "You'd think a band with this much influence on rock's trajectory would have been shoe-ins for the rock and roll hall of fame."
**Sophisticated Image and Future-Sounding Music** : In an era characterized by bell-bottom jeans and polyester leisurewear, Sheik cultivated a stylish, "natty," and elegant image. Nile Rogers, Bernard Edwards, and drummer Tony Thompson wore sharp suits, while their singers, led by Alfa Anderson, donned reinvented jazz-age outfits. This visual "classy throwback" contrasted sharply with their music, which sounded like the future. "Sheik looked like a very classy throwback, but their music sounded like the future."
**"Faceless Band" Concept** : Nile Rogers conceived of Sheik as a "faceless backup band professionally," drawing inspiration from two rock acts: Roxy Music's album covers (which featured glamorous models instead of band members like Brian Ferry, Amanda Lear, or Marilyn Cole) for sophistication, and KISS's full makeup for theatrical anonymity. Ironically, this concept became a hard lesson in "facelessness" when Rogers and Edwards were denied entry to Studio 54 because they weren't recognized. "Nile wanted to invert what KISS had done, writing in his memoir quote, what if we played the faceless backup band professionally?"
**Adaptation and Underestimation** : The enduring success of Sheik's sound and its extended "universe of hits" well into the 2010s is a testament to the band's remarkable adaptability and refutes the notion of disco's short-lived phenomenon. Their work, alongside that of Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards with other acts, also challenges the idea that rock killed disco, instead showing how disco elements were absorbed by rock, despite their underestimation by some. "The story of the Sheik universe of hits right through the 2010s is one of both adaptation and underestimation."

## curiosities
**Tumultuous Childhood and Early Musical Exposure (1952-1968)** : Born in 1952 to a 13-year-old Beverly Goodman, Nile experienced a tumultuous childhood, shuttling between New York and Los Angeles and various relatives' or his mother's boyfriends' homes for his first 16 years. His biological father, Nile Roger Sr., a traveling musician, was seen only occasionally and often inebriated, while his mother and white stepfather were both heroin addicts. Despite this, he grew up surrounded by music, learning flute and clarinet in school. Around 1968, at 15 in the Bronx, he taught himself guitar; initially struggling with tuning "A Day in the Life" until his mother's live-in boyfriend taught him how to tune his guitar properly. "Born in 1952 to a 13-year-old Beverly Goodman."
**Ability to Read Music** : Rogers cited his ability to read music, developed during his years learning wind instruments like the flute and clarinet in school, as a significant factor that distinguished him from other guitarists. This skill helped him secure gigs in his late teens and 20s. "Roger's later said that his ability to read music thanks to his years with wind instruments set him apart from other guitarists and got him gigs in his late teens and 20s."
**Manhattan Music Scene Immersion (Late 1960s)** : After moving to Manhattan in the late 1960s, Rogers discovered an ideal environment for a budding musician. He became a regular at Max's Kansas City, where he saw The Velvet Underground multiple times and played with his first band, New World Rising. Identifying as a "hippie" more interested in avant-garde and jazz (influenced by his stepfather) than soul, he honed his guitar skills by studying with jazz and classical guitarists. "Indeed, once the teenager moved out on his own and settled in Manhattan in the late 60s, he discovered he'd stumbled onto an ideal scene for a budding musician."
**Professional Debut with Sesame Street** : Rogers marked his transition to a professional musician with a touring gig as a guitarist for the live road show of the hit PBS children's program, *Sesame Street*. This experience allowed him to tour the country and meet the show's cast, one of whom, Loretta Long (who played Susan), helped him secure his next significant gig. "The HIT PBS Children's program Sesame Street hired Nile Rogers as a guitarist for its live road show."
**Apollo Theater's Influence on "Groove"** : Rogers' time in the house band at Harlem's Apollo Theater was crucial for his musical development. On his very first night, he backed Screamin' Jay Hawkins, who hazed him onstage. More importantly, the "old timers" in the Apollo band taught him how to truly play funk and soul using R&B notation, infusing his previously classical and jazz-trained playing with a distinct "groove" that he then applied to subsequent club and session work. "The Apollo gave Nile his groove, which he then poured into more club and session."
**Meeting Bernard Edwards** : Nile Rogers met Bernard Edwards at a gig in the Bronx with Journeyman Hack Bartholomew, where Rogers spontaneously joined the band on stage. Edwards, who was "natty dressed" and initially unimpressed by Rogers' "hippie threads," recognized Nile's guitar skills when he effortlessly adapted to play The Meters' syncopated funk classic, "Sissy Strut." The two became inseparable collaborators. "One night at a gig in the Bronx by Journeyman, Hack Bartholomew, Nile jumped on stage to join the band, and he met a Natalie Dressed bassist named Bernard Edwards."
**Influences for Sheik's Mystique** : To craft Sheik's sophisticated mystique, Rogers drew inspiration from two contrasting rock bands. He was wowed by British art rockers Roxy Music's glam style (especially Brian Ferry) and their use of glamorous models like Amanda Lear and Marilyn Cole on album covers, leading to his "faceless" band concept. He also took cues from American glam metal band KISS's onstage gimmick of performing in full makeup, aiming to invert it by having Sheik professionally play as an anonymous backup band. He sought to combine these with the intricacy of jazz, influenced by artists like Herbie Hancock and Roy Ayers, who were branching into funk and R&B. "Nile did have some heady ideas of his own about how the band could maintain its Sheik mystique."
**Signature "Chicken Scratch" Guitar Style** : Nile Rogers developed an irresistibly syncopated, danceable, funk-and-R&B guitar style, which became his signature "chicken scratch" technique. Bernard Edwards specifically encouraged Rogers to play in this rhythmic manner to complement his distinctive bass lines, making it a foundational element of their sound. "Edwards' patented, chucking-based style is now complemented by Rogers' equally rhythmic, danceable, funk-and-d guitar, an irresistibly syncopated style that Edwards encouraged Rogers to play to complement his bass line."
**Rock Icon to Pop Star Producer** : After Sheik's primary chart run, Nile Rogers became a highly influential producer, noted for his ability to "turn certain rock icons into pop stars" from the early days of MTV through the dawn of the 1990s. His production work helped bridge the gap between rock and pop, integrating danceable, disco-informed sounds into mainstream music. "Nile Rogers would turn certain rock icons into pop stars, from the dawn of MTV. To the dawn of the 1990s."
**Possible Inventor of Hip-Hop Brand Name Trope** : According to Nile Rogers, he may have inadvertently originated the now common hip-hop trope of shouting out brand names. This occurred when he instructed Kathy Sledge of Sister Sledge to sing the line "Halston, Gucci, Fiorucci" in their hit "He's the Greatest Dancer," a detail he had to convince the religiously raised singer to perform. "According to Rogers, he may have even invented the now common hip-hop trope of shouting out brand names when Kathy sang, From the moment it dropped, the Sister Sledge album sounded like a chic, backed, greatest hits album."

## curiosities
**Brooklyn Background and R&B Roots** : Raised in Brooklyn, Bernard Edwards was born just weeks apart from Nile Rogers. Edwards initially presented as a "natty dressed" traditional R&B player, who was not impressed by Rogers' "hippie threads." "Raised in Brooklyn, Edwards was the same age as Rogers."
**Distinctive "Chucking" Bass Approach** : Edwards was recognized for his unique "distinctive, fleet-fingered, chucking approach on the bass." This patented bass style became a signature element of Sheik's sound, with Nile Rogers believing that "nobody but Edwards could play" the intricate bass part of "Everybody Dance." "What set him apart was his distinctive, fleet-fingered, chucking approach on the bass."
**Musical Director for New York City (1973)** : In 1973, Edwards secured a pivotal gig as the musical director for the R&B vocal troupe New York City, which had a number 17 hit with "I'm Doing Fine Now." This role required both Edwards and Nile Rogers, as backing musicians, to master contemporary R&B styles like Philadelphia Soul. "By 1973, Edwards landed a gig that would prove pivotal for both of them. He became the musical director for an R&B vocal troupe calling itself New York City."
**Proposing the Name "Sheik"** : Following a name conflict for their band, The Big Apple Band, with Walter Murphy's "A Fifth of Beethoven," Edwards, described as "ever-sophisticated," proposed the name "Sheik." Nile Rogers initially found the name "funny" and "a bit pretentious" but ultimately couldn't come up with a better alternative. "Rogers and Edwards realized their unrelated band needed a new name, and the ever-sophisticated Bernard Edwards proposed the name Sheik."
**Simplifying Nile Rogers' Arrangements** : Edwards possessed a talent for simplifying Nile Rogers' "overly busy arrangements." This skill was instrumental in establishing Sheik's signature template of "simplicity masking complexity" in their songs. "He was no nonsense and had a knack for simplifying Nile's overly busy arrangements."
**Continued Production Success (Post-Sheik)** : After Sheik's final chart-topping hit, Bernard Edwards, alongside Nile Rogers, remained a potent force in music production, successfully producing a variety of hits for other artists for decades, thus "keeping the Good Times coming" long after Sheik's primary chart run. "As we'll discuss Bernard Edwards and Nile Rogers scarcely disappeared from the top of the charts. They kept the Good Times coming for decades."
**Rare Lead Vocal on "You Can Get By"** : Edwards provided a rare lead vocal performance on the Sheik album cut "You Can Get By," a shuffling track from their self-titled debut LP. This song reached number one on the Disco Chart. "A shuffling album cut called You Can Get By featuring a rare lead vocal by Bernard Edwards himself."

## albums

"Every track was a banger, and many of them scored club, black, and pop radio airplay, like the R&B hit Lost in Music."
**Thinking of You (1979)** : This song from the *We Are Family* album, produced by Bernard Edwards and Nile Rogers, was highlighted as an "all-time favorite Chic organization single." Its quality contributed to the album's reputation as a consistent collection of hits. "my all-time favorite chic organization single Thinking of You."
**We Are Family (1979)** : Bernard Edwards and Nile Rogers specifically wrote this title track for Sister Sledge, which became a powerful national "feel-good sing-along" anthem in 1979, a year marked by national unrest. The single went gold, returning Sister Sledge to number one on the R&B chart and reaching number two on the pop chart, positioned between Donna Summer's "Hot Stuff" and Anita Ward's "Ring My Bell." It was famously adopted by the 1979 Pittsburgh Pirates as a backdrop to their World Series championship. "Bernard Edwards and Nile Rogers wrote the album's title track specifically for Sister Sledge."

**US Breakthrough and British Invasion (1964)** : The Beatles' monumental breakthrough in the United States in 1964 initiated the British Invasion, significantly altering the American music landscape. While this era made it more challenging for established easy-listening crooners, girl groups, and doo-wop acts, it did not completely eradicate these older styles, as many continued to find success. "the US breakthrough of The Beatles in 1964, which spawned the British invasion in America, did make it harder for easy-listening crew-ners, girl groups, and do-up."

This club was used as a historical comparison by Nile Rogers to describe the similar formative role that Max's Kansas City played in his own early musical development. "Max's Kansas City was to Nile Rogers what the Kaiser Keller in Hamburg was to the Beatles, the club where he honed his chops."
