## albums
**Run DMC (1984) - Self-Titled Debut** : This album, co-produced by Run's brother Russell Simmons and Larry Smith, pioneered by placing rock elements front and center in a rap recording. It achieved gold certification just before Christmas 1984, selling half a million copies within nine months of its release, marking a significant milestone for a rap album. "The album, co-produced by Run's brother Russell Simmons, and celebrated hip hop producer Larry Smith, placed rock elements front and center, like no rap recording had before."
**King of Rock (1985) - Sophomore Album** : Issued by Profile in early 1985, this album's lead single video famously featured the group crashing an imaginary museum of rock artifacts to "diss" rock and pop acts. It quickly surpassed its predecessor's sales, achieving RIAA gold certification in June 1985, less than four months after its release. "The album went gold even faster than its predecessor."
**Raising Hell (1986) - Breakthrough Album** : Released in May 1986, this was Run DMC's third album and their first full production by Rick Rubin. It featured "Walk This Way" as its second single, a track instrumental in elevating Run DMC to platinum superstar status. "Walk This Way materialized in May 1986 on Raising Hell, Run DMC's third album and first full Rick Rubin production."

## songs
**Sucker MC's (1983)** : This debut single, coupled with "It's Like That," revolutionized rap by breaking existing patterns and kicking off the genre's second wave. It introduced a minimalist, glowering sound that contrasted sharply with previous lavish and festive hip-hop, along with a more sharply defined MC personality. "Sucker MC's run DMC's debut 1983 single paired with the flip side, it's like that. Broke virtually every pattern in rap to that date, and kicked off the second wave."
**It's Like That (1983)** : Released as the flip side to their debut single "Sucker MC's," this track contributed to Run DMC's groundbreaking minimalist and hard-edged sound. It helped to establish a new sonic footprint for rap, diverging from the prevailing styles of the time. "Sucker MC's run DMC's debut 1983 single paired with the flip side, it's like that."
**Rock Box (1984)** : As the third single from their self-titled debut album, this track was notable for featuring "squealing metal style guitar" played by Axeman Eddie Martinez. It demonstrated Run DMC's early and bold integration of rock elements into their rap music. "Rockbox, the album's third single, featured squealing metal style guitar by Axeman Eddie Martinez, who went on to play with everyone from David Lee Roth to Robert Palmer."
**My Adidas (1986)** : This lead-off single from the "Raising Hell" album served as a commemoration of Run DMC's Live Aid performance. Backed by "Peter Piper," it reached number five on the Hot Black singles chart, becoming Run DMC's biggest R&B hit and notably spawning a sponsorship deal with Adidas. "Backed by the potent B-side Peter Piper, Maya Ditas, which actually spawned a sponsorship deal with the shoemaker, got as high as number five on Hot Black singles. Run DMC's biggest R&B hit."
**Walk This Way (1986)** : This landmark 1986 reinvention of Aerosmith's 1970s hit, featuring Steven Tyler and Joe Perry, became rap's first top 10 hit on the pop charts, peaking at number four on the Billboard Hot 100 on September 27, 1986. It was pivotal in rap achieving platinum sales for the first time and its influential music video, directed by John Small, famously depicted the breaking of a wall between rock and rap, securing significant MTV rotation. "What is indisputable is it was rap's first top 10 hit on the pop charts, and it was the centerpiece of a year when rap went platinum for the first time."

## curiosities
**Early Rock Integration** : Joseph Run Simmons, Darryl DMC McDaniels, and Jason Jam Master J Myzel boldly positioned themselves as the future of both rap and rock and roll, integrating rock elements into their music from the start. They openly demanded a place at rock's cultural table, establishing a distinctive identity for the genre. "For years before Walk This Way, Joseph run Simmons, Darryl DMC McDaniels, and Jason Jam Master J. Myzel had been boldly presenting themselves as the future, not just of rap, but of rock and roll, openly demanding a seat at Rock's culture-commanding table and incorporating rock elements into their music from the start."
**Distinctive Style and Presentation** : Run DMC rejected the close-fitting denim and breakdance attire common in early rap, instead adopting a stark and powerful uniform of black jackets, black fedoras, gold chains, and unlaced white sneakers. Darryl McDaniels' large, square-framed glasses also made a statement, inspiring Rick Rubin and reflecting the group's "no joke" attitude. "As shewing the close-fitting denim and break dance gear of rap's first wave, Joseph Darrell and Jason came on stage in a uniform as succinct as their music. Black jackets, black fedoras, gold chains, and unlaced white sneakers."
**Live Aid Performance (1985)** : On July 13, 1985, Run DMC made history as the sole rap act invited to perform at Live Aid from the RFK Stadium stage in Philadelphia. They embraced their role as rap ambassadors, with DMC noting, "we have no band, just Jam Master J," a moment Run described as cherished for their careers. "So high was Run DMC's profile by the summer of 1985 that on July 13, they made history by being the only rap act invited to perform at Live Aid."
**Initial Skepticism for "Walk This Way" Remake** : Run DMC was deeply skeptical of Rick Rubin's proposal to collaborate with Aerosmith on "Walk This Way," fearing it would be "corny." DMC famously recalled his objection, calling the idea "hillbilly gibberish, country bumpkin bullshit," and Run even disliked some of the lyrics during the recording session. "Hell no, this ain't gonna happen, DMC later recalled in the Washington Post. This is hillbilly gibberish, country bumpkin bullshit."
**"Walk This Way" Credits Discrepancy (1986)** : For the 1986 single of "Walk This Way," Aerosmith's Steven Tyler and Joe Perry were not credited as artists on the record cover or Billboard charts, which listed the song solely under Run DMC. However, Tyler and Perry received proper credit as the only songwriters in the fine print on the Hot 100 and on the label, as the remake was deemed a new arrangement but not a new lyrical work. "While Aerosmith singer Steven Tyler and guitarist Joe Perry are hard to miss on the Walk This Way remake, Aerosmith were not actually given artist credit on this 1986 single, not even his featured on the cover of the 45 RPM vinyl sold in stores that year and on billboards charts, the song was simply credited to run DMC."
**"The Giving Tree of Hip Hop"** : Despite their pioneering success and bravado, Run DMC's period of peak mainstream dominance was relatively short. They were highly influential in giving rap a distinctive identity and boosting the careers of other acts, including rock artists, effectively paving the way for the genre's future, even as their own time in the sun faded. "For all their bravado, run DMC were the giving tree of hip hop, providing a future for the genre, and a career boost for other acts, including rock acts, while their own days in the sun were relatively short."

# artist - Aerosmith

## albums
**Toys in the Attic (1975) - Acclaimed Album** : This album achieved a million in sales and featured two classic tracks, "Sweet Emotion" and "Walk This Way." Its success was a significant factor in Columbia's decision to reissue "Dream On" as a single in 1976. "It helped spur their album Toys in the Attic to a million in sales, and encouraged Columbia to reissue Dream on as a single in early 1976 when it finally reached the top 10."
**Done With Mirrors (1985) - Comeback Attempt** : Released on their new label, Geffen Records, after Joe Perry's return, this album was widely considered uninspired and an underperformer. It barely reached the top 40 on the album chart, reflecting the band's struggle to connect with audiences during the mid-1980s. "Even after Perry rejoined, their 1985 so-called comeback album, Done With Mirrors, on a new label, Geffen Records, was an underperformer and, by general agreement, the band's least inspired."

## songs
**Dream On (1973/1976)** : Initially released in 1973, this song did not reach the top 40 on its first run. However, following the success of subsequent acclaimed albums and hits, Columbia reissued it as a single in early 1976, when it finally broke into the top 10. "Dream on two tries to become a hit. The original 1973 release peaked well short of the top 40, but after several acclaimed albums began to sell, the band started scoring serious radio hits."
**Sweet Emotion (1975)** : This classic track became Aerosmith's first top 40 hit in the summer of 1975 and contributed to "Toys in the Attic" selling a million copies. Its unique sound featured Steven Tyler's syncopated, black-derived rhythms on the verses, punctuated by the band's catchy, blues-derived start-stop rhythms. "The classic Sweet Emotion was Aerosmith's first top 40 hit in the summer of 1975."
**Walk This Way (1975/1977)** : From the "Toys in the Attic" album, this song was first released as a single in summer 1975 and later peaked at number 10 on the Hot 100 in February 1977. Guitarist Joe Perry drew inspiration from New Orleans funk legends The Meters for its Boogie Rock groove, while Steven Tyler "rapped" the nonsensical, tongue-twisting lyrics about a high school boy losing his virginity. "Walk this way like Dream on took two tries to become a hit."
**Walk This Way (1986) - Run DMC Remake** : While not given artist credit on the 1986 single, Steven Tyler and Joe Perry provided additional guitar and vocals to Run DMC's landmark reinvention of their 70s hit. They were credited as the sole songwriters, a crucial factor in the song reaching number four on the Billboard Hot 100 and revitalizing Aerosmith's career. "Steven Tyler and guitarist Joe Perry are hard to miss on the Walk This Way remake."

## curiosities
**"Toxic Twins" Era** : By the late 1970s, Steven Tyler and Joe Perry earned the moniker "Toxic Twins" within the music industry due to their extensive drug use. This period coincided with Joe Perry's departure from the band in 1979 for five years and a general downturn in the band's fortunes, as they released only three studio albums between 1979 and 1985, none achieving radio success. "By the late 70s, the band became infamous for their drug intake. The music industry dubbed Tyler and Perry the Toxic Twins."
**Band in Wilderness (1979-1985)** : Aerosmith endured a period in the music business wilderness during the first half of the 1980s. Joe Perry's departure in 1979 lasted five years, and the band released only three studio albums without radio hits. Their 1985 "comeback" album, "Done With Mirrors," was an underperformer and widely considered their least inspired work, barely scraping the top 40. "Aerosmith spent the first half of the 1980s in the music business wilderness."
**Unlikely Collaboration with Run DMC (1986)** : In 1986, Aerosmith was "as out of favor as a formerly multi-platinum rock band could be" when Rick Rubin proposed the "Walk This Way" remake. Steven Tyler, a minor hip-hop fan from buying mixtapes, and Joe Perry, seeing connections to blues, were initially wary but had little to lose, making them receptive to Rubin's desire to legitimize the hip-hop experiment. "They needed Run DMC even more than Run DMC needed them."
**"Walk This Way" Recording Anecdotes** : During the recording session for the "Walk This Way" remake, Steven Tyler and Joe Perry were surprised by Run DMC's unfamiliarity with the song. Joe Perry improvised a bass part by borrowing one from the Beastie Boys, who were also in the studio. Tyler and Perry left the session feeling charmed and bemused, but unsure if the recording would ever be released. "Tyler and Perry were a guest that Run DMC came into the studio barely knowing the song."

## albums
**Radio (1985) - Debut Album** : Released in November 1985, this album received critical acclaim for its sparse and uncluttered production style, a signature of Rick Rubin. The LP cover featured a prominent photo of a boombox, and it became Def Jam's first gold album in April 1986. "Upon its release in November of 1985, the album was critically acclaimed for its sparse and resolutely uncluttered production style."

## songs
**I Need a Beat (1985)** : This was LL Cool J's debut single for Def Jam, produced by Rick Rubin. The title was "aptly coined" for its directness, and the track was a precursor to the hits generated by his "Radio" album in early 1986. "The very apt coinage, reduced by Rick Rubin."
**I Can't Live Without My Radio (1986)** : The titular lead-off single from the "Radio" album, this song reached number 15 on Billboard's Hot Black singles chart in February 1986. Its success contributed to the album's strong performance and Def Jam's growing influence. "Its titular lead-off single, I can't live without my radio, reached number 15 on Billboards Hot Black singles chart in February."
**Rock The Bells (1986)** : Dropping weeks after "I Can't Live Without My Radio," this single peaked at number 17 in April 1986. Its chart success coincided with "Radio" becoming Def Jam's first gold album, solidifying LL Cool J's status as a major solo rap artist. "Weeks later, Rock The Bells dropped, peaking at number 17 in April."

## curiosities
**Origin of Stage Name** : Born James Todd Smith, he adopted the self-given nickname "Ladies Love Cool James" (LL Cool J), envisioning himself as an "irresistible lothario." His charming, handsome, and witty persona made him an easily marketable solo star for magazine profiles and teen pinups. "Charming, handsome, and witty, he gave himself the nickname Ladies Love Cool James, or LL Cool J."
**Early Rapping Career** : LL Cool J began rapping at the young age of nine. By 1984, at 16, he was a recognized talent, and within months of turning 17, Rick Rubin signed him to Def Jam Recordings, leading to his seminal debut album "Radio." "When Rick Rubin met him, Teenage LL had been rapping since age nine."

## albums
**Cookie Puss (1983) - EP** : This EP, a "moronic mix of Jerky Boys style hijinks" and "dumb down rapplingo" set to hip-hop beats and samples, originated from a recorded prank call to the Carvel ice cream chain. It became an underground hit, prompting the Beastie Boys to transition from hardcore punk to rap. ## songs
**Cookie Puss (1983)** : This track, created from a recorded prank call to Carvel, combined "Jerky Boys style hijinks," "dumb down rapplingo," and hip-hop beats and samples. Its underground success was the catalyst for the Beastie Boys' full commitment to rap music. "They recorded the prank call and they turned it into a record."
**Rock Hard (1984)** : This was the Beastie Boys' first 12-inch single for Def Jam, produced by Rick Rubin. It "shamelessly stole" the core riff from ACDC's "Back in Black," leading to its quick removal from record store shelves due to unauthorized sampling. Despite this, it established Rubin's raw, maximalist, and rock-foregrounded production template for the group. "In 1984, Rubin produced the Beastie's first 12-inch single for deaf jam, Rock Hard, a single that shamelessly stole the core riff of multi-platinum metal band ACDC's hit Back and Black and let the Beastie's rap over it."
**She's On It (1985)** : This rap-rock single was featured on the soundtrack for the Def Jam-affiliated hip-hop movie "Krush Groove." It garnered the group MTV exposure during their stint opening for Madonna's Virgin Tour, despite the lukewarm reception from Madonna's fans. "But the tour got the trio promotion and MTV exposure, particularly for their rap rock single She's On It, which appeared on the 1985 soundtrack to the depth jam-affiliated hip-hop movie Crush Crew."
**Hold It Now, Hit It (1986)** : This sample-heavy lead-off single, released six months before their formal debut album, gained airplay on Black Radio in spring 1986 and peaked at number 55 on Billboard's Hot Black singles chart. It featured sound bites from artists like Trouble Funk, Kool & The Gang, Bob James, Curtis Blow, and Doug E. Fresh, showcasing the group's bold and innovative use of sampling. "The sample heavy track, Hold It Now, Hit It, featured sound bites from songs by Trouble Funk, Cool In The Gang, Bob James, Curtis Blow, and Doug E. Fresh."
**She's Crafty (1986 album track)** : This song began with an "unmistakable riff bitten from" Led Zeppelin's "The Ocean" from their "Houses of the Holy" album. It exemplifies the Beastie Boys' brazen approach to sampling and their pioneering efforts in pushing sampling technology, even with often "puerile" lyrics. "Their single She's Crafty led off with an unmistakable riff bitten from Led Zeppelin's crunching houses of the Holy Track, The Ocean."

## curiosities
**Hardcore Punk Origins (1981)** : The Beastie Boys began as a hardcore punk trio in 1981, with Michael Diamond, John Berry, and Kate Schellenbach, later joined by Adam Yauch. In 1982, they played support gigs for punk luminaries like Bad Brains and The Dead Kennedies at iconic New York clubs such as Maxis Kansas City and CBGBs. "The Beastie Boys formed as a hardcore punk trio in 1981."
**Transition to Rap** : Following the underground success of their "Cookie Puss" EP in early 1983, the Beastie Boys decided to abandon hardcore punk and fully commit to rap. This led to drummer Kate Schellenbach's departure in 1984, solidifying the group as the trio of Mike D, MCA, and Adrock focused on rap. "The cookie-puss EP did well enough that the Beastie Boys decided to ditch hardcore punk and commit full-on to rap."
**Rick Rubin Collaboration and "Hip-Hop Elvis Presleys"** : Rick Rubin, whom the Beastie Boys hired as a DJ, signed them to Def Jam and cultivated a raw, maximalist sound for the group, foregrounding rock guitar. Their punk heritage and race made their rock elements more obvious, leading Rubin and Russell Simmons to regard them as "hip-hop Elvis Presleys." "Fortuitously, one of the DJs they hired for their live gigs was Rick Rubin, the multi-hyphenate NYU student who offered to sign them to his budding deaf jam record label."
**Opening for Madonna's Virgin Tour (1985)** : In 1985, the Beastie Boys gained valuable promotion and MTV exposure by opening dates for Madonna's "Virgin Tour," despite receiving an unwelcoming reaction from her "army of Madonna wannabes" who disliked their "loud antics." This tour proved to be a significant "lucky break" for the emerging trio. "By 1985, the Beasties didn't have an album out yet, but they scored a lucky break when the biggest pop star in America, Madonna, invited the trio to open dates on her virgin tour."
**Sampling Pioneers** : Regardless of their often "puerile lyrics and brazen antics," the Beastie Boys, in collaboration with Rick Rubin, were at the forefront of rap styles and boldly pushed sampling technology into new, often audacious, directions. Their records incorporated diverse sound bites and riffs, influencing the evolution of sampling. "However, poor aisle their lyrics and craving their antics, their records were on trend with current rap styles, and they and Rick Rubin pushed sampling technology in bold new directions."

It highlighted the band's long-standing success in funk music before their incidental contribution to rap's origins. "They'd been scoring funk hits since 1973, including their eponymous instrumental fatbacken, which was beloved by early hip hop DJs for its catchy, loopable drum breaks."

## curiosities
**Accidental Rap Progenitors** : The Fatback Band, a funk group active since 1973, released "King Tim III" in 1979, which, despite featuring rap elements, was presented almost as a novelty by a funk band. This track emerged when hip-hop was primarily a live performance medium, making the band an "almost accidental progenitor of rap" by committing an MC to vinyl. "Fatback's King Tim III is an almost accidental progenitor of rap, while it features all the basic hallmarks of rap, including an invitation to clap one's hands, and King Tim spelling his name and making self-aggrandizing boasts."

## songs
**Rapper's Delight (1979)** : Released in the fall of 1979, this single is "unquestionably the first major rap hit," reaching number four on the R&B chart and number 36 on the Hot 100, marking rap's first top 40 hit. It gained fame for popularizing the term "hip hop" in its opening line and was notorious for lifting rhymes from Grandmaster Kaz and its hook from Chic's "Good Times," leading to Bernard Edwards and Nile Rodgers receiving co-writing credits. "It may not be the very first rap single, but it is unquestionably the first major rap hit, reaching number four on the R&B chart and number 36 on the Hot 100, rap's first top 40 hit."
**Apache (1981)** : This 1981 single was a cover of a cover, originating from Cliff Richard and the Shadows' 1960 instrumental, then reinterpreted as a 1973 funk version by The Incredible Bongo Band. The Sugar Hill Gang transformed it into their "much beloved party record," exemplifying hip hop's eclectic and "omnivorous identity." "For example, the Sugar Hill Gang's 1981 single, Apache, was a cover of a cover of a rock song that dated back to 1960."

## curiosities
**"X Factor Style" Formation** : The Sugar Hill Gang was a prefabricated rap collective of three men from Englewood, New Jersey, assembled by soul singer and budding mogul Sylvia Robinson. Signed to her Sugar Hill Records label, the group consisted of Michael "Wonder Mike" Wright, Henry "Big Bank Hank" Jackson, and Guy "Master G" O'Brien. "Assembled X Factor Style by Soul Singer and budding mogul Sylvia Robinson, and signed to her Sugar Hill Records label, The Sugar Hill Gang released rappers to light in the fall of 1979."
**"Hip Hop" Popularization** : The group's seminal hit "Rapper's Delight" is widely recognized for helping to popularize the term "hip hop" through its prominent use in the song's opening line, embedding the phrase into mainstream culture. "It is famous for helping to popularize the term hip hop, right in its opening line."
**Lifting Rhymes and Baselines** : "Rapper's Delight" is infamous for incorporating rhymes from contemporary rapper Grandmaster Kaz and its hook from Chic's 1979 hit "Good Times." Although the baseline was replayed rather than directly sampled, its clear debt to Chic's Bernard Edwards resulted in him and Nile Rodgers being co-credited as songwriters within months of its release. "It's also famous or infamous for lifting its rhymes from a contemporary rapper, Grandmaster Kaz, and its hook from a contemporary hit, 1979 Number One Smash, Good Times."

## curiosities
**"The Message" - Pop Chart Underperformance** : Despite its monumental influence, "The Message" achieved only modest commercial success on pop radio, peaking at number four on Billboard's R&B chart but a surprisingly low number 62 on the Hot 100 in November 1982. This underperformance underscored the significant "glass ceiling" for rap hits on pop charts during the early 1980s. "But for all its influence, the message was a fairly modest hit."
**"White Lines" - Flash's Non-Participation** : Grandmaster Flash received co-credit for the influential 1983 single "White Lines," even though he "did not actually take part" in its creation. This detail highlights the often complex and sometimes disputed credit attribution practices in early hip-hop productions. "This immortal single co-credited to Grandmaster Flash even though he did not actually take part."

# genre - Hip-Hop

## curiosities
**Rap's Golden Era (1988-1994)** : Rap's widely recognized golden era typically commenced around 1988, defined by seminal acts like Public Enemy and N.W.A. This classic period extended through 1994, a pivotal year dominated by artists such as Nas, Tupac, and The Notorious B.I.G., showcasing significant artistic and commercial evolution within the genre. "For example, rap's widely agreed upon golden era usually starts around 1988, marked by such hall of famous as public enemy and NWA. And the classic era continues through 1994, another pivotal year, dominated by the likes of Nas, Tupac, and the notorious BIG."
**Rap's Dominance in 2010s** : In the 2010s, rap emerged as the undisputed leading genre across all recorded music. By 2017, the combined Hip-Hop and R&B genre accounted for a quarter of all music consumed in America, a remarkable achievement for an art form that originated as street music, was once dismissed as a fad, and did not exist as a recorded medium 40 years prior. "Rap in the 2010s is the undisputed top genre in all of recorded music. Just last year, 2017, the Hip-Hop and R&B genre accounted for one quarter of all music consumed in America."
**1986 - Ground Zero for Pop Dominance** : The year 1986 is considered "Ground Zero for rap's dominance of popular music as we know it today," primarily due to Run DMC's "Walk This Way" becoming rap's first top 10 pop hit and rap achieving platinum status for the first time. This year marked a crucial turning point for the genre's commercial trajectory and mainstream acceptance. "If like me, you are a fan of the pop charts, you might call it Ground Zero for rap's dominance of popular music as we know it today."
**Emergence from Black Music Forms** : Hip-hop organically grew out of several forms of 1970s black music, including funk and disco, making it challenging to definitively declare the "first rap single," akin to identifying the first rock and roll song. It emerged from the same "stew" as rock, disco, funk, and R&B, constantly borrowing from and building on contemporary music ideas. "Hip hop grew out of several forms of 1970s black music, including funk and disco."
**Omnivorous Identity** : Hip-hop spent much of its initial decade actively "honing its own omnivorous identity," ravenously incorporating and reinterpreting a wide range of influences. Examples include The Sugar Hill Gang covering an instrumental, Afrika Bambaataa transforming Kraftwerk samples, and Melle Mel interpolating Liquid Liquid, demonstrating its eclectic nature. "Indeed, hip hop spent much of its first decade honing its own omnivorous identity, hungrally devouring a range of influences."
**DJ to MC Shift** : Rap's first wave established the music's potential, with a gradual but significant shift in emphasis from the DJ to the MC. This evolution led to a greater focus on the individual rapper's personality and lyrical ability, setting the stage for groups like Run DMC. "Raps first wave had established the music's potential, and the emphasis had begun to shift from the DJ to the MC."
**Glass Ceiling on Pop Charts (Early 80s)** : By 1983 and 1984, despite rap's pervasive influence on both white and black radio, actual rap singles consistently encountered a "glass ceiling" on the Billboard pop charts. Influential tracks like "The Message" and "White Lines" significantly underperformed on the Hot 100, despite their cultural impact and success on R&B or dance charts. "By 1983 and 1984, this was the chart situation for rap. Its influence was everywhere, on both white and black radio, but the actual rap singles would hit a glass ceiling in Billboard."
**Rap as Pop Culture "Music"** : In the early days of hip-hop, especially before the breakthrough of "Walk This Way," there was a prevailing belief that hip-hop and rap were not considered "music" by those unfamiliar with the genre. Rick Rubin aimed to "bridge that gap" by finding a familiar, hip-hop-friendly piece of music to help non-fans understand its musicality. "There was also this conversation about how hip-hop and rap were not music."
