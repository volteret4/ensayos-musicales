## albums
**U2. Three (1979) - Demo EP** : A three-song EP recorded in a Dublin studio in the summer of 1979 under a demo deal with CBS Records. All 1,000 copies sold out in Ireland, leading to further releases and a global contract. "When the EP, called You Too. Three, sold out all 1,000 copies in Ireland, the band followed up with another Irish only single, another day, that won them a global contract with Ireland Records."
**Boy (1980) - Debut Album** : U2's debut album recorded for Island Records, produced by Steve Lillywhite, who encouraged the band toward a more expansive sound. It reached number 63 on the Billboard LP chart and earned early critical acclaim. "The debut album You Too recorded for Ireland in 1980, titled Simply Boy, was pivotal in several ways."
**October (1981) - Follow-up Album** : This album arrived just months after *Boy* had peaked and emphasized the band members' growing spirituality, notably with the lead-off track "Gloria" being described as a "hymn in rock clothing." It did well in the UK and Ireland but performed poorly in America. "The appropriately titled October doubled down on the band members growing spirituality."
**War (1983) - Third LP** : The third album produced with Steve Lillywhite, *War* cemented U2's image as "firey but fire brands" in the 80s. Its tracks addressed the Irish troubles, nuclear proliferation, and the Polish Solidarity Movement. It became U2's first major US album, peaking at number 12 on the Billboard album chart and going gold by summer 1983. "War, the third LP produced with Steve Lillewight affirmed You Too's image in the 80s, not just firey but fire brands."
**The Unforgettable Fire (1984) - Atmospheric Album** : Produced by Brian Eno, this album marked a change in pace for the band, moving toward a more artistic and ambient sound. Despite being named one of the "50 worst albums of all time" by some critics, it became U2's most atmospheric album to date and yielded their first US Top 40 hit. "The result was the aforementioned 1984 LP, The Unforgettable Fire, which would become You Too's most atmospheric album to date."
**The Joshua Tree (1987) - Tribute to America Album** : This album was conceived as U2's tribute to America, filtering influences from American folk, blues, and country music through their atmospheric rock sound. Released in March 1987, it was an instant success, debuting at number seven and rising to number one on the American chart for nine weeks, becoming U2's first platinum album in 1985. "Released in March 1987, the Joshua Tree was a near-instant success."
**Rattle and Hum (1988) - Studio, Live, and Soundtrack Album** : An overstuffed double LP that served as a studio album, a live album, and a movie soundtrack, capturing the group's fascination with rock history and American culture. While the film was a critical and box office failure, the album reached number one on the album chart in November 1988 and stayed there for six weeks, though fan enthusiasm waned by summer 1989. "The project became Rattle and Hum, a film whose soundtrack was divided between live tracks and new material."

## songs
**Street Missions (1978)** : One of the first songs U2 wrote, performed by the band (then called The Hype) during an audition for an Irish Public Television Network RTE program on youth music in March 1978, just before their name change. "performing on the RTE show one of the first songs they wrote, called Street Missions."
**Another Day (1979)** : An Irish-only single that followed their *U2. Three* EP, which secured them a global contract with Island Records. "the band followed up with another Irish only single, another day, that won them a global contract with Ireland Records."
**A Day Without Me (1980)** : The lead single from their debut album *Boy*, on which The Edge first employed a delay effect unit, a guitar echo that defined his playing style and contributed significantly to U2's overall sound. "And on the albums lead single, A Day Without Me, the edge employed a delay effect unit for the first time, a guitar echo that made his chords shine and helped establish not only his playing style, but You Too's entire sound."
**I Will Follow (1980)** : The second single and lead-off track from *Boy*, this "clarion rocker" built on The Edge's hammering chords, Mullen's and Clayton's marching rhythms, and Bono's fervent quasi-religious lyrics, coming to define the band early on. It charted better in America than in England or Ireland, cracking the top 20 on Billboard's then-new Top Tracks chart in spring 1981. "The song's zealous title was, I Will Follow."
**Gloria (1981)** : The lead-off track from the *October* album, essentially a "hymn in rock clothing," with Bono singing the chorus in Latin: "Gloria in Tadomine, Gloria exultate, Gloria in U-Lord, Gloria exult him." This track reflected the band's growing spirituality. "The LP's lead-off track, Gloria, was essentially a hymn in rock clothing."
**Sunday Bloody Sunday (1983)** : The opening track of the *War* album, which directly addressed Ireland's troubles as a metonym for anti-war activism. At a live performance at Red Rocks in June 1983, Bono declared it "not a rebel song" and marched with a white flag to symbolize their nonpartisanship. "The opening track, Sunday Bloody Sunday, directly addressed Ireland's troubles as a metonym for anti-war activism."
**New Year's Day (1983)** : The first single from *War*, released in winter 1983, obliquely alluded to Lech Wałęsa's Polish Solidarity Movement and became U2's first solid global hit, reaching Top 10 in the UK and Number 2 in Ireland. Its striking music video, shot on a snowy hillside in Sweden, made it a major MTV hit, pushing it to Number 2 on the Rock Tracks chart and Number 53 on the Hot 100. "Released as wars first single in the winter of 1983, New Year's Day became You Too's first solid global hit."
**Pride (In the Name of Love) (1984)** : A song Bono wrote about American Civil Rights Hero Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., which became U2's first US Top 40 hit, peaking at number 33. Bono has expressed frustration with its vague lyrics and the lyrical inaccuracy regarding the time of Dr. King's death, which he corrects in concert. Chrissie Hynde of The Pretenders provided guest backing vocals. "A song Bono wrote about American Civil Rights Hero Dr. Martin Luther King Jr."
**Wire (1985)** : A song from *The Unforgettable Fire* album that generated multiple hits at rock radio. "generating multiple hits at rock radio, including Pride, Wire, and a sort of homecoming."
**A Sort of Homecoming (1985)** : A song from *The Unforgettable Fire* album that generated multiple hits at rock radio and peaked on US rock stations just weeks before U2's landmark Live Aid performance. "generating multiple hits at rock radio, including Pride, Wire, and a sort of homecoming."
**Bad (1984)** : A hypnotic deep cut from *The Unforgettable Fire* album, famously performed for 12 minutes at Live Aid on July 13, 1985, due to Bono's improvisational theatrics, including pulling fans from the audience and mashing up other songs. "YouTube began playing their second song of the set, a hypnotic deep cut from the unforgettable fire album called Bad."
**With or Without You (1987)** : The first true American pop smash from *The Joshua Tree*, described as a "power ballad" and "U2 theology turned into boudoir music." It topped the Hot 100 in May 1987, less than a month after *The Joshua Tree* topped the album chart. The Edge used a device called the "infinite guitar" for its chiming sustain. "With or without you was a mood."
**I Still Haven't Found What I'm Looking For (1987)** : The follow-up single to "With or Without You" from *The Joshua Tree*, described as U2's "most theological hit" and essentially a gospel song about the search for enlightenment, which Bono called "an anthem of doubt more than faith." It reached number one on the Hot 100 by August 1987. "At root, it was a gospel song. I still haven't found what I'm looking for, is about the search for enlightenment."
**Where the Streets Have No Name (1987)** : The stirring, thunderous album opener and third single from *The Joshua Tree*, which peaked at number 13 on the Hot 100. Its video famously recreated The Beatles' 1969 rooftop concert stunt in downtown Los Angeles. "A third single from The Joshua Tree, the stirring thunderous album opener where the streets have no name."
**Desire (1988)** : One of U2's highest-charting singles from *Rattle and Hum*, described as a "banger" that "shamelessly emulated the classic shuffling bow-didly beat from early rock and roll." It reached number three on the Hot 100 and was one of the first number ones on Billboard's Modern Rock Tracks chart. "including one of their highest charting bangers, desire, a song that shamelessly emulated the classic shuffling bow-didly beat from early rock and roll."
**When Love Comes to Town (1988)** : A single from *Rattle and Hum* which was a duet with blues legend BB King, reflecting U2's embrace of American R&B influences. "Several of the singles on Ravel and Hum were aping American R&B, including a duet with blues legend BB King called When Love Comes to Town."
**Angel of Harlem (1988)** : The second single from *Rattle and Hum*, described as a "horn inflected, worshipfully hokey homage to Billie Holiday." It reached number 14 on the pop chart and topped Billboard's Album Rock Chart. "And the album's second single, a horn inflected, worshipfully hokey homage to Billie Holiday called Angel of Harlem."
**All I Want Is You (1989)** : The last single from *Rattle and Hum*, a "majestic love song" that only reached number 83 on the Hot 100, signaling a decline in fan enthusiasm for the project by fall 1989. "The majestic love song All I Want Is You only reached number 83 on the Hot 100."
**One (1992)** : A significant song that cracked the top 10 on the Hot 100 in the week ending May 16, 1992, a month after reaching number one on Billboard's Modern Rock Chart. It affirmed U2's successful transition from the earnest 80s to the moody, sardonic 90s. "The week ending May 16th, 1992, when one by you too cracked the top 10 on the Hot 100, a month after reaching number one on Billboard's Modern Rock Chart."

## curiosities
**US Chart Breakthrough in 1984** : U2's US chart breakthrough in 1984 was by an Irish quartet whose name sounded like an American military spy plane, at a time when most Americans were only just getting to know them. "The US chart breakthrough by an Irish quartet whose name sounded like an American military spy plane."
**Evolution of Sound (80s to 90s)** : In the late 80s, U2 made conscious rock cool and romantic, then rebooted their sound for the 1990s to become sleeker, funkier, more synthetic, and ironic. They later pivoted again toward new conscious anthems, solidifying their status as the world's ultimate stadium band. "rebooting their sound for the 1990s to become sleeker, funcier, more synthetic, even more ironic."
**Longevity and Lineup Stability** : Bono, The Edge, Adam Clayton, and Larry Mullen Jr. kept themselves on the charts longer than virtually all of their rock peers, scoring rock radio hits for about four decades and maintaining the same four-member lineup for nearly 50 years, which is highly unusual for a band of their profile. "Bano, Edge, Adam and Larry kept themselves on the charts longer than virtually all of their rock peers."
**Self-Awareness and "Full of Shit"** : Rolling Stone critic Jimmy Gooterman and Owen O'Donnell, in their book *The Worst Rock and Roll Records of All Time*, famously characterized U2 by stating, "if you two weren't full of shit, they wouldn't be as great as they often are," highlighting the band's self-importance and lack of subtlety, especially from frontman Bono. "if you two weren't full of shit, they wouldn't be as great as they often are."
**Stadium Sized Music from the Start** : U2 cultivated a stadium-sized sound and ethos even before they achieved stadium-selling fame, drawing inspiration from the DIY spirit of punk music but delivering uplifting anthems instead of nihilistic screeds. "They made stadium-sized music even before they were selling out stadiums."
**Admiration and Contempt** : U2, much like Billy Joel, elicits both strong admiration and contempt from audiences, often due to their characteristic "bigness"—encompassing grand emotions, sweeping gestures, and a fervent belief in rock and roll's ability to inspire and even transform the world. "Not unlike Billy Joel, you two inspire both admiration and contempt."
**Bono's 1985 Live Aid Quote** : Backstage at Live Aid in 1985, Bono reflected on "Pride," a song inspired by Martin Luther King Jr.'s death, expressing its continued relevance for volatile situations in Ireland and South Africa, and his hope for peaceful change. "Well, the song cried, which was inspired by the untimely death of Martin Luther King. I think still carries today, especially in my own country, you know, which is a volatile situation in Ireland."
**Early Criticism from Bob Geldof** : Bob Geldof, frontman of the Boomtown Rats and organizer of Band-Aid and Live Aid, admitted to initially disliking U2 when they were young punks from Dublin, calling them "posers" and "frankly, dire" for what he perceived as feigned punk stardom. "I thought they were, frankly, dire. Geldoff told the BBC. They would pretend they were punk stars. They absolutely were not, unquote."
**Bono's Line in "Do They Know It's Christmas"** : Bob Geldof's early contempt for Bono may have influenced his decision to give Bono a "notoriously tone-deaf line" in Band-Aid's charity single "Do They Know It's Christmas," contributing to a long history of public criticism aimed at Bono. "May have contributed to Geldoff giving his fellow Irishman the most notoriously tone-deaf line in Band-Aid's Do They Know It's Christmas."
**Bobcat Goldthwait's Parody (1987)** : At the 1987 MTV Video Music Awards, comedian Bobcat Goldthwait parodied Bono by appearing in a leather vest reminiscent of Bono's video attire and performing a mock vocalization, illustrating the ongoing trend of satirizing the U2 frontman. "Bobcat showed up in a leather vest reminiscent of Bono's video garb and did a little vocalizing."
**South Park Portrayal** : Two decades after the MTV VMAs, the TV show *South Park* also famously portrayed Bono, adding to the extensive history of comedic takes on the U2 lead singer. "I won't even go into detail about how TV's South Park portrayed him two decades later."
**Multiple Lives of the Band** : U2's hit-making career is distinctive for its "multiple lives," as the band has undergone significant evolution in its sound while maintaining its original lineup over nearly five decades. "Like a cat, this band has had multiple lives."
**Dublin Formation (1976)** : In 1976, four teenagers in Dublin formed a band, reportedly only able to play a few songs, including Peter Frampton's "Show Me The Way" and the Bay City Rollers' "Bye-Bye Baby." "When four teenagers in Dublin formed a band that year, this was one of the few songs they could play."
**Bono Thanks Peter Frampton** : Decades after U2's formation, Bono met Peter Frampton at a charity event and expressed gratitude to Frampton for helping him discover his singing voice. "Three decades later, when Bono met Peter Frampton at a charity event, he thanked him for helping him discover his voice."
**Larry Mullin Jr.'s Band Notice** : The band was initiated in 1976 by 14-year-old drummer Larry Mullin Jr., who posted a notice on the bulletin board at his Dublin High School, Mount Temple, seeking other musicians. "the band was formed by a 14-year-old drummer named Larry Mullin Jr., who posted a notice on the bulletin board at his Dublin High School Mount Temple, seeking other musicians."
**Early Lineup (Mullin's Kitchen)** : The initial lineup gathered in Larry Mullin Jr.'s kitchen included 16-year-old bassist Adam Clayton, 15-year-old guitarist David Evans (The Edge), who had cobbled together a refurbished guitar, and 16-year-old Paul Hussin (Bono), who arrived without an instrument but full of ideas. "Among those who turned up to play in Mullin's kitchen were a stylish 16-year-old who owned his own bass guitar, named Adam Clayton. Along faced 15-year-old, born David Evans in Wales, but raised in Ireland, who had cobbled together a refurbished guitar. And a 16-year-old, born Paul Hussin, who showed up without a guitar but full of ideas."
**Bono's Leadership** : Almost immediately, Larry Mullin deferred the role of band leader to Paul Hussin (Bono), who as a "loud mouthed teen" in Dublin, had already earned the sarcastic nickname "Bono Vox" from a local hearing-aid shop, which he then shortened to Bono. "Almost immediately, Larry Mullin deferred the role of band leader to Paul Hussin, who, as a loud mouthed teen, roaming around Dublin with a surrealist street gang, had been given the sarcastic nickname Bono Vox, taken from the name of a local hearing-age shop."
**Nicknames Origin** : Paul Hussin acquired the sarcastic nickname "Bono Vox" from a surrealist street gang in Dublin, derived from a local hearing-aid shop, which he later shortened to Bono. David Evans was nicknamed "The Edge" as a pun on his angular-shaped head, and both monikers permanently stuck with the band members. "Hussin shortened his nickname to just Bono. That same gang nicknamed David Evans, the edge, upon on his angular-shaped head. Those irreverent nicknames stuck."
**Feedback and Dick Evans** : The band was initially known as Feedback and comprised five members, including David "The Edge" Evans' older brother, Dick Evans, as a second guitarist. "At first, the band were called Feedback, and they had five members. David the edge Evans brought along his older brother, Dick Evans, as a second guitarist."
**Mount Temple School Talent Show** : During their performance at the Mount Temple School talent show, Feedback reportedly knew only two songs: one by Peter Frampton and the Bay City Rollers' version of "Bye-Bye Baby." Despite being "pretty terrible," they showed a compelling spark on stage. "When Feedback played the Mount Temple School talent show, they only knew the Frampton song and the Bay City Rollers version of Bye-Bye Baby."
**Punk Rock Influence on Proficiency** : The emergence of punk rock in England and Ireland in 1977 led the band to realize that strict technical proficiency was not a prerequisite for musical success. "When punk rock broke out across England and Ireland in 1977, the band realized that technical proficiency was not essential."
**The Hype and Cover Songs** : Feedback subsequently rebranded as The Hype and began incorporating punk covers from bands such as The Stranglers, Buzzcocks, and The Ramones into their repertoire. For approximately a year, they passed off their cover of The Ramones' "Glad to See You" as an original composition. "Soon enough, Feedback changed their name to The hype, adding punk covers to their repertoire, from bands like The Stranglers, Buzzcocks, and The Ramones."
**RTE TV Audition (1978)** : In March 1978, The Hype auditioned for a youth music television program on the Irish Public Television Network RTE, performing "Street Missions," one of their earliest original songs, just before their impending name change. "In March of 1978, here is The hype, who were just about to change their name, performing on the RTE show one of the first songs they wrote, called Street Missions."
**Name Change to U2** : Bassist Adam Clayton consulted with his friend Steve Averall, a graphic artist and founding member of The Radiators from Space, for new band name suggestions. After rejecting options like The Blazers or Flying Tigers, the band settled on U2, the name they "hated the least," finding it vague and open to interpretation. "But the name on Averall's list that they hated the least, because it seemed so vague, so open to interpretation, was You Too."
**Dick Evans' Departure** : The band informed Dick Evans, The Edge's older brother, that their name change to U2 also signified their transition to a four-piece lineup, leading to his departure. "So they terminated The hype, telling the edges brother Dick Evans that as part of their name change, they would now be a four piece band, and the band became You Too."
**Early Irish Phenomenon** : In its initial stages, U2 was primarily establishing itself as a local phenomenon within Ireland. "At first, You Too were shaping up as strictly an Irish phenomenon."
**Manager Paul McGuinness** : U2 secured British manager Paul McGuinness, who then facilitated a demo recording deal with CBS Records. "After they signed with British manager Paul McGinnis, he got them a demo deal with CBS Records."
**Spirituality and Shalom Fellowship** : Raised within various Catholic and Protestant faith traditions, Bono, The Edge, and Larry Mullin, as young adults, joined the Shalom Fellowship, a non-sectarian charismatic Christian group dedicated to Jesus and social justice. Although U2 later distanced themselves from Shalom and were never officially a Christian rock band, many Christian fans globally continue to interpret their music through this spiritual lens. "Bano, the Edge and Mullin joined the Shalom Fellowship, a non-sectarian charismatic Christian group devoted to Jesus and social justice."
**Nonpartisanship in Irish Troubles** : During their June 1983 concert at Red Rocks Ampitheater, Bono famously stated that "Sunday Bloody Sunday" was "not a rebel song," emphasizing U2's nonpartisan stance on the Irish troubles and their broader anti-war message, dramatically marching with a white flag during the song's climax. "On Sunday Bloody Sunday, Bono, who declared it was, quote, not a rebel song, an illusion to You Too's nonpartisanship in the Irish troubles and their antiviral instants, marched out at the song's climax with a white flag."
**Meta Iconography (Red Rocks)** : The live video of "Sunday Bloody Sunday" from the Red Rocks concert became a major MTV hit, solidifying U2's image and establishing their "meta iconography," even for audience members who might not have been fully informed on Irish politics. "the performance was essential to establishing You Too's meta iconography."
**Persuading Brian Eno** : U2 had to convince art-rock pioneer Brian Eno to produce *The Unforgettable Fire*, as he was trying to move away from rock production and found U2's earlier work "rather square." The band agreed to allow him to guide them toward a more artistic and ambient sound. "You Too had to persuade Eno to work with them, as he was trying to transition out of rock production and found You Too's early work rather square."
**Bono's Frustration with "Pride" Lyrics** : Bono has consistently expressed dissatisfaction with the lyrics of "Pride (In the Name of Love)," finding them vague and impressionistic, and noting a factual error regarding the time of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.'s death, which he corrects during live performances. "Pride in the name of Love has long frustrated Bono, he regards his lyrics as vague and impressionistic. It even gets its most specific detail wrong."
**Largest Crowd Ever (Pre-Live Aid)** : Weeks after "A Sort of Homecoming" peaked on US rock stations, U2 performed for their largest audience ever at Live Aid, where, despite not being the headliner, they arguably eclipsed most other acts, with the possible exception of Queen. "You Too played to their largest crowd ever, a show where they were not the headliner, but stole the day from just about everybody, except maybe Queen."
**Live Aid Performance of "Bad"** : During their Live Aid performance of "Bad," Bono spontaneously incorporated lines from Lou Reed's "Satellite of Love," descended into the audience to pull three young women onto the stage (one of whom later credited him with saving her life from the crushing crowd), and upon his return, mashed up snippets of The Rolling Stones' "Ruby Tuesday" and "Sympathy for the Devil," along with Lou Reed's "Walk on the Wild Side." "Bono wound up pulling three young women from the audience. One of them later claimed he saved her life as she was being crushed by the surgeon crowd."
**Live Aid Setlist Change** : The excessively long and spontaneous performance of "Bad" at Live Aid necessitated the removal of "Pride (In the Name of Love)," then their biggest global hit, from U2's scheduled setlist. "The performance of Bad ran so long, YouTube had to drop from the set their biggest global hit to that point, pride in the name of love."
**Live Aid: From Disaster to Legend** : Initially, the band "brewed" over their "messy" Live Aid appearance, believing it had been a failure. However, critics and fans universally lauded it, transforming it into a "career-making moment" that propelled all their albums back onto the UK charts, solidified their presence in the USA, and elevated them to worldwide stardom. "In the immediate aftermath, the band brewed it over their messy live-aid appearance until critics and fans universally praised it."
**Tribute to America (Joshua Tree)** : Following extensive touring in the US from 1981 to 1985, U2 decided their next album, *The Joshua Tree*, would serve as a tribute to America, blending American folk, blues, and country influences with their atmospheric rock sound, recognizing a shared musical heritage with Irish music. "Accordingly, they decided their next LP would be, in essence, their tribute to America."
**Desert Songs and Joshua Tree Plant** : U2 initially considered "Desert Songs" as the title for their album. During a photoshoot in the Mojave Desert with photographer Anton Corbijn, Bono decided to name the album *The Joshua Tree* after a resilient desert plant Corbijn introduced them to. "At one point, YouTube were going to title the album Desert Songs, and they commissioned photographer Anton Corbine to shoot them for the cover in the Mojave Desert in California."
**"Anthem of Doubt"** : Bono characterized "I Still Haven't Found What I'm Looking For" as "an anthem of doubt more than faith," reflecting its lyrical exploration of an unfulfilled spiritual quest. "Bono has described it as quote an anthem of doubt more than faith."
**Gospel Choir Collaboration** : During The Joshua Tree tour, at a concert in New York's Madison Square Garden, U2 performed "I Still Haven't Found What I'm Looking For" alongside an authentic gospel choir, Harlem's New Voices of Freedom. "at one stop on the Joshua Tree tour at New York's Madison Square Garden, YouTube performed the song with an actual gospel choir, Harlem's new voices of freedom."
**Out of Step with 1987 Charts** : In 1987, U2's sound, characterized by "stately meditation on the divine," stood remarkably distinct from the prevailing chart hits by artists like Madonna and George Michael, establishing them as "a genre of one" with their unique blend of ethereal, anthemic, moody, romantic, post-punk, and pure pop elements. "What was remarkable about all this was how out of step YouTube sounded with virtually everything else on the charts at the time."
**Recreating Beatles Rooftop Concert** : As a testament to their newfound megastar status, the music video for "Where the Streets Have No Name" controversially recreated The Beatles' iconic 1969 rooftop concert stunt, performing on the roof of a liquor store in downtown Los Angeles while police and thousands of fans gathered below. "the video for where the streets have no name recreated the Beatles 1969 rooftop concert stunt on the roof of a liquor store in downtown Los Angeles as police and thousands of fans looked on."
**Rock's Conscience** : By 1987, sincerity had become U2's signature brand; having participated in Amnesty International's Conspiracy of Hope tour alongside other socially conscious artists, they were now, along with Bruce Springsteen, regarded as "Rock's conscience." "Along with Bruce Springsteen, they were now seen as Rock's conscience."
**Time Magazine Cover Story (1987)** : U2 appeared on the cover of Time Magazine in the spring of 1987, with the magazine describing the band as being "about spiritual search and conscience and commitment." "They appeared on the cover of Time Magazine in the spring of 87. Time said of the band quote, they are about spiritual search and conscience and commitment."
**Rolling Stone Cover Story** : An earlier cover story in Rolling Stone had heralded U2 as "the band that matters most, maybe even the only band that matters," signifying their perceived importance in the music landscape. "an earlier rolling stone cover story called you to quote, the band that matters most, maybe even the only band that matters."
**Rattle and Hum Film Failure** : The *Rattle and Hum* film, which aimed to capture the group's fascination with rock history, roots music, and American culture, premiered in the fall of 1988 to "very modest box office and mostly scathing reviews," with one Washington Post critic dismissing it as a "hagiographic fanzine on celluloid, stagie and overproduced." "When the film opened in the fall of 88, it was just shy of a disaster, with very modest box office and mostly scathing reviews."
**Rattle and Hum Album Reception** : The *Rattle and Hum* album received a mixed reception; its live recordings were generally considered "plotting," and Bono drew particular criticism for his "showlly self-righteous stage monologues," though the studio material was more positively reviewed. "The live recordings were mostly plotting and Bono came in for particular criticism for his showlly self-righteous stage monologues."
**Fan Dissatisfaction with Rattle and Hum** : Despite *Rattle and Hum* reaching number one on the album chart and holding that position for six weeks, by the summer of 1989, fan enthusiasm for the project seemingly waned, as the album dropped into the lower half of the chart within approximately six months. "by the summer of 89, it seemed even fans had soured on the project, as it dropped to the lower half of the album chart in roughly six months."
**Sensing a Problem (Post-Rattle and Hum)** : Following *Rattle and Hum*, the members of U2 recognized a growing issue: their "saviors of rock image had bloated beyond their wildest conceptions," trapping them in a self-serious persona and leading to perceptions of them as a "messianic self-parity." "At this point, even the members of U2 sensed they had a problem. Their saviors of rock image had bloated beyond their wildest conceptions, yes, including bonos, and their self-seriousness had become a trap."
**Negative Land's "U2" Single** : The band's public image as a "messianic self-parity" was further underscored by Negative Land's early 90s art project single titled "U2," which famously incorporated foul-mouthed outtake footage of American Top 40 host Casey Kasem. "as indicated by negative lands turn of the 90s art project single simply titled U2."
**Extended Hiatus and Reinvention** : After the promotional cycle for *Rattle and Hum* concluded, U2 embarked on an extended hiatus, deciding a radical reinvention was necessary and that they would "take the piss out of U2" themselves, rather than letting others do it. "After the promotional cycle for Rattle and Hum wound down, the band took an extended hiatus. They read the room and decided they'd need to reinvent themselves."
