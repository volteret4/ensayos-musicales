## albums
**Writer (1970) - Debut Album** : Carol King's largely ignored debut album as a recording artist, released in 1970. It failed to chart, despite employing similar soulful singing, warm piano, and reinterpretation of 60s songs for the 70s, which would later make her a legend. "Upon its release in 1970, the writer LP didn't chart even though it uses some of the same approaches as the album that would make King a legend just one year later."
**Tapestry (1971) - Breakthrough Album** : This album featured songs that were both distinctly Carol King's and adaptable to other artists. Its expectations were initially modest due to the poor sales of her previous album, *Writer*, but it benefited from a market opened by Joni Mitchell and James Taylor's successful solo singer-songwriter albums. It went on to spend 15 consecutive weeks at #1 on the Billboard Top LPs chart, a record for the longest run since The Beatles' *Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band* in 1967. "This was the thing about the album, Carol King, would ultimately name tapestry. Its songs were both distinctly Carol King's and adaptable to others."
**Music (1971) - Tapestry Follow-up** : Carol King's immediate follow-up to *Tapestry*, released in the same year. Less remembered today, this album was rumored to have sold a massive 1.3 million copies on its first day. Due to data lag on the Billboard charts, it reached number one just after Christmas on a chart dated January 1st, 1972, concluding the musical year of 1971 with Carol King at number one. "Though less well remembered today, King's other 1971 album was rumored to have sold a positively gargantuan, Taylor Swift like 1.3 million copies in its first day."

## songs

"To Little Eva, Goffin and King's babysitter, for whom they wrote the instructional dance song, The Locomotion."

King's own recording of the track unexpectedly became a hit for her, peaking at number 22 on the Hot 100 in 1962, though it was initially perceived as a fluke in her early career as a performer. "One of those compositions, it might as well reign until September, originally intended as a demo for Bobby V, wound up a hit for King."
**He's a Bad Boy (1963)** : A follow-up single released by Carol King in 1963, which stalled at number 94 on the Hot 100. "A follow-up single, He's a Bad Boy, stalled at number 94 in 1963."
**You Make Me Feel Like A Natural Woman (1967)** : A very mature and self-actualizing hit from 1967, written by Carol King and Jerry Goffin for Aretha Franklin. The song originated from a title given to them by Atlantic Records President Jerry Wexler, around which Goffin and King composed a track that became an Aretha standard and was later covered by King on her *Tapestry* album. "The very mature, self-actualizing 1967 hit You Make Me Feel Like A Natural Woman began as a title. Atlantic Records President Jerry Wexler gave them."
**You've Got a Friend (1971)** : A milestone song for Carol King, written entirely by herself after the dissolution of her partnership with Jerry Goffin. This hopeful and reassuring track was written as a response to James Taylor's "Fire and Rain" and appeared on both King's *Tapestry* album and James Taylor's 1971 LP, becoming Taylor's biggest hit and signature song despite him not writing it. "The hopeful reassuring you've got a friend, was a milestone for Carol King."

It was issued as the designated A-side of a single with "I Feel the Earth Move" and became the bigger hit with DJs. "It's too late. Written with lyricist Tony Stern was a breakup lament with a subtly feminist point of view, chronicling the end of a relationship where the woman is the one moving on."
**I Feel the Earth Move (1971)** : A piano-pounding barn burner that opens Carol King's *Tapestry* album, described by All Music critic Stuart Mason as "the ultimate in hippie chick eroticism." This track was a rather frank ode to sexual gratification and was issued as the B-side of the same single as "It's Too Late." "I feel the Earth move, a piano pounding barn burner that would wind up opening the album, was a rather frank ode to sexual gratification."
**So Far Away (1971)** : A heartfelt hit generated by Carol King's *Tapestry* album, which peaked at number 14 after the album finally relinquished its number one spot in September 1971. This song continued the album's success by providing another charting single. "The heart felt so far away, which peaked at number 14."
**Go Away Little Girl (1962)** : A song written by Carol King and Jerry Goffin in 1962, originally recorded by King as a demo for Bobby Vee, whose version was not a hit. It was later taken to number one by Steve Lawrence in January 1963, and then again by 13-year-old Donnie Osmond in September 1971, making it the first song to top the Hot 100 twice. "Like so many of her future smash hits, Go Away Little Girl was recorded by Carol herself back in 1962."

## curiosities
**Birth Name Change** : Carol King was born Carol Klein in Manhattan, later adopting her more well-known professional name. "King, born Carol Klein in Manhattan, was the melody writer and Goffin, the lyricist, for a string of singles from a range of artists."
**Brill Building Songwriter** : Carol King, alongside her then-husband and lyricist Jerry Goffin, worked as a songwriting team from the famed Brill Building at 1650 Broadway in New York City. They ranked among the building's most successful "two-person hit factories," channeling youthful emotions into sophisticated pop singles for various artists. "The songwriting team and then married couple wrote four and from the Fiend Brill building at 1650 Broadway in New York City."
**Early Solo Career Struggles** : In the early 1960s, Carol King's attempts to establish herself as a front-line recording artist largely fizzled. Her voice was considered an "odd fit" for the Girl Group and Teen Idol era, with a follow-up single "He's a Bad Boy" stalling at #94 after the perceived fluke success of "It Might as Well Rain Until September." "King's voice was an odd fit for the Girl Group and Teen Idol era of the early 60s, and attempts to promote her as a front-line act quickly fizzled."
**Marriage Disintegration and West Coast Move** : By 1967, Carol King's marriage to Jerry Goffin was disintegrating, prompting her to move out west to Los Angeles. This move introduced her to the vibrant Laurel Canyon scene, a hotbed of 60s rock counterculture and a key instigator of the singer-songwriter movement, where she befriended influential artists like Joni Mitchell and James Taylor. "By 1967, as Goffin and King's marriage was disintegrating, King moved out west."
**Aged 29: New Artist, Veteran Songwriter** : At 29 years old, Carol King presented a unique profile as she gained commercial success: she was a relatively new artist as a singer, yet a seasoned veteran as a songwriter with a decade of experience, which equipped her with an innate ability to deliver hits. "At age 29, King was both a relatively new artist as a singer and a veteran as a songwriter, and she knew how to deliver hits."
**Tapestry's Chart Dominance and Records** : Carol King dominated the summer of 1971 with *Tapestry*, which spent a stunning 15 consecutive weeks atop the album chart, the longest any LP had commanded the list since The Beatles' *Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band* in 1967. It also held the record for the longest run on the Billboard album chart for years, with 302 consecutive weeks from April 1971 through January 1977, a record eventually surpassed by Pink Floyd's *The Dark Side of the Moon* in 1980. "Carol King dominated the summer of 1971. Her tapestry album spent a stunning 15 consecutive weeks atop the album chart."
**Best-Selling LP of All Time (Temporarily)** : For a period, Carol King's *Tapestry*, certified at 13 times platinum, was considered the best-selling LP of all time. This record was eventually surpassed in 1978 by the Bee Gees-fronted *Saturday Night Fever* soundtrack. "And for a time tapestry now certified at 13 times platinum was the best-selling LP of all time."

## albums
**Debut Album (1969) - Rustic Sound** : Rod Stewart's first solo album, released in 1969, featured a surprisingly rustic acoustic-plus-electric sound. It incorporated folk and even country elements, a departure from the "rough and tumble rock" he had been known for with his previous groups. "After all of this rough and tumble rock, the sound of Rod Stewart's 1969 debut album was fairly surprising."
**Gasoline Alley (1970) - Chart Breakthrough** : This album, released in 1970, further refined Rod Stewart's rustic sound. It marked his first album to crack the Top 40 on the Billboard album chart, peaking at number 27, though it gave no hint of the massive success he was about to achieve. "It was Stewart's first album to crack the top 40 on the Billboard album chart, where it peaked at number 27."
**Every Picture Tells a Story (1971) - Acclaimed Peak** : Rod Stewart's third solo album, released in May 1971, which simultaneously topped the LP chart along with its single "Maggie May" in the same week. This period is still considered the most acclaimed of his career, with the album routinely making greatest albums of all time lists and often serving as a benchmark against which critics judge his later work. "When Rod released his third solo album, every picture tells a story. In May 1971, executives at his label, Mercury Records, were convinced that the hitbound track would be his cover of American Fokie Tim Hardin's Reason to Believe."
**Never a Dull Moment (1972) - Follow-up Success** : The follow-up album to Rod Stewart's *Every Picture Tells a Story*. This album spawned the single "You Wear It Well," which became a number 13 hit, recreating some of the magic of "Maggie May." "Or recreating the Maggie May magic with his own follow-up solo single, You Wear It Well."

## curiosities
**Early Aspirations as Footballer** : Rod Stewart, born Roderick Davis Stewart, initially harbored a dream of becoming a footballer, possibly for his beloved Arsenal. He was considered a good soccer player during his youth. "Roderick Davis Stewart thought he might want to be a footballer when he grew up. Perhaps for his beloved Arsenal, he really was a good soccer player."
**Unique Voice as Ticket to Fame** : In his teenage years, Rod Stewart realized his unique, raspy husk of a voice was a significant asset. He believed singing would be an easier path to success than football, and this distinctive vocal quality became his trademark and "ticket to fame." "But sometime in his teenage years, young Rod Stewart realized he was only any good at two things, football and singing. And the latter he thought would be easier to break into, especially given the unique sound of his voice."
**Collaborative Years in the 60s** : Throughout the 1960s, Rod Stewart spent his time joining or recording with approximately half a dozen different groups across a range of styles. These included Brian Auger Trinity and the Jeff Beck Group, where he gained experience before his solo breakthrough. "Stewart spent the 60s joining or recording with around a half dozen groups in a range of styles, including the Brian Augertrenity, who in 1966 helped Rod fulfill his dream of recording a cover of his favorite singer, Sam Cook."
**"Proto-Metal" with Jeff Beck Group** : Rod Stewart's work with the Jeff Beck Group, particularly their cover of The Yardbirds' "Shapes of Things," was described as bordering on "Proto Metal." This material represented probably the hardest rocking Rod Stewart ever sang and showcased his vocal power alongside Jeff Beck's guitar playing. "The Jeff Beck group's cover of the yardbirds classic Shapes of Things bordered on Proto Metal."
**Simultaneous Solo and Group Career** : In 1969, Rod Stewart joined the newly reformed Faces as their singer, even as he was concurrently planning his solo debut album for Mercury Records. This dual commitment allowed him to pursue both group and individual artistic paths. "Around the same time, English band The Small Faces lost their vocalist and went looking for a new one. In 1969, Rod joined the Recrecent Faces as their singer, even as he planned his solo debut."
**Critical Benchmark for Later Work** : *Every Picture Tells a Story* remains a critical benchmark for Rod Stewart's career. Critics often compare his later releases to this acclaimed album, frequently finding the new material lacking in comparison to his early 70s output. "In a backhanded compliment, critics often compare whatever Stewart is releasing currently to every picture tells a story and find the new material lacking."
**Genre Versatility and Roguish Persona** : *Every Picture Tells a Story* notably flaunted Rod Stewart's versatility across genres, while the hit "Maggie May" solidified his star power and established his "rogish persona." This early peak in his career earned him decades of goodwill, despite later venturing into disco in 1979 and synth-pop in 1981. "If nothing else, every picture tells a story flaunted Rod Stewart's versatility and Maggie May established his star power and Rogesh persona."

**Hot Buttered Soul (1969) - Landmark Album** : Isaac Hayes' 1969 album on Stax Records, considered a landmark in the development of Progressive Soul and Black Rock.
