## albums
**Hole Oates (1972) - Debut Album** : This was the duo's debut album for Atlantic Records, produced by Arif Mardin. It featured a plush folk-inflicted sound with soulful overtones. All Music's Stephen Thomas Erlewine noted its "haziness," suggesting it reflected Hall and Oates as "two singer songwriters playing their own tunes in tandem." The album failed to chart upon its release. "On Hole Oates, Martin guided Hall and Oates toward a plush folk-inflicted sound with soulful overtones."
**Abandoned Luncheonette (1973)** : This second album was recorded in New York with studio professionals and marked a shift towards a more "collective identity" for Hall and Oates, moving away from their individual folkie sound. It became their first album to appear on Billboard's Top LP's chart, albeit at a modest number 192. After the success of "Sarah Smile" and the re-release of "She's Gone," the album re-entered the LP chart, cracked the top 40, and went gold in October 1976. "On 1973's Abandoned Luncheon at LP, the duo would sound less like a pair of individual folkies and more like Hall and Oates, a collective identity."
**War Babies (1974)** : Hall and Oates' third album, produced by Todd Rundgren, was their "most experimental rock album" and was described as "an act of willful commercial suicide." The duo reportedly made it out of annoyance with Atlantic Records for under-promoting "Abandoned Luncheonette." The album managed to reach number 86 on the LP chart but led to Atlantic dropping the duo due to their "identity crisis." "In essence, War Babies was an act of willful commercial suicide."
**Daryl Hall & John Oats (1975) - The Silver Album** : This self-titled album, recorded for RCA and produced by Christopher Bond, became known as "The Silver Album" due to its metallic cover. The cover photo, taken by David Bowie's stylist Pierre La Roche, featured Hall and Oates in heavy, glamorous makeup, styled as women, with only John's mustache breaking the illusion. While it caused blowback and homophobic insinuations from some narrow-minded rock fans, it was in line with glam rock aesthetics and potentially contributed to its notoriety. The album debuted on the LP chart in Fall 1975 and went gold, rising into the top 20, after "Sarah Smile" became a hit. "In a cover photo taken by David Bowie's stylist Pierre La Roche, Hall and Dotes appear in heavy, glamorous makeup, styled as women."
**Bigger Than Both Of Us (1976)** : Released in the late summer of 1976 and again produced by Chris Bond, this album was an attempt by the duo and their label to emulate the rock and soul sound that had finally brought them breakthrough success. Despite their efforts, choosing a single from this album initially proved difficult. "When bigger than both of us dropped in the late summer of 1976, the duo and the label again had a hard time choosing a singly."
**Beauty on a Backstreet (1977)** : On this 1977 album, producer Chris Bond pushed Hall and Oates towards a stripped-down style reminiscent of contemporary rock titans like The Eagles or Kansas. This direction proved to be a "bad fit" and a failed attempt to steer the duo towards album rock, resulting in singles that did not perform well on the charts. "On their 1977 album Beauty on a Backstreet, producer Chris Bond insisted on recording Hall and Oats in a stripped down style that echoed current rock titans like the Eagles or Kansas."
**Along the Red Ledge (1978)** : Produced by LA-based journeyman David Foster, this 1978 album successfully generated a top 20 hit for Hall and Oates. The album represented a continued exploration of their sound, including disco elements. "Produced by LA-based journeyman David Foster, 1978's Along the Red Ledge did generate a top 20 hit in the mellow It's a Loud."
**X-Static (1979)** : David Foster produced this 1979 album, which saw Hall and Oates delve even deeper into disco influences. Unfortunately, its release coincided with the 1979 disco backlash, causing it to struggle on radio and the charts. "X-Static" became their first album not to go gold since 1974's "War Babies." "Extatic landed in the middle of the 1979 disco backlash."
**Voices (1980)** : This self-produced album became a blockbuster for Hall and Oates, marking a significant turning point in their career. Its sound was inspired by the "unclustered new wavy, fripper-tronics production" of Darryl Hall's 1977 solo album, "Sacred Songs." The album featured strong new material co-written by Hall, Oates, and Sarah Allen. "would inspire the sound of 1980s voices, the self-produced LP that would become Hall and Oats's blockbuster."

## songs
**I Need Your Love (1966)** : This was a single by The Masters, an all-purpose rock and soul group fronted by John Oates. It marked one of Oates's early recordings before he teamed up with Darryl Hall. "This 1966 single, I Need Your Love, is by The Masters, an all-purpose rock and soul group fronted by John Oates."
**Girl I Love You (1967)** : This 1967 single was sung by Darryl Hall and credited to his group, The Temp Tones. Hall later explained the group's name was a tribute to The Temptations, whom they regarded as "the gods" of vocal groups at the time. "This 1967 single is Girl I Love You sung by Darryl Hall...and credited to a group called The Temp Tones."
**She's Gone (1973)** : This became Hall and Oates' first signature song, originating from a collaboration between the two. John Oates started it as a bluesy lament on acoustic guitar after a girlfriend stood him up, and Darryl Hall transformed it into an R&B track on his keyboard while his first marriage was dissolving. The song featured distinctive octave-based harmonies showcasing both men's voices. Released in November 1973, it took months to gain traction, eventually breaking onto the charts in February 1974 and peaking at a modest number 60 on the Hot 100. Tavares covered the song in summer 1974, with producer Dennis Lambert replicating the arrangement, and their version reached number one on Billboard's Hot Soul Singles chart by December 1974. Hall and Oates's original version was reissued in Fall 1976 and peaked at number 7 on the Hot 100. "She's gone. Was a true collaboration...Jon Oates started it on acoustic guitar as a bluesy lament. One night when a girlfriend stood him up. Then, Darryl Hall, whose first marriage was falling apart, transformed it on his keyboard into something closer to R&B."
**Las Vegas Turn Around, The Stewardess Song (1973)** : This song, featured on the "Abandoned Luncheonette" album, was written by Darryl Hall about Sarah Allen, who was his companion and working as a flight attendant at the time. It highlights the early connection between Hall and his future long-term collaborator and muse. "Hall wrote a song on a band in luncheon at called Las Vegas Turn Around, the stewardess song about her."
**It's Uncanny (1974)** : Released as Hall and Oates's final single for Atlantic Records, this track sounded like a fusion of Billy Joel and The Doobie Brothers. It peaked at number 80 on the Hot 100, serving as their parting gift to the label before they moved to RCA Records. "Hall and Dotes' parting gift to the label was a final single, It's Uncanny, that sounded like Billy Joel crossed with the Dubie Brothers."
**Sarah Smile (1976)** : This gentle, pillowy ballad was written by Darryl Hall for his companion, Sarah Allen. Hall initially doubted its hit potential, but a Cleveland DJ named Lynn Tolliver began playing it as an album cut, leading to its widespread popularity. RCA eventually issued it as the third single from "The Silver Album," and it rose to number 4 on the Hot 100 in June 1976, also reaching number 23 on the R&B chart. "Sarah Smile was named for Sarah Allen, who was more than Hall's companion."
**Do What You Want, Be What You Are (1976)** : This slow-burning single was issued from the "Bigger Than Both Of Us" album in an attempt to replicate the success of the revived "She's Gone." However, it only managed to reach number 39 on the charts. "In an effort to emulate the revived She's Gone, they issued the slow-burning Do What You Want, Be What You Are, but it only reached number 39."
**Rich Girl (1977)** : This uptempo and snarky single from "Bigger Than Both Of Us" became Hall and Oates's first number one hit by March 1977. Darryl Hall revealed he originally wrote it about a "rich boy"—an old college boyfriend of Sarah Allen whose father owned a fast-food chain—but changed "rich guy" to "rich girl" because it "didn't sound right." The song was pure Philly Soul, featuring lush strings reminiscent of Kenny Gamble, Leon Huff, and Tom Bell's productions. "Here's the funny story about Rich Girl. Darryl Hall wrote it about a rich boy."
**Why Do Lovers Break Each Other's Hearts (1977)** : A single from the 1977 album "Beauty on a Backstreet," this track reflected the album's failed attempt to push Hall and Oates towards album rock. It peaked at a modest number 78. "Why do lovers break each other's heart, peaked at number 78."
**Don't Change (1977)** : Another single from "Beauty on a Backstreet," this song completely missed the Hot 100, underscoring the commercial struggles of the album's stripped-down rock direction. "don't change, missed the hot 100 entirely."
**It's a Laugh (1978)** : This mellow track from the 1978 album "Along the Red Ledge" became a top 20 hit for the duo, demonstrating their continued ability to connect with audiences despite ongoing stylistic explorations. "It's a Loud" (transcription likely error for "It's a Laugh")
**I Don't Want to Lose You (1978)** : Featured on "Along the Red Ledge," this song saw Hall and Oates lean back towards a more disco-fied version of Philly Soul. It narrowly missed the top 40. "Hall and Oats leaned back toward a more disco-fied version of Philly Soul on I Don't Want to Lose You, which just missed the top 40."
**Who Said the World Was Fair (1979)** : A "club crazy" track from the 1979 album "X-Static," this song showcased the duo's deeper dive into disco, an unfortunate timing given the prevailing disco backlash. "on the club crazy, who said the world was fair? I can't get in line."
**Portable Radio (1979)** : This song from "X-Static" was described as a disco rock hybrid, reflecting the album's attempt to incorporate heavier disco influences. "And the disco rock hybrid portable radio."
**Wait For Me (1980)** : This soulful rocker from the "X-Static" album was instrumental in salvaging the LP and ushering Hall and Oates into the 1980s. It peaked at number 18 on the Hot 100 in January 1980, and its crisp sound hinted at the direction the duo would take in their subsequent self-produced work. "The only thing salvaging the LP and ushering the duo into the 80s was a soulful rocker called Wait For Me...It peaked at number 18 on the Hot 100 in January of 1980."
**I Can't Go For That (No Can Do) (1982)** : In January 1982, this song achieved the remarkable feat of topping both Billboard's Hot 100 pop chart and the Hot Soul Singles chart simultaneously. It was also present on the dance chart and the album rock chart that same week. Characterized by a "cutting edge digital rhythm track," "icy fluttery keyboards," and a "sizzling baseline," it sounded both "frigid and fiery" and "chilled out but club ready." This song marked the culmination of a decade of effort, fulfilling their dream of achieving universal appeal for their "rock and soul" sound. "America had a new number one song built out of a cutting edge digital rhythm track. It sounded both frigid and fiery with icy fluttery keyboards and a sizzling baseline."
**Everytime You Go Away (1980)** : This love ballad, written by Darryl Hall, was one of the strong new tracks featured on their self-produced blockbuster album "Voices." It highlights the quality of material they were producing during this era. "such as Darryl's love ballad every time you go away."
**How Does It Feel To Be Back (1980)** : Penned by John Oates, this mid-tempo rocker was chosen as the lead single from "Voices" to serve as a "statement of purpose" for the duo's new, self-produced sound. It reached number 30 on the Hot 100. "For voices lead single, as a statement of purpose, they chose the John Oats' pen mid-tempo rocker, how does it feel to be back?"
**You've Lost That Lovin' Feelin' (1980)** : This cover of The Righteous Brothers' classic song was Hall and Oates's first single not written by either of them. It quickly became a radio staple and rose to number 12 on the charts in November 1980. "Hall and Oats's take on the righteous brothers, you've lost that love and feeling. Still, a radio staple to this day rose to number 12 in November 1980."

## curiosities
**Early 70s "Full Hippie" look** : Around 1970, Darryl and John adopted a "full hippie" look, growing their hair down to their shoulders, which was reflected in their early work that leaned more towards John Oates's trippy folk and blues experiments rather than Philly soul. "By 1970, they had gone full hippie with long hair down to their shoulders."
**Considered "Hole Oates" as band name** : At one point, the duo considered naming their project "Hole Oates" (spelled without the 'E', unlike John's last name) and even performed a few gigs under that moniker. However, they ultimately decided to use "Hole Oates" for their debut album title instead. "At one point, they considered naming their project, Hole Oates, spelled the normal way, without the E, unlike John's last name."
**Tommy Mottola's First Success** : Tommy Mottola, who signed Hall and Oates in 1971 when he was 21 and barely a manager, considered them his first major success. He was instrumental in securing their deals with Atlantic in 1972 and later RCA in 1975, even being pleased when "War Babies" flopped as it cleared the way for a better deal. "Hall and Dotes were Tommy Matola's first success."
**"Little Gina" and "Gina, the Manager" song** : Hall and Oates affectionately called their manager Tommy Mottola "little Gina" and even wrote a song titled "Gina, the manager" about him, reflecting his significant, if sometimes unconventional, role in their early career. "Hall and Dotes called him little Gina, and they even wrote the song Gina, the manager, about him."
**"The Silver Album" Cover Controversy** : The metallic cover of their 1975 self-titled album featured Hall and Oates in heavy, glamorous makeup, styled as women, photographed by David Bowie's stylist Pierre La Roche. This gender-bending illusion, only broken by John Oates' mustache, led to blowback from "narrow-minded rock fans" who made homophobic insinuations, though it was consistent with the glam rock aesthetic of the era and contributed to its notoriety. "Though the cover led to blowback from certain narrow-minded rock fans who made homophobic insinuations about the duo's sexual identity, it was in keeping with the look of glam rock at the time."
**Darryl Hall's "Silver Album" Cover Joke** : Regarding the "Silver Album" cover where he was styled as a woman, Darryl Hall later joked that he looked like the kind of woman he would want to date. "Daryl in particular makes an especially cumley woman. He later joked that he looked like the kind of woman he would want to date."
**Sarah Allen's Role as Collaborator** : Sarah Allen, Darryl Hall's companion since 1973, transitioned from his muse (inspiring "Las Vegas Turn Around" and "Sarah Smile") to a professional collaborator. After several years of living with Hall and observing their songwriting process, she began contributing lyrical and musical ideas, earning songwriting credits starting with "The Silver Album" and continuing for over 20 years. "To their credit, rather than regarding Alon as an interloper, Hall and Oats brought her in as a credited songwriter. Starting with the silver album, Sarah Allen's name began appearing in the liner notes of their LPs."
**Rolling Stone Cover (1977)** : By April 1977, following the success of "Rich Girl," Darryl Hall and John Oates were featured on the cover of Rolling Stone magazine. This marked a significant moment in their burgeoning mainstream recognition. "By April, Darryl Hall and John Oats were on the cover of Rolling Stone."
**Three Gold Albums Simultaneously (1977)** : In April 1977, at the height of their initial breakthrough, three of Hall and Oates' albums—presumably "Abandoned Luncheonette," "The Silver Album," and "Bigger Than Both Of Us"—were simultaneously riding the LP chart and had all achieved gold status. "Three of their albums were gold, and all three had been riding the LP chart simultaneously."
**Identity Crisis Persistence (late 70s)** : Even after topping the charts with "Rich Girl," the duo's "identity crisis" persisted through the late 1970s. This led to varied stylistic choices across albums like "Beauty on a Backstreet," "Along the Red Ledge," and "X-Static," as they continued to search for their consistent sound. "The duo's identity crisis persisted, even after topping the charts."
**Self-Production Demand (1980)** : In February 1980, Darryl Hall and John Oates had a crucial conversation with their manager, Tommy Mottola, insisting on producing their own music from then on. They believed that if they couldn't define their sound themselves, they shouldn't be making music, a stance Mottola ultimately supported with the statement, "you're absolutely right... And we have nothing to lose." "The next month Darryl and John had a long talk with their manager Tommy Metola insisting that they wanted to produce themselves from here on."
**"Top Act of the 80s So Far" (1980)** : In 1980, Casey Kasem declared Hall and Oates the "top act of the 80s so far" on American Top 40, comparing their chart dominance to Elvis Presley in the 50s, The Beatles in the 60s, and the Bee Gees in the 70s. While later eclipsed by Michael Jackson and Madonna, this statement highlighted their significant early 80s success. "At number one, the top act of the 80s so far. Darryl Hall and John Oates."
**Public Image Tied to a Specific 80s Moment** : Like the Bee Gees, Hall and Oates are largely tied in the public imagination to a specific moment in time—the 1980s. This era is characterized by "obligatory saxophone solos, kitschy early MTV videos, John Oates's peak 80s mustache, and hits that came with synthesized clapping." "Hall and Oates are tied in the public imagination to a specific moment, a time of obligatory saxophone solos, kitschy early MTV videos, John Oates's peak 80s mustache, and hits that came with synthesized clapping."
**Universally Consumed, Held Close by No One Audience** : By blending black and white musical cultural boundaries, Hall and Oates' music was "universally consumed" but perhaps "held close by no one audience." This led to a situation where some listeners felt the need to enjoy their music ironically or categorize them incorrectly. "by stravelling the cultural boundaries of music perceived as white and black, Hall and Oates were universally consumed, but perhaps held close by no one audience."
**Preference for "Darryl Hall and John Oates"** : The duo preferred to be known as "Darryl Hall and John Oates," and often omitted the "and" on many of their album covers, emphasizing their individual identities while collaborating. "By the way, they prefer to be known as Darryl Hall and John Oates. On many of their album covers, they even leave off the and."

## curiosities
**Producer Dream Team Ranking (2015)** : Robert Fripp was ranked by Rolling Stone in 2015 as part of a "producer dream team" with Brian Eno on their list of the 20 greatest duos of all time. "producer dream team Robert Fripp and Brian Eno."
**Collaboration with Darryl Hall (1977)** : The subversive British progressive rocker and King Crimson leader, known as a "thinking person's guitar hero," befriended Darryl Hall in the early 1970s and was impressed by Hall's "supple voice." Fripp made good on a pledge to work with Hall, producing Hall's solo album "Sacred Songs" in 1977. "King Crimson Leader Robert Fritt The subversive British progressive rocker and thinking person's guitar hero...had befriended Darryl Hall in the early 70s...Impressed with Hall's supple voice, Fritt pledged to work with him someday."
**"Pop Trilogy" (1977-1979)** : Robert Fripp recorded a trio of albums between 1977 and 1979 that he considered his "pop trilogy." These albums were accessible by his standards but experimental for the artists involved, confronting various projects. The first was Darryl Hall's solo LP, "Sacred Songs." "Between 1977 and 1979, Robert Fritt recorded a trio of albums that he considered his pop trilogy, accessible by his standards, but experimental for the artists, confronting the projects."
**Influence on Peter Gabriel's Sophomore Album (1978)** : The year after producing Darryl Hall's "Sacred Songs," Fripp brought many of the same musicians to work on Peter Gabriel's self-titled 1978 sophomore album. This album echoed the sound and experimental approach of Hall's solo effort. "The next year, Fritt brought many of the same musicians to work on Peter Gabriel's self-titled 1978 sophomore album, which echoed the sound of Darryl Hall's solo album."
**Solo Debut "Exposure" (1979)** : Robert Fripp's own solo debut outside of King Crimson, titled "Exposure," was released in 1979. He brought in Peter Gabriel and Darryl Hall to sing on this album, with Hall performing lead vocals on several tracks. "And the year after that, Robert Fritt brought in both Peter Gabriel and Darryl Hall to sing on exposure, Fritt's own solo debut outside of King Crimson."
