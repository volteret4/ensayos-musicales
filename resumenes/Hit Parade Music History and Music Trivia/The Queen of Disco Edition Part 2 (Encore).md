## albums
**Live and More (1978) - Double Live Album** : This double album was Summer's most ambitious project to date, capturing her eclectic live show which ranged from disco to jazz to show tunes. The two-record set included three vinyl sides of concert material, with the fourth side reserved for a new studio recording. It became Summer's first number one album the same week "MacArthur Park" topped the Hot 100, and later her first platinum album, spending nearly a year and a half on the album chart. "Live and More wound up spending nearly a year and a half on the album chart, and it became Donna Summers' first platinum album."
**Bad Girls (1979) - Double Album** : Released in May 1979, this was Summer's third consecutive double album and her second in a row to top the album chart. It was her longest studio album and best seller, going double platinum, and was critically acclaimed for its sonic diversity, with rock critics even praising it despite their general dislike for disco. The album contained 15 songs, about half written by Summer herself, ranging from ballads to electro dance tracks. "Bad Girls was not only Summer's longest studio album ever, it was her best seller, going double platinum, and her most acclaimed and sonically diverse work."
**On the Radio (1980) - Greatest Hits Compilation** : This double album, released in January 1980, spanned Donna Summer's entire Casablanca era and featured two new songs: the Barbra Streisand duet "No More Tears" and the album's title track, "On the Radio." The collection topped the chart, marking Summer's record as the only active recording artist to score three straight number one double albums. "Entitled on the radio, the collection spanned Donna's entire Casablanca herb and featured two new songs, the Barbra Streisand duet and the album's title track."
**I'm a Rainbow (1981) - Shelved Album** : This album was the last collaboration between Summer, Moroder, and Bellotte. Geffen Records, unsatisfied with its commercial prospects, decided it was unfit for the marketplace and shelved it, releasing only a couple of tracks from it later. "Geffen, unsatisfied with the album's commercial prospects, decided I'm a rainbow was unfit for the marketplace and shelved the album."
**Donna Summer (1982) - Self-Titled Album** : Produced by legendary producer Quincy Jones, this album was a collaboration aimed at creating a smash hit for Geffen Records in Summer's post-Moroder years. While it didn't achieve the blockbuster success Geffen hoped for, the disc did go gold and produced a top 10 hit, "Love Is in Control." "While the Quincy album was not the smash Geffen was hoping for, the self-titled disc did go gold and produce one solid top 10 hit."
**Another Place and Time (1989)** : This album featured the lead single "I Know It's For Real," which was a savvy amalgamation of the Stock Aitken Waterman Europop style and Summer's vintage disco sound. The album went gold in the UK and generated two more top 20 hits there, demonstrating Summer's continued power and relevance in the late 1980s. "The single reached number seven on the Hot 100. Summer's last US top 10 hit and went gold."

## songs
**Love to Love You Baby (1975)** : This sultry track redefined disco for the era of the extended mix and was Summer's first American release. Its distinctive style led many to initially consider it a "flash in the pan" and indicative of a specific time and movement in music. "Initially, everybody thought that that first song was going to be yet. It was a flash on the pan."
**I Feel Love (1977)** : A seminal single co-produced by Georgio Moroder and Pete Bellotte, this song is credited with helping to invent electronic dance music through its innovative use of synthesizers. The track was a key part of Summer's "Imperial phase," where her projects consistently met with success. "She and her producers, Georgio Maroder, and Pete Balotti even helped invent electronic dance music with the seminal single I Feel Love."
**Last Dance (1978)** : Summer's first hit of 1978, this song became her most iconic single, widely recognized as a quintessential disco record despite its highly unusual structure, which included a ballad for more than a third of its running time. Co-producer Bob Esty modeled its structure after Diana Ross's "Ain't No Mountain High Enough," knowing Summer's vocal capabilities could handle it. The song reached number three on the Hot 100 in summer 1978, was featured in the film "Thank God It's Friday" (in which Summer appeared), and earned its primary writer, Paul Jabara, an Oscar the following year for Summer's dramatic vocal performance. "Last dance was featured in the musical film Thank God It's Friday."
**MacArthur Park (1978)** : Originally recorded by Richard Harris in 1968, this ungainly song was re-envisioned by Georgio Moroder with a propulsive brass arrangement, ghostly synthesizers, screaming guitars, and melodramatic power vocals. Donna Summer's version became a mini-movie, a disco "thriller" that, even in its four-minute radio edit, went through several movements, and became her first ever number one hit, topping the Hot 100 in November 1978. "The craziest thing of all, MacArthur Park became Donna Summers' first ever number one hit, topping the Hot 100 in November 1978."
**Heaven Knows (1979)** : This soaringly catchy song, a mid-section of the "MacArthur Park" suite from the "Live and More" album, was issued as a standalone single. Co-credited to Brooklyn Dreams, whose leader Bruce Sudano would later marry Summer, the song became a number four hit in early 1979. "Heaven Nose, not the way it should be in Heaven Nose, not the way it could be in Doctoral Nose, there's no need to need."
**Hot Stuff (1979)** : This ultimate hybrid single combined the crunch of a rock song with the thump of a disco song, effectively moving onto rock's turf on Summer's terms. Written by Pete Bellotte, Keith Forsey, and Harold Faltermeyer, it won the very first Grammy Award for Best Female Rock Vocal Performance and featured a guitar solo by Jeff "Skunk" Baxter. "Hot Stuff was the ultimate hybrid single, the Crunch of a Rock song, The Thump of a disco song."
**Bad Girls (1979)** : The chugging, chanting title track of Summer's album, co-written with future husband Bruce Sudano, scaled the Hot 100 alongside "Hot Stuff" and became her first number one on the R&B chart. This success made her the top crossover act of the year, enhancing her popularity with Black audiences alongside her existing white pop audience base. "Two weeks after Hot Stuff exited the top slot, Bad Girls replaced it, and also became her first ever number one on the R&B chart."
**Dim All the Lights (1979)** : The album's third single, written by Summer herself, is a multi-movement song that starts as rock balladry with a country twang, transforms into a Latin-flavored dance record, includes an electro-funk breakdown, and even a section for disco dancers to do the bump. Summer originally conceived the song for Rod Stewart, and it features one of the longest sustained notes in pop music history. It reached number two in the fall of 1979, behind the Eagles' "Heartache Tonight," and was also competing with Summer's own duet, "No More Tears." "Dim all the lights is to Donna Summer, what over the hills and far away is to Led Zeppelin."
**No More Tears (Enough Is Enough) (1979)** : This feminist anthem was a duet with Barbra Streisand, who invited Summer to collaborate, ratifying Summer's status as a premiere vocalist on the charts. Released in the fall, the single topped the chart in less than two months, giving Summer her third number one hit of 1979 and famously preventing her own "Dim All the Lights" from reaching the top. "Released in the fall, the single, subtitled Enough is Enough, Top the Chart in less than two months, giving Summer her third number one hit of 1979, and actually preventing her own Dim all the lights from reaching the top."
**On the Radio (1980)** : This title track from her greatest hits compilation was another of Donna's patented blends of torch song and dance jam. The single reached number five on the Hot 100 by March, further cementing her ability to create diverse and successful tracks. "Oh, I'm the lady on the radio."
**The Wanderer (1980)** : The title track of Summer's first Geffen album, this song marked her first full-on attempt at New Wave, showcasing a radical shift to staccato vocals from her previous lush, full-throated singing. It soared into the top 10 in the fall of 1980, ultimately peaking at number 3, and was regarded as a modest triumph for her transition from disco to new wave. "Summer's staccato vocals on the track were a radical shift from the lush full-throated singing she'd employed at her disco high point."
**Cold Love (1981)** : A number 33 hit in early 1981, this song was written by the same team, led by Pete Bellotte, who penned "Hot Stuff," and sounded even more like rock and less like disco. It continued Summer's musical remodeling efforts on "The Wanderer" album. "Cold love, a number 33 hit in early 1981, was written by the same team, led by Balotti, who penned the prior year's hot stuff, and it sounded even more like rock and even less like disco."
**Romeo (1983)** : This synth-pop track was one of only a couple of songs from the shelved "I'm a Rainbow" album to see release. It wound up on the chart-topping "Flashdance" soundtrack, providing a glimpse of the unreleased album's sound. "Only a couple of tracks saw release, including the synth pop Romeo, which wound up on the chart topping Flash Dance soundtrack."
**Love Is in Control (1982)** : This on-trend synth-funk track was the single from Summer's self-titled album produced by Quincy Jones. It became a solid top 10 hit and, in hindsight, echoed the sound Quincy Jones would produce to much greater success months later on Michael Jackson's "Thriller." "The on-trend synth funk track Love is in control."
**She Works Hard for the Money (1983)** : Inspired by Summer's encounter with an exhausted attendant cleaning a ladies' room in Los Angeles, this title track, co-written with Michael Omartian, became her biggest pop hit of the decade and her biggest R&B hit ever, reaching number three pop and number one R&B in the summer of 1983. Its elaborate music video, featuring Summer in Wayfarer sunglasses and a geometric dress, made her the first African-American female to be placed in heavy rotation on MTV. "Inspired by an encounter Summer had with an exhausted attendant, cleaning a Los Angeles ladies room, she works hard for the money was a number three pop, number one R&B smash in the summer of 1983."
**Unconditional Love (1980s)** : A reggae song and minor hit for Summer in the mid-1980s, this track was a duet with Musical Youth. It showcased Summer's continued versatility during a period when musical trends seemed to be moving away from her. "She continued to show her versatility through the middle of the decade, scoring minor hits with the reggae song Unconditional Love, a duet with musical youth."
**There Goes My Baby (1984)** : Summer released a synth-pop cover of The Drifters' classic, which became a number 21 hit in 1984. This cover continued to demonstrate her adaptability to changing pop trends and sounds of the mid-80s, even as major chart success eluded her. "She continued to show her versatility through the middle of the decade, scoring minor hits with the reggae song Unconditional Love, a duet with musical youth, and a synth pop cover of the Drifters There Goes My Baby, the latter a number 21 hit in 1984."
**I Know It's For Real (1989)** : The lead single from Summer's 1989 album "Another Place and Time," this track was a savvy blend of the Stock Aitken Waterman Europop style and Summer's classic disco sound. It reached number seven on the Hot 100, becoming her last US top 10 hit and going gold, while also reaching number three in the UK. "This time, I know it's for real. The lead single from summers 1989 album, Another Placentime, was a savvy amalgamation of the Stock Aitken Waterman Europop style and Summer's vintage disco sound."
**Con Te Partirò (1999)** : A house cover of the Andrea Bocelli hit "Con Te Partirò (I Will Go With You)," this track became one of the five biggest club hits of 1999 and sold a quarter million copies in the US alone. This demonstrated Summer's enduring popularity and relevance on the dance charts decades after her disco heyday. "One of the most successful was her 1999 house cover of the Andrea Bocelli hit Colante Partiro."
**To Paris With Love (2010)** : This song became Summer's last club number one hit, reaching the top of Billboard's club play chart in November 2010. It cemented her record as the only artist to score a number one hit on that chart in five different decades. "Her last club number one to Paris with love reached the top of that list in November 2010."

## curiosities
**Birth Name and Early Career** : Born Ladonna Gaines, she moved to Germany and performed in musicals before adopting the stage name Donna Summer and embarking on her recording career. This European experience helped shape her early artistic development. "We walked through the pre-stardon history of the woman born Ladonna Gaines, who moved to Germany, performed in musicals, and became Donna Summer."
**Imperial Phase (1978-1979)** : During this period, everything Donna Summer touched "turned to platinum," a moment in a music superstar's career where every project is met with success, similar to Elton John in 1975, Prince in 1984, or George Michael in 1988. This phase, coinciding with the peak of disco, significantly expanded her cultural reach within the genre. "We are now entering 1978, and Donna is entering her Imperial phase, where everything she touches turns to platinum."
**Film Acting Debut - Thank God It's Friday (1978)** : Summer made her only film acting appearance in this musical film, alongside young stars Jeff Goldblum and Deborah Winger. The movie, an Los Angeles answer to "Saturday Night Fever," did modest box office business and was panned by critics, but it featured her hit song "Last Dance." "Summers actually appears in the movie. Her only film acting experience alongside young stars Jeff Goldblum and Deborah Winger."
**Oscar Win for Songwriter (1979)** : "Last Dance" earned its primary writer, Paul Jabara, an Oscar the year after its release. The award was ultimately a recognition of Summer's powerful and dramatic vocal performance on the track, which showcased her range from balladry to high notes. "The following year, one an Oscar for its primary writer Paul Jabara."
**First Grammy Award for Best Female Rock Vocal Performance (1979)** : Donna Summer won this inaugural Grammy for her song "Hot Stuff," beating out tracks by Bonnie Raitt, Carly Simon, and Rickie Lee Jones. This award highlighted her successful fusion of rock and disco elements and her snarling vocal delivery, which was compared to Pat Benatar. "Hot Stuff would go on to win the very first Grammy award for best female rock vocal performance, over songs by Bonnie Raite, Carly Simon, and Ricky Lee Jones."
**Blockading the Hot 100 (Summer 1979)** : For the better part of two months in the summer of 1979, Donna Summer dominated the top of the Hot 100 with "Hot Stuff" and "Bad Girls," with the latter replacing the former at number one. This remarkable achievement, counted down by Casey Kasem on American Top 40, saw her holding the number one spot for five out of eight weeks and appearing in the top three for 11 weeks. "For the better part of two months, Donna was blockating the top of the chart."
**Transition to New Wave and Geffen Records (1980)** : After her Imperial Highpoint, Summer sought to diversify her musical palette and became the first signing on David Geffen's new label, Geffen Records. Her first Geffen album, "The Wanderer," was a full-on attempt at New Wave, representing a radical shift from her disco sound. "Long after, Summer became the first signing on Geffen Records, the then new label founded by legendary music in Prasario David Geffen."
**Post-Maroder Years and Producer Collaborations (1982 onwards)** : After the "I'm a Rainbow" album, the Summer-Moroder-Bellotte trio disbanded. Summer then entered a period of working with various new collaborators, including legendary producer Quincy Jones for her self-titled 1982 album. "Summer entered the wilderness of her post-Maroder years working with a string of new collaborators to varying degrees of success."
**MTV Breakthrough (1983)** : Despite MTV's initial scarcity of Black artists, Summer achieved heavy rotation with her 1983 single and video for "She Works Hard for the Money." She became the first African-American female artist to receive heavy rotation, just months after Michael Jackson and Prince had been embraced by the channel, marking a significant cultural moment for the network. "The video was not only played by MTV, Summer became the first African-American female to be placed in a heavy rotation, just months after the channel embraced Michael Jackson and Prince."
**Stock Aitken Waterman Collaboration (1989)** : In the late 1980s, Summer collaborated with the British production team Stock Aitken Waterman (SAW) on her album "Another Place and Time," leading to the hit single "I Know It's For Real." This collaboration helped her return to the pop charts, leveraging SAW's sound that was openly indebted to vintage 70s club music. "This time, I know it's for real. The lead single from summers 1989 album, Another Placentime, was a savvy amalgamation of the Stock Aitken Waterman Europop style and Summer's vintage disco sound."
**Club Chart Dominance (1970s-2010s)** : Summer consistently scored chart-toppers on Billboard's Club Play chart deep into the 1990s and 2000s, becoming one of the most consistent club DJ favorites after Madonna. She holds the record as the only artist to score a number one hit on Billboard's club play chart in five different decades: the 1970s, 80s, 90s, 2000s, and 2010s. "Summer remains the only artist to score a number one hit on Billboard's club play chart in five different decades."
**Gay Community Rumor and Reconciliation (1980s)** : A controversial and persistent rumor in the gay community alleged that Summer made disparaging remarks about gay people at a 1983 concert meet and greet, particularly after she became a born-again Christian at the turn of the 1980s and during the height of the AIDS crisis. Summer spent years attempting to correct the record and express devotion to her gay fanbase, and her revived popularity in dance clubs in the 90s and 2000s indicated the rumor was not a permanent blight on her character. "A controversial and persistent rumor in the gay community alleged that Summer at a meet and greet with fans after a 1983 concert had made disparaging remarks about gay people."
**Rock and Roll Hall of Fame Snub and Posthumous Induction (2000-2013)** : Summer first became eligible for induction into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2000 but was nominated four times during her lifetime (2007-2012) and passed over by voters each time, a decision that became increasingly controversial, especially after Madonna's earlier induction. Insiders regarded her as a pioneer not just in disco but in electronic music and the shape of rock. She was finally inducted posthumously in 2013 on her fifth nomination, accepted by her widow Bruce Sudano and her three daughters. "Summer would not live to see her induction."
**Death (2012)** : Donna Summer died on May 17, 2012, at 63 years of age, from lung cancer, despite being a non-smoker. She privately theorized to friends that she had inhaled toxic fumes after 9/11 while living in New York City. Tributes poured in from the music community, including Quincy Jones, Barbra Streisand, Aretha Franklin, Kylie Minogue, Moby, and Elton John. "On May 17, 2012, the woman born Ladana Adrienne Gaines died at 63 years of age."

"Special thanks to Critic, historian, and R&B and dance music expert Christian John Waikin, whose research and extensive writings on Donna Summer were invaluable to me in putting this episode together."
