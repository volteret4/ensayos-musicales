## curiosities
**Early Stereotypes** : For decades, there were closed-minded complaints that rap "isn't music" or "has nothing to do with singing," often voiced by grumpy, rock-loving figures like David Crosby or Gene Simmons. However, the fact is that from its very origins, rap records have carried a tune, contradicting these purist dismissals. "For decades, we've heard closed-minded complaints that rap, quote, isn't music or has nothing to do with singing."
**Radio Programmers' Skepticism** : In the 1980s, an infusion of melody into hip-hop hits was partly an applied effort to get rap on the radio. Station programmers, especially R&B radio program directors, were often more skeptical about rap than their Top 40 counterparts and needed convincing that rap wasn't just a fad. "This infusion of melody into hip-hop hits was in some ways, applied to get rap on the radio."
**Machismo and Lyricism** : A sociocultural subtext underlying the question of melody in rap was the perceived need for rappers to maintain "hardness" and hence a certain machismo. Rappers emphasizing "lyricism" over pop or R&B crossover consciously maintained a wall between rap and musical cadence, sometimes even vilifying anything seen as "soft," "gay," or "female." "This brings up a sociocultural subtext underlying the question of melody in rap, the need for rappers to maintain hardness and hence a certain machismo."
**Female Pioneers in Melodic Rap** : During hip-hop's first decade, male rappers largely avoided the melodic rap lane due to prevailing purism and machismo, leaving it wide open for women to experiment. Artists like Neneh Cherry and Queen Latifah were able to embrace melody while still sounding "street" and achieve significant commercial success. "All of this purism ultimately meant was that during hip-hop's first decade, male rappers largely avoided the melodic rap lane, leaving it wide open for women to give it a try."
**Post-Drake Era** : In the early 2020s, the "post-Drake era" continued, characterized by hip-hop superstars like Polo G singing entirely through their "rap" songs, sometimes over unexpected instruments like a ukulele. This shows how profoundly blurred the lines between singing and rapping have become, with songs containing no actual rapping still being labeled as rap. "In the early 20s, this so-called post Drake era certainly hasn't ended."

# curiosity

**"Sing-Rapping" Evolution** : The merging of singing and rapping evolved from early experiments by non-hip-hop artists like Joe Bataan and The Fatback Band, who saw it as an extension of call-and-response music, to the self-conscious rapping of The Sugar Hill Gang. It continued through bold integrations by Blondie and Tom Tom Club, and later, the explicit melding by artists like Bone Thugs-n-Harmony and The Fugees, culminating in a blurred genre where rappers often sang unassisted by the 2010s. "When and how did singing and rapping merge into one chart topping genre?"
**Early Rappers' Stance on Singing** : Most of the first wave of rappers in the 1980s actively avoided singing, expressing that they "wouldn't be caught dead singing." This strong resistance contributed to the initial separation of rap from more melodic forms and set the stage for later breakthroughs that defied this norm. "Most of the first wave of rappers in the 80s wouldn't be caught dead singing."
**Hip-Hop Production in R&B** : Along the way to the fusion of singing and rapping, hip-hop production styles found their way into pure R&B music, even when the R&B artists were still singing. This integration of production techniques predated rappers themselves incorporating melody into their bars, showing a gradual sonic shift across genres. "Along the way hip hop production styles found their way into pure R&B, even when the R&B artists were still singing."
**Digital Era Singing Tools in Rap** : Eventually, rappers began slipping melody into their bars, sometimes singing unassisted and sometimes making digital era singing tools part of their rap. This technological integration further blurred the lines between the genres, leading to a new generation of rappers in the 2010s who seamlessly combined singing and rapping. "And eventually rappers were slipping melody into their bars, sometimes singing unassisted. And sometimes making digital era singing tools part of their rap."
**John Caramonica's Influence** : New York Times critic John Caramonica published a 2019 article titled "Rappers Are Singers Now," in which he credited Drake with fundamentally rewriting the rules of what it meant to be a rapper in the 2010s. Caramonica argued that Drake achieved this by weaving singing as rapping, rapping as singing, and both together into "one holistic hole," creating a constant graceful dialogue between the two forms. "New York Times critic John Caramonica went so far as to credit Drake with inventing or at least popularizing a new form. In his 2019 article quote, rappers are singers now."
**Frank Farian's Europop Hybridization (Late 80s)** : Frank Farian's Europop confections, such as Millie Vanilli in the late 1980s, were already fusing tunes and bars from the jump, as early as 1989. This demonstrated that the hybridization of rapping and singing was occurring even before the 21st century and in musical contexts outside of traditional American hip-hop. "Frank Farion's late 80s Europop confection, Millie Vanilli, fused tunes and bars from the jump, way back in 1989, including on the hit they stole from a Baltimore hip-hop crew."
