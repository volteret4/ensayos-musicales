It is part of the "wet sound" template frequently utilized by producers Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, sharing a very close chord progression with songs like "Human" by The Human League, "Tender Love" by Force M.D.'s, and "Can You Stand The Rain" by New Edition. ## curiosities
**Childhood Fandom** : Aisha Harris was a longtime fan of Janet Jackson, growing up in a household filled with Jackson 5 and Michael Jackson music. While she was initially obsessed with Michael, she became a true hardcore Janet fan around age 12 or 13 after seeing the "All for You Tour" as an eighth-grade graduation gift, describing the experience as "amazing." "I'm definitely a longtime fan. I'm 31, so I was actually only a very small taught when Rhythmation is released."
**Establishment of Identity** : The album "Control" was significant for Janet Jackson as it was about her establishing her identity and career independently from her famous brothers and her father. "control was all about her sort of establishing herself away from her brothers, away from her father."
**Influence on Later Generations** : Janet Jackson's music, particularly her dance style and synthesis of R&B and hip hop, continues to feel incredibly relevant and has a "long tail" of inspiration, influencing two to three generations of pop acts including artists like Ciara, JLo, and Normani. The video for Normani's "Motivation" directly echoes Janet's "Pleasure Principle" and "All Right" visuals and choreography. "it's kind of like this just long tail of Janet inspiring, not just one generation, but like now two, three generations that have come after her."
**Admiration for Mötley Crüe** : In an MTV interview around late 1990, Janet Jackson stated her love for Mötley Crüe's song "Dr. Feel Good." This demonstrates her appreciation for hard rock/metal sounds, which aligns with the metal vocal style she adopted for her song "Black Cat." "She was talking about how she really loved the Motley Cruise song, Dr. Feel Good."
**Collaboration with Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis** : Janet Jackson's partnership with producers Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis is highlighted as a brilliant collaboration where "they all brought the best out of each other." Their work together spanned many albums, with each project sounding distinct. "I feel like they all brought the best out of each other."
**90s Sound Development** : In the 90s, producers Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis crafted a more "lush" sound for Janet Jackson's music, distinguishing it from her earlier 80s work. "Jimmy and Terry were creating a more lush sound for her."

# curiosity

**Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis's "Wet Sound" Template** : These brilliant record makers, known for their collaboration with Janet Jackson, developed a distinct "wet sound" or "raindrop" template. They ingeniously reworked this sturdy song template, characterized by a very close (though not identical) base chord progression, to produce five different hit songs: "Tender Love" (Force M.D.'s, 1985), "Human" (The Human League, 1986), "Can You Stand The Rain" (New Edition, 1988), "Come Back to Me" (Janet Jackson, 1989), and "On Bended Knee" (Boyz II Men, 1994). They achieved genuinely different feelings through variations in instrumentation and arrangement. "They were reworking the same template. And I've checked with a couple of music colleges friends and the chord progression is not identical in each song, but it's very very close."
**The Year 1989 in Pop Music** : The year 1989 was a pivotal moment in music, not only for the success of Janet Jackson's "Rhythm Nation" but also arguably as the year Goth music broke onto the US charts, with dark, gloomy, and sharp-edged British rock bands like The Cure, Depeche Mode, The Smiths, and New Order gaining traction and even crossing over to pop radio. "And 1989 was not only the year of rhythm nation, it was arguably the year Goth broke on the US charts."
**Launch of Billboard's Modern Rock Chart (1988)** : The introduction of Billboard's Modern Rock Chart in 1988 played a crucial role in creating a "feedback loop" for British alternative rock of the post-punk era. This new chart helped these bands build an audience and eventually achieve crossover success on mainstream pop charts. "The launch of Billboard's Modern Rock Chart the year before in 1988 provided a feedback loop for British alt rock of the post-punk era."
