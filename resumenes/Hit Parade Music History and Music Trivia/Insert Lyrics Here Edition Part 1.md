# genre - Instrumental

## curiosities
**Decline in Mid-80s** : Following the mid-1980s, instrumental hits became exceedingly rare and ephemeral, likened to "comet sightings." This represented a dramatic shift after decades of consistent chart-topping success across diverse musical styles, largely attributed to the rise of rap music, which redefined instrumentals as "beats." "But after the mid 80s, instrumental hits were like comet sightings, rare, fleeting, fluky."
**Modern Functional Music for Mood Enhancement** : In the current streaming age, instrumental music is increasingly perceived as "functional music for mood enhancement," rather than as traditional chart hits or musical mementos. This category of music is often "not designed for conscious listening" but engineered to facilitate specific cognitive states, contributing to a "functional audio market" that generates an estimated 120 billion streams annually. "As functional music for mood enhancement."
**Definition - Lacks Full Verse and Chorus** : For the purpose of charting and historical analysis, a song is generally classified as an instrumental if it lacks a full verse and chorus, even if it contains a few spoken words or vocalizations. Chart historian Joel Whitburn's books are considered the authoritative source, or "Bible," for these classifications. "Any song without a full verse and chorus is generally categorized as an instrumental."
**Early Rock Era - Respite from Rock and Roll** : In the nascent period of rock and roll in the mid-1950s, instrumental music frequently served as a "respite" from the emerging genre. Pop charts were often topped by orchestral easy listening tracks, and successful early instrumentals typically featured instantly familiar melodies, sometimes originating from non-American cultures. "Instrumental music was, at first, a respite from rock and roll."
**High Water Mark in 1962** : The year 1962 marked a significant "High Watermark in Hot 100 History" for instrumentals, with three songs reaching number one: David Rose's "The Stripper," Mr. Acker Bilk's "Stranger on the Shore," and The Tornados' "Telstar." "The Stripper was one of three instrumental number one hits in 1962, the High Watermark in Hot 100 History."
**Comeback via Movies and TV (Late 60s/Early 70s)** : Instrumentals experienced a "huge comeback" at the close of the 1960s and into the early 1970s, primarily spurred by their prominent use in movies and television shows, leading to numerous film and TV themes achieving chart success. "The instrumental also made a huge comeback at the end of the 60s into the early 70s thanks to movies and TV."
**High Water Mark in Early 70s** : The early 1970s represented a "highlight of the show," or a high watermark for instrumentals, characterized by the phenomenon where "wordless hits from seemingly any genre could come out of left field and climb the charts." This success was often propelled by movie connections or by innovators utilizing new musical technologies. "The highlight of the show was the high watermark of the instrumental."
**Impact of Disco on Instrumentals** : Disco is arguably the genre that most significantly benefited from the surge of instrumental hits in the 1970s, with several pivotal tracks, such as Barry White's "Love's Theme" and Van McCoy's "The Hustle," reaching number one and actively shaping the genre's sound. "The genre that arguably benefited most from the flurry of hit 70s instrumentals was disco."

**Early Instrumental Hit Characteristics** : Early instrumental hits, particularly during the mid-1950s, were often characterized by melodies that felt "instantly familiar." **"Twangy Guitar Sound" Pioneered** : The distinctive "Twangy guitar sound" was pioneered by instrumentalist Bill Justis with his 1957 hit "Raunchy." This particular sound became widely regarded as a rite of passage for young rock fans learning to play guitar and significantly influenced subsequent artists like Duane Eddy. "In 1957, a ranger and instrumentalist Bill Justice helped pioneer the so-called Twangie guitar sound with Ron Shee, another number two bestseller."
**1968 - Good Year for Instrumentals** : The year 1968 was identified as a particularly favorable period for instrumentals, witnessing several prominent wordless hits such as Paul Mauriat's "Love Is Blue," Mason Williams' "Classical Gas," and Hugh Masekela's "Grazing in the Grass" all achieving high chart positions. "In general, 1968 was a good year for instrumentals."

"As in the 1950s, the late 60s was a good time for earworms built out of catchy instrumental hooks."
