**The Hustle (1975) - Disco Arrives** : McCoy had been a songwriter, producer and arranger for over a decade for Gladys Knight, the Shirelles and Aretha Franklin before he wrote "The Hustle" on a lark. It was an instructional record built around an elaborate partner dance taking off in New York nightclubs. McCoy, a fan of classical and ballroom music, admired the dance's old-school steps. It was written and recorded in under an hour at the end of a session with his Soul City Symphony, came six months after Average White Band's No. 1, and spent one week at No. 1 in summer 1975. It opened the door for more openly disco instrumentals. "The Hustle announced once and for all that disco was happening."

# genre - Instrumental

## curiosities
**1985: Three Instrumentals in the Top 20** : In less than five months in the summer and fall of 1985, three instrumentals reached the Hot 100 Top 20: "Axel F," "Love Theme from St. Elmo's Fire" and the No. 1 "Miami Vice Theme." Instrumentals had done well in the early '80s thanks to movies and TV. After this, vocal-free singles never again charted so high in such numbers. "We would never see so many vocal free singles placed so highly on the charts again."
**Decline After the Mid-'80s** : Instrumentals had topped the charts for decades in easy-listening, soul, hard rock and dance styles. After the mid-'80s, instrumental hits became rare and fluky, and the rise of rap turned instrumentals into beats. Today such hits work more like memes, though the rise of the superstar DJ may be reviving the instrumental. "instrumental hits were like comet sightings, rare, fleeting, fluky."
**What Counts as an Instrumental** : Brief words do not disqualify a song, such as an invitation to drink tequila, instructions to do the Hustle or the Harlem Shake, or a stadium chant of "hey!" Any song without a full verse and chorus is generally classed as instrumental. A song with very few words but a real refrain, like "White Horse," is not. Joel Whitburn's Billboard chart books are the reference for classification. "any song without a full verse and chorus is generally categorized as an instrumental"
**Pure vs. De Facto, Like One-Hit Wonders** : Instrumentals can be divided the way one-hit wonders are. Pure one-hit wonders charted on the Hot 100 only once (Soft Cell, Nena, Biz Markie). De facto ones returned only with a low-charting follow-up (Dexys Midnight Runners, a-ha). Likewise, "Take Five" is a pure instrumental, while "Gonna Fly Now" has a few words. "there are many pure instrumental like Dave Brubeck's Take 5, but some instrumental hits do have a few words."
**A Respite from Rock and Roll** : In rock's early days, "pop music" usually meant a big band or orchestra. That explains why orchestral instrumentals topped the charts right after "Rock Around the Clock." The melody did not have to be American, but it had to feel instantly familiar. "Instrumental music was, at first, a respite from rock and roll."
**Rougher Meant Lower** : The rougher a rock instrumental was, the less likely it was to top the charts. "Generally, the rougher the rock instrumental, the less likely it was to top the charts."
**1962: High-Water Mark** : 1962 had three instrumental No. 1s ("The Stripper," "Stranger on the Shore" and "Telstar"), the most in Hot 100 history. Instrumentals of the time spanned every genre and were usually hard to follow up. "the thing about instrumental is they adhered to no one genre."
**Early '70s Peak** : In the early 1970s, wordless hits from almost any genre could come out of nowhere and climb the charts, sometimes from films and sometimes with no Hollywood connection. "Wordless hits from seemingly any genre could come out of left field and climb the charts."
**Where Nerds Could Innovate** : Instrumentals were a space for technical experimentation, as with "Popcorn," which was written to demonstrate the Moog synthesizer. "Instrumentals were where nerds could innovate"
**Dance Floor Not the '80s Venue** : Dance music kept producing occasional instrumental hits after disco, but the dance floor was not the main home for instrumentals in the 1980s. "The dance floor would not be the main venue for instrumentals in the 1980s."

# genre - Functional Music

## curiosities
**120 Billion Streams a Year** : A Billboard report estimates that the "functional audio" market generates around 120 billion streams annually. Billboard defines it as music "not designed for conscious listening." Some of it is generated by algorithm, and some comes from artists who are not household names. Young listeners in the streaming age often treat instrumental music as functional mood enhancement. "not designed for conscious listening, engineered to help people achieve a certain cognitive state."
**Streams Without Chart Hits** : Algorithm-friendly tracks do not generate the radio airplay or single sales that drive the Billboard charts. This is the main difference between today's instrumentals and those of past generations. "But none of these algorithmically friendly tracks are chart hits."
