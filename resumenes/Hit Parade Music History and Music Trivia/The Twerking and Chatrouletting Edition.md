## songs
**We Can't Stop (2013)** : This breakthrough adult pop hit for Miley Cyrus reached number two on Billboard's Hot 100, largely fueled by its visual imagery and viral video play, which helped her reinvent her image from a Disney Channel star. "Video play got her breakthrough adult pop hit. We can't stop all the way to number two on Billboard's Hot 100."
**Wrecking Ball (2013)** : Miley Cyrus's only number one song, an operatic torch-ballad, topped the Billboard Hot 100 for the week ending September 28, 2013, and then returned to number one nearly three months later, for the week ending December 14, 2013. This made it set an all-time Hot 100 record for the longest gap at number one for a song in a single chart run, proving it could be both viral and enduring. The song hurtled from number 22 to number one in a single week upon its initial ascent, largely due to its video. The song was produced by Lucas Gottwald (Dr. Luke). "The week in September 2013 that the operatic torch-balled wrecking ball, Miley Cyrus' only number one song reached the top."
**See You Again (2008)** : As Miley Cyrus, the emerging star first entered the pop top 10 with this song in 2008, which took five months to slowly rise to number 10 on the Hot 100. "As Miley Cyrus, the emerging star first breached the pop top 10 in 2008 with See You Again, a song that took five months to squeak to number 10 on the Hot 100."
**Party in the USA (2009)** : This single debuted at number two on the Hot 100 in August 2009, driven by blockbuster download sales on iTunes. After its video premiered on YouTube a month later, the song, which had initially slipped out of the top 5, climbed back up the chart to reclaim its number 2 peak. It almost became a number one pop hit but was held back by The Black Eyed Peas' "I Gotta Feeling." "In August of 2009, Miley's new single Party in the USA debuted on the Hot 100 all the way up at number 2, fueled by blockbuster download sales on iTunes."

## curiosities
**Disney Channel Stardom** : Miley Cyrus was incubated as a pop star on the Disney Channel, specifically starring in "Hannah Montana," a show where she played a regular schoolgirl who was a global pop sensation by night. The show, which ran for four seasons and was one of the highest-rated Disney Channel programs, mirrored Cyrus's real life as her fictional father, Robby Ray, was played by her real-life dad, Billy Ray Cyrus. "Like Hillary Duff, Miley Cyrus broke her career playing a character on a Disney Channel show. In her case, the titular star of Hannah Montana, a show with pop music baked into its premise."
**Wrecking Ball Video Impact** : The video for "Wrecking Ball" was critical to its chart success, featuring Cyrus in a tank top, panties, and boots, roaming an industrial room with a sledgehammer and riding a giant wrecking ball completely nude. On its first day, September 9, 2013, it amassed 19.3 million views on YouTube and Vivo, surpassing One Direction's previous record by 7 million. In its first week, it generated 36.5 million US views, instantly sending the song to number one. The video's risky content and its director, Terry Richardson, generated significant headlines. "That was largely because of its video, which featured Cyrus in a tank top panties and boots, roaming with a sledgehammer around an industrial room full of masonry and a giant Recking Ball hanging from the ceiling, one she rode in the altogether."
**Question of Chart Legitimacy** : Critics questioned if "Wrecking Ball" truly represented the biggest song in the USA, as its chart-topping success was largely attributed to its racy video rather than just the song's musical appeal, suggesting a "chart-technicality" was exploited. "If Cyrus's only chart-topper reached the penthouse largely due to a racy video, can we actually call it the biggest song in the USA?"
**Career Reinvention via Viral Video** : Miley Cyrus, like her father, utilized viral video and a sense of sexual energy to transform her career and break away from her Disney Channel past. This was a carefully coordinated strategy to launch her as a legitimate pop star while still linked to her Hannah Montana alter ego. "Like her dad, Miley would ultimately use viral video and a frisson of sexual energy to reinvent her career."
**Early Beneficiary of Vivo** : Miley Cyrus was one of the first artists to benefit from the 2009 launch of Vivo, the music video platform that allowed the music business to monetize videos with advertising revenue for the first time, helping her scale the Hot 100 under her real name while still on "Hannah Montana." "One of the first artists to benefit from the 2009 launch of Vivo was Miley Cyrus, who by this point was in her penultimate season on Hannah Montana and successfully scaling the Hot 100, not as Hannah, but under her given name."
**YouTube Views Not Counting (2009)** : In 2009, despite her "Party in the USA" video debuting on YouTube and helping the song climb the charts, none of Miley's YouTube views were actually counted toward the Billboard Hot 100, which at the time only factored in digital download sales and terrestrial radio airplay. "None of Miley's YouTube views actually counted toward the Hot 100 in 2009."

**Sledgehammer (1986)** : This song was Peter Gabriel's first number one song in America, breaking into the Billboard Top 10 in June 1986 and reaching the top spot by late July. The innovative Stephen Johnson-directed video, featuring stop-motion animated figures from Aardman Studios (of Wallace and Gromit fame) swirling around Gabriel, was the most acclaimed of MTV's first decade and reinvented Gabriel's career. It won nine MTV Moonmen at the 1987 Video Music Awards, an all-time VMAs record. To this day, it remains an enduring hit, receiving nearly 20,000 plays a year on US Terrestrial Radio, second only to "In Your Eyes" in his catalog. "But the innovative Sledgehammer video took Gabriel to a whole other level of fame. It broke into the Billboard Top 10 in June of 1986, and by late July was the number one song in America."
**In Your Eyes (1986)** : This smash ballad from Peter Gabriel's catalog receives more plays on US Terrestrial Radio than any of his other songs, including the video-fueled hit "Sledgehammer," which ranks second. "To this day, Sledgehammer receives nearly 20,000 plays a year on US Terrestrial Radio, second only in Gabriel's catalog to his smash ballad in your eyes."

## curiosities

# curiosity

**Billboard Hot 100 Chart Evolution (2013)** : In the late winter of 2013, Billboard made a pivotal change to its Hot 100 chart formulation by adding YouTube data, more than seven years after YouTube's launch and over 30 years after MTV's debut. This marked the first time the flagship US pop chart counted music videos played on YouTube and its affiliated service, Vivo, directly towards a song's chart position, in addition to sales and radio airplay. "five years ago, in the late winter of 2013, Billboard made a pivotal change in how it formulates its charts. They added YouTube data to the Hot 100, more than seven years after the launch of the online video service, and more than 30 years after the launch of MTV."
**MTV's Initial Chart Impact (1981)** : When MTV launched on August 1, 1981, it had no immediate impact on the number one song, Rick Springfield's "Jesse's Girl," which lasted only one week at the top and wasn't even played on the channel's first day. It took about a year and ten more number one hits for MTV to affirm its chart clout, as early videos were often "shoddy-looking clips" and many number one songs in late 1981 and early 1982 didn't have official music videos at all. "MTV had no real impact on Springfield's chart triumph. Indeed, on the first day of MTV programming, the channel didn't play the video for the number one song in America even once."
**MTV to Radio Feedback Loop** : The Human League's "Don't You Want Me" in 1982, propelled by its strong video and MTV airplay, effectively created an "MTV to radio feedback loop" where video exposure on the channel drove requests to radio stations, which then took cues from MTV, contributing to a song's chart dominance. "With Don't You Want Me, the human league essentially created the MTV to radio feedback loop."
**Billboard's Monitoring of MTV (1984)** : By 1984, Billboard and the record industry began closely monitoring MTV, with labels signing exclusivity agreements for videos, and Billboard itself tracking MTV rotations. In October 1984, the Hot 100 was redesigned to include a symbol indicating video availability, signifying that by the mid-80s, the overwhelming majority of hits had videos, and labels considered them essential for promoting priority singles. "In October of that year, Billboard began tracking MTV rotations, and the magazine redesigned its hot 100 to include a symbol by each song indicating the availability of a video."
**Music Videos Driving Sales and Airplay (1980s)** : In the 1980s, music videos had a "second hand knock-on effect" on the charts; they didn't directly count towards chart positions but indirectly drove consumers to record stores and prompted requests to radio stations, which then influenced airplay based on MTV's popularity. "On the charts, music videos had a second hand knock-on effect. They drove consumers into record stores and drove requests to radio stations, which would then take their cues from MTV."
**MTV Video Music Awards (1984)** : MTV held its first Video Music Awards in the fall of 1984, where winners of the "Moonman" statues, including The Cars' "You Might Think" for Video of the Year, were recognized as hits made bigger by their acclaimed music videos. "That fall, MTV held its first video music awards, and the winners of the first Moonman statues were all hits made bigger by music videos, including the first ever video of the year, the cars you might think."
**Video's Role in Enduring Hits** : Contrary to critics who claimed video-driven hits were fleeting, many songs that became popular due to their videos, like Peter Gabriel's "Sledgehammer" and A-Ha's "Take On Me," proved to be enduring, receiving significant radio play years later and becoming sources of YouTube memes. "Ever since the music video was devised, critics and listeners have been accusing it of distracting from the actual music, or claiming that the songs that become hits because of videos are fleeting and flimsy. But there's plenty of evidence to the contrary."
**Visual Connection for Immortal Hits** : MTV's first decade cemented the idea that while a great song is important, a visual connection makes a hit "immortal." This phenomenon had been observed since earlier visual milestones like Elvis's appearance on "The Steve Allen Show" or The Beatles on "Ed Sullivan," with visual aids—whether TV appearances, movie soundtracks, or viral dances—helping establish audio favorites. "A great song is important, but a visual connection makes a hit immortal."
**90s Shift in Music Video Relevance** : In the 1990s, the connection between glossy music videos and chart success began to weaken due to the rise of genres like Alt Rock that performed better on MTV than on Top 40 Radio, and MTV's gradual pivot away from music videos in favor of reality TV shows, notably "The Real World," which premiered in 1992. "By the 90s, the connection between glossy music videos and chart success began to fray, given the rise of genres like Alt Rock that did better on MTV than on Top 40 Radio, and the channel's gradual move away from music videos in favor of reality TV shows like their Juggernaut The Real World, which premiered in 1992."
**Total Request Live (TRL)** : In the late 90s, the launch of MTV's midday after-school show "Total Request Live" (TRL), coinciding with the rise of Teen Pop, re-established a link between video ubiquity and Hot 100 success, despite TRL often only playing short clips of videos by artists like Christina Aguilera and the Backstreet Boys. "In the late 90s, the rise of Teen Pop, coinciding with the launch of the midday MTV after school show Total Request Live, did reconnect the Hot 100 with video ubiquity, even if TRL wouldn't play more than a minute or two of a Christina Aguilera or Backstreet Boys clip before cutting to host Carson Dale."
**Recording Industry's Post-MTV Strategy** : With MTV dedicating fewer hours to music videos, the recording industry in the new millennium sought alternative channels to incubate pop stars, turning to platforms like Kids Television, particularly the Disney Channel, which launched many future stars. "With fewer hours of MTV's broadcast day now devoted to music videos, the recording industry sought other avenues for gestating pop stars, avenues like Kids Television."
**Napster and iPod Impact on Sales** : The launch of Napster at the turn of the millennium caused industry sales to "crater," and with millions of consumers turning to iPod earbuds, the recording business faced a radically changed chart landscape, marking a difficult period for traditional hit-making machinery. "After the launch of Napster at the turn of the millennium, which we talked about in our Great War against the single episode of Hip-Head, industry sales had begun to crater."
**Mid-2000s Music Video Decline** : By the mid-2000s, music videos were in a "critical condition" as MTV had largely ceased playing them outside of brief, late-night slots, and sharing videos online before 2005 was a "tedious and bandwidth hogging effort." "By the mid-2000s, the music video was, if not dead, in fairly critical condition."
**YouTube Founding and Proof of Concept (2005)** : Launched in early 2005 by former PayPal employees, YouTube aimed to simplify video sharing in a broadband-enabled world, initially inspired by the difficulty people faced finding footage of the 2004 "Nipplegate" controversy. A faux music video, "Lazy Sunday," on a December 2005 "Saturday Night Live" episode, became an unwitting proof of concept for YouTube's technology, garnering millions of bootleg views on blogs and early social media, demonstrating that professionally produced, shareable music videos were ideal content for the platform. "YouTube was the brainchild of several former employees of PayPal who wanted to make video sharing easy on the internet in a broadband-enabled world."
**Music Industry's Battle and Partnership with YouTube** : In the latter half of the 2000s, the music industry initially "battled YouTube" over unauthorized music content that generated wealth for the site without industry monetization. This conflict eventually led to a partnership in 2009 with the launch of Vivo, an all-music site utilizing YouTube's technology to cross-post content and generate advertising revenue against music videos for the first time. "In the second half of the Aughts, the music industry would battle YouTube for the piles of unauthorized music content that found its way to the site, which was making the fledgling service rich, eventually leading to their acquisition by Google, but none of which the industry was monetizing."
**First Direct Monetization of Music Videos (2009)** : The 2009 launch of Vivo, in partnership with YouTube, marked arguably the first time the music business directly monetized music videos through advertising revenue. This was a significant shift, as in the 80s heyday of MTV, labels had given away their videos for promotional purposes, a move they later regretted. "By the way, this was arguably the first time the music business had directly monetized music videos period."
**Billboard Hot 100 Formula Expansion (2010s)** : In the 2010s, Billboard began expanding the Hot 100 formula beyond digital downloads and terrestrial radio airplay to include a wider array of data streams. Spotify and other streaming services were added in 2012, though their initial impact was not enormous as Spotify was new to America, with most streaming action still occurring on YouTube. "But in the 2010s, Billboard would begin expanding the formula for the Hot 100, taking into account a wider array of data streams."
**YouTube's Indirect Chart Influence (Pre-2013)** : Before 2013, YouTube activity, despite generating massive views for songs like Justin Bieber's "Baby," Carly Rae Jepsen's "Call Me Maybe," Gotye's "Somebody That I Used to Know," and PSY's "Gangnam Style," did not directly count toward the Hot 100. Instead, this viral attention indirectly boosted songs by improving sales and increasing radio playlist placements, which then fueled their chart ascent. "Again, as of 2012, none of this YouTube activity actually counted directly for the hot 100. It made those songs sell better, and in turn show up on radio playlists, which fueled them up the charts."
**Populist Chart Rules (2013)** : When Billboard officially added YouTube data to the Hot 100 in 2013, the new chart rules were "exceedingly populist." Not only did plays of official label-sanctioned videos on Vivo count, but YouTube fan videos incorporating at least half a minute of the original recording also contributed to chart performance, as seen with Baauer's "Harlem Shake." "Harlem Shake, the song, benefited from Billboard's new chart rules, which were exceedingly populist. Not only were plays of official label sanctioned videos on vivo now chart legal, but YouTube fan videos incorporating at least half a minute of the original recording also counted for the chart."
