## albums
**Please Please Me (1963) - Debut Album Cut in One All-Day Session** : The Beatles recorded their debut album in a single marathon all-day session, "saving nothing for later," knowing their first chance to get out of Liverpool could be their last. Producer George Martin deliberately left "Twist and Shout" for the end of the session because John Lennon was fighting off a winter cold. Critic Rob Sheffield describes the session in his book Dreaming the Beatles. "They bashed out their first album in a mammoth all day session, saving nothing for later, knowing their first chance to get out of Liverpool could be their last."

## songs
**Can't Buy Me Love (1964) - Lead Single of A Hard Day's Night and Top Five Sweep** : The official Capitol follow-up to "I Want to Hold Your Hand," and the lead single for the film A Hard Day's Night, which the Beatles were shooting in England for release later that summer. It was preceded by global retail pre-orders of 2.1 million copies, more than Adele's "Hello" (2015) sold in its opening week. It debuted at No. 27 the same week "Twist and Shout" gave the band the top four. In its second week it jumped from No. 27 to No. 1, a new chart record at a time when Billboard's pre-computerized methodology made No. 1 debuts impossible and reaching the top in under a month or two difficult. It displaced "She Loves You," giving the Beatles their third straight No. 1 with no other artist in between. For the week ending April 4, 1964, it led the first and only single-artist sweep of the Hot 100's top five. "Can't Buy Me Love was preceded by global retail pre-orders of 2.1 million copies."

**Twist and Shout (1964) - No. 2 on Tollie and Two Chart Runs** : Written in 1961 by American songwriters Phil Medley and Bert Berns, and taken to No. 17 on the pop charts in 1962 by the Isley Brothers, a family act from Cincinnati. The Beatles recorded it as the last track of their Please Please Me debut album, with Lennon battling a cold. In England it was never issued as a single, only as a well-loved album cut. It was the last song the band played for the Queen at the Royal Command Performance in November 1963. In mid-March 1964, Vee-Jay released it as a single on a new subsidiary, Tollie, which had never been issued on 45 in England. It debuted on the Hot 100 the week ending March 14, 1964, as the Beatles' seventh simultaneous hit. It rose to No. 3, giving the band the top four, and sat at No. 2 during the top five sweep. After appearing in the 1986 John Hughes film Ferris Bueller's Day Off, it re-charted and peaked at No. 23 in September 1986, this time on Capitol. That made it the only Beatles song with two full US top 40 runs. "You can hear John completely blow out his throat in the last song they cut that night, Twist and Shout."

**She Loves You (1963) - Licensed to Swan for Practically Nothing** : Known for John, Paul and George's three-part harmonies, the "yeah, yeah, yeah" chorus, and the high-pitched "ooh"s that let the band shake their floppy haircuts. Capitol rejected it outright, with executive Dave Dexter declaring the Beatles "Stone Cold Dead in the US marketplace." By late summer 1963, Vee-Jay's finances were so damaged that it could not take the single. EMI's Transglobal team then begged Philadelphia's tiny Swan Records to release it, less than six months before the band reached the US, and practically no money changed hands. It was No. 21 on the Hot 100 the week ending February 1, 1964. It later replaced "I Want to Hold Your Hand" at No. 1 after that song's seven weeks on top, still on Swan, and was No. 3 during the top five sweep. "In Swan's deal to distribute the Beatles, she loves you, practically no money changed hands."

**I Want to Hold Your Hand (1963) - First US No. 1, Sparked by a DC Teenager** : Capitol planned to press only 5,000 copies and release it in late January 1964. In mid-December 1963, Washington DC teenager Marsha Albert saw a televised news report about the Beatles and called local station WWDC to request the band. The station found an import copy and let her introduce the song live on air. The airplay forced Capitol to rush-release the 45 on December 27, 1963, a month early. It debuted on the Hot 100 at No. 45 the week ending January 18, 1964, as the only Beatles song on the chart. It then went to No. 3 and reached No. 1 the week ending February 1, staying on top seven weeks. It was No. 4 during the top five sweep. "The station even let Miss Albert introduce this song Live on the Air."

**Please Please Me (1963) - First UK Smash and First US Release, on Vee-Jay** : John Lennon originally wrote it as a slow, stately Roy Orbison-style song, then sped it up at George Martin's request. It became the group's first major UK hit, topping the New Musical Express and Melody Maker charts and reaching No. 2 on the official Record Retailer chart. It was the first Beatles single released in America, issued by Chicago's Vee-Jay Records in February 1963 after Atlantic and the other US majors passed. The band's name was misspelled with two T's on the label. American radio largely ignored it in 1963. It re-entered the market in 1964 and was No. 68 by the week ending February 1, then No. 5 during the top five sweep. "the band's name was misspelled with two T's in Beatles."

**Love Me Do (1962) - Debut Single with a Borrowed Harmonica Intro** : When Bruce Channel and his sideman Delbert McClinton toured England in 1962, John Lennon learned the harmonica intro of "Hey Baby" from McClinton and adapted it for "Love Me Do." It was recorded with George Martin in September 1962 and reached No. 17 in the UK. Manager Brian Epstein helped by buying extra copies for his record shop. It reached the US Hot 100 the week ending April 11, 1964, more than 18 months after it was recorded, in the better-known version with Ringo Starr on tambourine and session drummer Andy White on drums. "Love Me Do, their first ever single, finally made its American debut more than 18 months after it was first recorded."

**From Me to You (1963) - First Beatles Song on Any Billboard Chart** : Lennon and McCartney wrote it specifically to make teen fans swoon. Anticipation in England was so high that it debuted in the British top 10. In the US it was the second Vee-Jay single. It missed the Hot 100 but became the first Beatles song on any Billboard chart, peaking at No. 116 on "Bubbling Under the Hot 100" in August 1963. Two months earlier, Del Shannon's cover had reached No. 77. Both low placings arguably hurt the band, since the Hot 100 blends sales and airplay. Bob Spitz writes that American disc jockeys ignored it. It finally debuted on the Hot 100 in the first week of March 1964. "American disc jockeys ignored it completely, a silence even more devastating than scorn."

**I Saw Her Standing There (1964) - B-Side That Charted on Its Own** : It was not a new release but the B-side of Capitol's "I Want to Hold Your Hand" 45. It got so much radio airplay that it debuted on the Hot 100 at No. 68 the week ending February 8, 1964, as the fourth Beatles single on the chart. "The Irresistible Song was receiving so much radio airplay, it was now charting on its own."

**My Bonnie (1961) - Backing Tony Sheridan in Hamburg** : A traditional Scottish folk song that the Beatles recorded in 1961 as the backing group for Hamburg-based rock and roll singer Tony Sheridan, the year before they had their own contract. After the Beatles broke in America, MGM pulled the single from its vaults. It debuted on the Hot 100 in mid-February 1964, despite being sung by the German-accented Englishman Sheridan. "At this point, anything with the Beatles name on it, even this ungainly cover sung by the German accented Englishman Sheridan would sell."

**Roll Over Beethoven (1964) - Chuck Berry Cover Charting as a Canadian Import** : The Beatles' cover of Chuck Berry's song, with George Harrison on lead vocal. Capitol of Canada, more optimistic about the band than its American counterpart, had released it the prior November. Imported copies sold in US shops, and it debuted on the Hot 100 as the band's eighth charting track, the same week they held the top three. "Roll Over Beethoven charted as an import."

**Do You Want to Know a Secret / All My Loving / You Can't Do That (1964) - The Lower-Chart Flood** : Sung respectively by George, Paul and John, these were among seven other Beatles songs below the top five during the sweep week of April 4, 1964. That brought the band to twelve singles across the Hot 100. "That's a dozen Beatles singles spread across the hot 100."

**There's a Place (1964) - Part of the 14-Song Record** : It debuted alongside "Love Me Do" the week ending April 11, 1964. At that point the Beatles still held five of the top 10, and the two debuts gave them 14 simultaneous Hot 100 entries. "making a new total of 14."

## curiosities
**Top Five Sweep - Week Ending April 4, 1964** : The Beatles held the entire top five of the Hot 100: "Can't Buy Me Love" (No. 1), "Twist and Shout" (No. 2), "She Loves You" (No. 3), "I Want to Hold Your Hand" (No. 4), and "Please Please Me" (No. 5). Billboard's printed chart showed "Beatles" five times down the right side. The record has stood for 53 years. "They locked down the entire top five, 53 years later, that feat has yet to be repeated."

**Five Hits, Four Labels** : Of the top five, only "Can't Buy Me Love" and "I Want to Hold Your Hand" were on Capitol. "Twist and Shout" was on Tollie, "She Loves You" on Swan, and "Please Please Me" on Vee-Jay. Only 4 of the 14 charting songs the following week were on American Capitol, with the rest on Tollie, Vee-Jay, Swan, and Capitol of Canada. "And those five Beatles hits, they're not on the same label."

**Top Three, Then Top Four** : The week "Roll Over Beethoven" debuted, the Beatles held the top three with "She Loves You," "I Want to Hold Your Hand," and "Please Please Me." A week later "Twist and Shout" rose to No. 3, giving them the top four on four different labels: Swan, Capitol, Tollie, and Vee-Jay. This would not have happened if Capitol had released singles one at a time a year earlier. "One act controlling the top three, then the top four."

**14 Simultaneous Hot 100 Hits - A 40-Year Record** : The week ending April 11, 1964, the Beatles set the record for most songs by one act on the Hot 100, which made possible by many labels flooding the market at once. Streaming has since broken it several times in the 21st century. "a record they would hold for more than 40 years."

**Three Consecutive No. 1s - Still Unbroken** : "I Want to Hold Your Hand," "She Loves You," and "Can't Buy Me Love" followed each other at No. 1. The Beatles were the first act to succeed themselves at No. 1 on a Billboard chart since Elvis Presley in 1956. Usher came close in 2004. "The Beatles were the first act to succeed themselves at number one on a Billboard chart, since Elvis Presley had done it in 1956."

**Decca Audition and Dick Rowe** : Decca's A&R team brought the band to a London studio on New Year's Day 1962, recorded their audition, hesitated for weeks, then turned them down. A&R head Dick Rowe dismissed them with a now-famous line, later parodied in The Rutles. "groups with guitars are on the way out."

**Turned Down by Half a Dozen UK Labels** : Rejections included EMI, the parent of the label that eventually signed them. Some executives barely listened to the demos because London labels would not consider a Liverpool band, and Londoners looked down on them as provincial. UK executives saw them as a rehash of older acts like Cliff Richard and The Shadows. "London-based labels wouldn't bother with a band from Liverpool."

**Signing to Parlophone, June 1962** : The Beatles signed to Parlophone, then a minor EMI subsidiary led by producer George Martin. "made most of its profits from comedy recordings by the likes of Peter Sellers."

**Brian Epstein Buys Up Love Me Do** : Manager Brian Epstein bought extra stock of the debut single for his record shop to push it up the charts. "who bought up extra stock of the single for his record shop and nudged it up the charts."

**Capitol's Year of Refusals** : Although EMI owned Capitol, it rarely gave Capitol orders for fear of antitrust action, and Capitol rejected nearly every British record. EMI's Paul Marshall was turned away again with "She Loves You," and executive Dave Dexter declared the band dead in the US. In November 1963, Brian Epstein visited the US and found no one willing to attend a reception for him. Capitol's West Coast office refused to meet him. EMI managing director Len Wood had to fly to New York and, for the first time in the EMI–Capitol relationship, order Capitol's president to release the next single. Capitol still planned only 5,000 copies and a release a month after Christmas. "command capital's president to issue the Beatles next single like it or not."

**Rejected by Atlantic and the US Majors** : EMI's Transglobal subsidiary offered "Love Me Do" and "Please Please Me" to Jerry Wexler at Atlantic, who declined, and then to Columbia, RCA, and Mercury. Only Vee-Jay accepted. "to lease its records across the United States, to whatever label was willing to distribute them."

**Topping US Charts Before Arriving** : Ed Sullivan booked the band himself after seeing the frenzy in England that fall, and they arrived in New York for his show in February 1964. The common story says Americans caught Beatlemania after President John F. Kennedy's assassination in November 1963. "the Beatles were topping the US charts before they even set foot on US soil."

**Hamburg Roots and the Self-Contained Band** : The Beatles were from Liverpool and were the house band at Hamburg nightclubs. Their story is told in Bob Spitz's The Beatles, Philip Norman's Shout!, Mark Lewisohn's Tune In, and Rob Sheffield's Dreaming the Beatles. "the inventors of the self-contained rock band as we now know it."

**Absorbing Early-60s Pop** : The band had covered or adapted songs from the era's pop, early Motown, and Brill Building material into Merseybeat. "the Beatles were synthesizing all of these influences into something new."

**Royal Command Performance, November 1963** : John Lennon introduced "Twist and Shout" for the Queen with a famous joke. "But the people in the cheaper seats clap your hands."

**McCartney and Starr Still Touring** : At ages 74 and 76, Paul McCartney and Ringo Starr were still touring the US. "Paul McCartney and Ringo Starr are still touring that Promised Land, still playing those hits from 1964."

# label - Vee-Jay Records

## curiosities
**First US Label to Release the Beatles** : The Chicago label was founded by African American couple Vivian Carter and James Bracken, the V and J of its name. It released "Please Please Me" in February 1963 and "From Me to You," and later used every gap in its EMI deal to release more Beatles records. In 1963 it was hurt by the Four Seasons dispute and by a Las Vegas gambling debt run up by its president, which wiped out its operating budget. "The first label to issue a Beatles single in America was a black-owned imprint based on Chicago's south side."

# curiosity

**British Music in the US Before 1964** : US labels saw England as a musical backwater. British artists rarely reached the upper US charts in the 1940s and 50s, and after 1962's two novelty hits Britain had no US No. 1s in 1963. "To the US recording industry, England was a musical backwater."

**Early-60s US Charts** : Hits in 1961–62 included light pop such as "Hey Baby," early Motown such as the Marvelettes' first No. 1, and Brill Building songs by Gerry Goffin and Carole King for artists like Little Eva. Guitar groups were in a lull after the rock boom of the mid-to-late 50s. "Straight up guitar groups were experiencing a bit of a dry spell after the early rock and roll explosion of the mid to late 50s."

**The Rutles Parody of Dick Rowe** : In the 1970s, Eric Idle and Dan Aykroyd parodied the Decca rejection in the satirical TV film The Rutles. "You said that guitar groups were on their way out and would never make any money at all in the 60s."

**Billboard's Hybrid Chart and Streaming** : The Hot 100 combines sales, streams, and airplay, unlike the UK chart, which uses only streams and sales. Because radio usually plays only one or two songs by an artist at once, sweeping the top five remains very difficult. "As long as some form of radio, whether terrestrial or Pandora, is baked into the Hot 100, the circumstances may never exist again for one artist to dominate the entire top 5."

**Fred Bronson on the Top Five Sweep** : In The Billboard Book of Number One Hits, Bronson listed the records set by "Can't Buy Me Love," including biggest leap to No. 1, most consecutive No. 1s, and largest share of the Hot 100. "no one had ever done anything even close to this before, and it is doubtful the conditions will ever exist for anyone to do it again."

**Bubbling Under the Hot 100** : This Billboard sidebar listed songs just missing the main chart, starting at No. 101. "Basically, a short list of songs that just missed the flagship chart."

**American Top 40 Didn't Exist Yet** : Casey Kasem started American Top 40 in 1970. In 1964 the Billboard charts were mainly for people in the record industry. "Back then, the Billboard charts were mostly for folks in the recording industry, not the general public."
