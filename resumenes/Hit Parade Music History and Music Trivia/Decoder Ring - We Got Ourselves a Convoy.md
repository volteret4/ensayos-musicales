## songs
**The Old Home Filler-Up and Keep On a-Truckin' Cafe (1970s) - First Single from the Bread Commercials** : The music from the Old Home Bread TV commercials, released by Bill Fries and Chip Davis as a single. They recorded it at Chip's studio, credited it to the C.W. McCall character, and first released it locally. Within weeks it was getting heavy jukebox play, helped by a guerrilla campaign in which the studio crew fed quarters into bar jukeboxes around Omaha. It sold 30,000 copies in the Midwest, which led MGM Records to release it nationally, and it reached roughly number 13 on the Billboard country chart. That success let the duo record a whole album of truck-driving songs under the C.W. McCall name. "We put that out and within a matter of just weeks it started getting so much jukebox play."
**Convoy (1976) - Number One Trucker Anthem** : A story song about a trucker with the CB handle "Rubber Duck" who gathers more and more trucks over the CB radio into a huge convoy running coast to coast. Along the way they clash with roadblocks, helicopters and the Illinois National Guard. By Tulsa the convoy has 85 trucks, and in Chicago ("shy town") the "bears" bring in armored cars, tanks and jeeps. Bill Fries took the lyrics from the CB lingo he heard while monitoring the channels during the trucker strikes and blockades of the oil crisis. When DJs played it, station switchboards lit up with requests to hear it again. In January 1976 it was number one on the Billboard Hot 100 and also topped the US country chart, and it reached number one in Great Britain, Australia and Canada. Truckers called it a national anthem for truckers and sent the duo their own "trucker poetry." The governors of New Jersey and Iowa reportedly wanted it banned for glorifying lawbreaking, which only increased sales. More than 45 years later it returned to the zeitgeist through Canada's trucker-led "Freedom Convoy" protests against vaccine mandates. It was used in protesters' YouTube videos and social media posts and inspired several modified cover versions. "This was the number one song on the Billboard pop charts, as well as the country charts."
**Convoy - Military-Style Arrangement** : Bill Fries asked Chip Davis to make the song sound military, with French horns, trumpet and a chorus, so that it would be a full "production number" rather than a simple talking-blues track. "I said to Chip, we've got to make this sound really military like we've got to have some horns, French horns, trumpet, and a chorus."
**Around the World with the Rubber Duck - Convoy Sequel** : A sequel to "Convoy" that, oddly, takes the convoy out onto the high seas. "There was a sequel to Convoy called Around the World with the Rubber Duck."

## curiosities
**Born in a Nebraska Ad Agency, Not Nashville (1972)** : C.W. McCall began in 1972 at a Nebraska advertising agency, not in a recording studio. Art director Bill Fries met touring musician Chip Davis while working on a new TV campaign and offered him a job, and Chip moved to Omaha to work as a jingle writer at a recording studio. "I didn't ever set out to be a country music performer."
**Old Home Bread Commercials - A Country Music Soap Opera** : Their first job was the music for TV commercials for Old Home Bread, a little-known brand of hamburger buns, rolls and donuts from Sioux City, Iowa. Bill pitched a folksy series of musical ads with a continuing storyline. C.W., a truck driver, stops at a small cafe in Pisgah, Iowa, and has a romance with Mavis, a gum-chewing waitress. Over 12 commercials the story went from a meet-cute at the diner to a date at the fireman's ball to C.W.'s proposal. Chip wrote the music. Bill wrote the talking-blues lyrics and voiced C.W. McCall. "It evolved around a couple of characters named CW and Mavis."
**Commercials Listed in TV Guide** : The ads ran in only a handful of states, including Minnesota, Iowa and Kansas, but they became a local phenomenon. They were so popular that TV Guide listed when each spot would air. "The ads became so popular that they actually had to put listings in the TV guide as to when the spots were coming on."
**Jukebox Quarter Campaign in Omaha** : To promote the first single, the studio crew took bags of quarters out on Friday nights to bars around Omaha. They played the same song five times in each jukebox and then moved on to the next bar. "I got all the guys from the recording studio, got them a bunch of quarters."
**Songwriters Who Were Not Truckers** : C.W. McCall was a trucker character, but neither songwriter had ever driven a truck. They took their material from the trucker culture of the oil crisis. "Of course, I was not a truck driver."
**Inspiration from the CB Airwaves** : Bill had a CB radio in his Jeep and would call Chip to tell him what he was hearing: truckers using colorful handles such as Big Sassy and Dopey Diesel. One of the duo used the handle "Music Man." "It sounds like a war going on."
**"Pure Entertainment," Not Politics** : The song mocks authority and imagines confrontations with government forces, but Bill Fries insisted "Convoy" was never meant as a political statement for either side. "No, this was pure entertainment."
**Convoy Sparked the CB Radio Craze** : The song set off a nationwide CB boom among the general public, not just truckers, who were drawn in by its lingo. In the year after its release, 400,000 to 500,000 new radios were sold each month, including a C.W. McCall signature model. "Sales of CB radio's skyrocketed, and not just to truckers, but to the general public."
**Six Albums and an Exit** : Six albums were recorded under the C.W. McCall name in the 1970s. As the CB craze faded, Bill Fries tired of it and quit both advertising and music, turning down industry pressure to chase another number one record. "So I'm not interested in that."
**Same Musicians as Mannheim Steamroller** : The C.W. McCall band and Mannheim Steamroller were the same musicians. They wore blue jeans with tailcoats for Mannheim Steamroller sets, then swapped the tailcoats for jean jackets to play as a country band, and they believe the audience never noticed. "We used to wear blue jeans with tails coats for the Manheim Steam Roller Park and then we'd take off the tails coats and put on a blue jean jacket and then we were a country band."
**Never Liked Country Music** : The duo behind the country hit never really liked country music. One of them had once vowed never to live in Nebraska or write country music, and ended up doing both. "There's two things I'll probably never do in my life. And one is live in Nebraska and one is right country music."

# instrument - Citizens Band Radio

## curiosities
**What a CB Is** : Citizens Band radios are walkie-talkie-like devices that let users talk to each other on set radio frequencies. They are small enough to fit in a truck cab, so truckers could talk while driving, and they built a whole culture around them. "Truckers constructed a whole culture around CB radios."
**CB Lingo** : Truckers used handles such as Big Sassy and Dopey Diesel. Los Angeles was "Shakey Town" because of its earthquakes, state troopers were "smokies" because their hats looked like Smokey the Bear's, and the 55 mph speed limit was "double nickels." "They called it double nickels, 55 double nickels."
**Tool of Trucker Resistance** : Truckers used CBs to warn each other about speed traps and to form tightly packed convoys. Trucks inside a convoy were shielded and could drive as fast as they liked, and police could do very little about it. "The drive now took on this sort of political cast."
**Half of US Vehicles Equipped by 1979** : After "Convoy," CB sales boomed. About half of the cars and trucks on US highways were expected to have CB units by 1979. "By 1979, about half of the cars and trucks on the nation's highways will be equipped with CB units."

# curiosity

**1973 Oil Crisis Origins** : In October 1973 war broke out between Israel and a coalition of Arab countries. The US gave military support to Israel, and in retaliation the Arab nations cut oil exports, pledging to reduce production by 5% a month until Israel withdrew from occupied territories. US gas supplies dropped, prices nearly doubled, and drivers waited four or five hours in line, often running out of gas while waiting. "The oil embargo made gas supplies in the US go way down."
**Cars as Living Rooms on Wheels** : Princeton historian Meg Jacobs, author of the oil-crisis book "Panic at the Pump," explains that early-1970s cars were the biggest and least fuel-efficient ever, so the shortage was a fundamental shock to how Americans saw themselves. "Americans living in this world where cars were like living rooms on wheels."
**Rationing and the 55 MPH Limit** : Gas stations closed on Sundays, and purchases were restricted by the last digit of a car's license plate. President Nixon proposed a 55 mph limit on all highways to save fuel, many states adopted it right away, and about a month after the December 1973 blockade Nixon signed it into federal law. Independent truckers, who were paid by speed and now faced higher diesel prices, were hit hardest. "And this pushed them really over the edge."
**J.W. "Riverrat" Edwards' One-Man Blockade (December 4, 1973)** : A fed-up trucker in Pennsylvania parked his rig in the middle of Interstate 80 in protest. Others heard about it on the CB and joined him, and for nearly five hours trucks backed up for 10 miles in each direction. Word spread nationwide: the Ohio Turnpike was blocked for seven miles between Cleveland and Toledo by a thousand trucks, and there were simultaneous shutdowns in New Jersey, Tennessee, Arkansas and Indiana. "It was a spontaneous, one-man demonstration."
**Independent Truckers' Strike (January 31, 1974)** : Independent truckers struck for 11 days, crippling the supply chain. Citrus rotted in Florida and California, livestock didn't reach market, East Coast supermarket shelves emptied, and the American Meat Institute warned that meatpackers would have to shut down. Strikers also blockaded fueling stations. Drivers in at least eight states were injured by snipers or thrown objects, and a truck driver was shot dead in Delaware, the second death tied to the shutdown. The Nixon administration prepared to use military force. "We're going to bring the country to its knees."
**Truckers as Modern Cowboys** : Nixon cast the strikers as violent desperados, but many Americans blamed him and believed the crisis was a hoax staged by oil companies to raise prices. Many saw the truckers as romantic modern cowboys standing up for their rights, and that sentiment opened the door for "Convoy." "Many people viewed the truck drivers as romantic modern cowboy figures."
**1970s Trucker Pop Culture Boom** : The CB craze produced films and TV including "Smokey and the Bandit" (Burt Reynolds as a bootlegging truck driver; the second-highest-grossing film of 1977) and its two sequels, "White Line Fever," "The Dukes of Hazzard," and the 1978 film "Convoy," directed by Sam Peckinpah and based on the song. "Hollywood got in on the craze too, with a ton of CB inspired movies and TV shows."
**Convoy and the Canadian Freedom Convoy** : The trucker-led protests in Canada against vaccine mandates, including the blockade of the Ambassador Bridge, brought the original "Convoy" back through protest videos and modified cover versions. "The original Convoy is also all over protesters' YouTube videos and social media posts."
