## songs
**Old Home Filler-Up an' Keep on a-Truckin' Cafe (Undated, early 1970s)** : This song was the first single released locally, credited to the CW McCall character, and was based on music originally created for Old Home Bread commercials. It sold 30,000 copies in the Midwest, partially due to a marketing tactic where studio employees would repeatedly play it on jukeboxes in Omaha bars, leading MGM Records to give it a national release where it reached number 13 on the Billboard Country Charts. "We put that out and within a matter of just weeks it started getting so much jukebox play and all that and we were by the way."
**Convoy (1976)** : This novelty single became a number one hit on the Billboard Hot 100 in January 1976, also topping the country charts and reaching number one in Great Britain, Australia, and Canada. The song, written by Bill Freese with music by Chip Davis, is a story about a trucker named Rubber Duck who gathers a large convoy of trucks via CB radio to travel across the country, eluding helicopters and the National Guard. While its creation was inspired by the 1973 oil crisis and truckers' resistance to the 55 mph speed limit, Bill Freese maintained it was purely entertainment. "Convoy was a number one hit single in 1976, like number one on the Billboard Hot 100."

## curiosities
**Old Home Bread Commercials Origin** : The CW McCall character originated from a folksy series of 12 musical TV commercials for Old Home Bread, a brand of hamburger buns, rolls, and donuts, aired in a limited number of states like Minnesota, Iowa, and Kansas. The campaign featured an ongoing storyline about a truck driver named CW and a gum-chewing waitress named Mavis in Pisgah, Iowa, whose romance developed from a first meeting at a diner to a marriage proposal. "It evolved around a couple of characters named CW and Mavis."
**Local Commercials Phenomenon** : The Old Home Bread commercials became a significant local phenomenon due to their engaging romance plot, with their popularity growing so much that TV Guide had to list their airing times. This "down home reality" resonated with viewers who identified with the characters. "The ads became so popular that they actually had to put listings in the TV guide as to when the spots were coming on."
**Jukebox Promotional Campaign** : For the local release of "Old Home Filler-Up an' Keep on a-Truckin' Cafe," Bill Freese orchestrated a unique marketing campaign: he gave employees from the recording studio quarters to visit bars in Omaha on Friday nights, plug the jukeboxes, play the song five times, and then move on to the next bar. "I got all the guys from the recording studio, got them a bunch of quarters and we'd all go out on like a Friday night, run around and plug the jukeboxes and all the bars and Omaha."
**Bill Freese's Post-Music Career** : After the widespread success of "Convoy" and recording six albums in the 1970s, Bill Freese, the lyricist and vocalist for CW McCall, grew weary of both the music and advertising industries. He subsequently quit both careers and relocated to Ouray, Colorado, where he served three terms as the town's mayor, remaining there until his death in April 2022 at the age of 93. "So he quit, both advertising and music."
**Political Banning of Convoy** : The governors of New Jersey and Iowa attempted to ban the song "Convoy" because its lyrics advocated breaking the law. However, this prohibition inadvertently boosted the song's sales even further. "Except for the governors of New Jersey and Iowa, one of the song bands because they're breaking the law. But that even made it sell even more."
**Pure Entertainment, Not Political Statement** : Despite the song's themes of truckers confronting government authority, Bill Freese, the lyricist, explicitly stated that "Convoy" was always intended as pure entertainment and not a political statement. "No, this was pure entertainment. I just make that very clear."
**Unexpected Country Music Success** : Both Bill Freese and Chip Davis, the creators of CW McCall, initially expressed no interest in country music. Chip Davis once famously declared he would likely never live in Nebraska or write country music, only to later achieve significant success doing both while residing in Nebraska. "At one point in time, I made a statement that I said there's two things I'll probably never do in my life. And one is live in Nebraska and one is right country music."

# instrument - CB Radio

## curiosities
**Trucker Culture and Specialized Lingo** : Citizen's Band (CB) radios were a staple for truckers, leading to the development of a unique subculture and an extensive, specialized language. This lingo included "handles" (nicknames like "Rubber Duck" or "Big Ben"), 10-codes for communication, specific terms like "Shakey Town" for Los Angeles due to earthquakes, "Smokies" for state troopers resembling cartoon bears, and "double nickels" for the disliked 55 mph speed limit. This coded communication allowed truckers to warn each other and coordinate actions. "Truckers constructed a whole culture around CB radios... Truckers developed an entire CB specific language."
**Coordination of Resistance Against Speed Limits** : Truckers effectively utilized their CB radios to organize resistance against the 55 mph national speed limit. They broadcast warnings about upcoming speed traps and formed "convoys"—tightly packed groups of trucks—to collectively drive faster, with interior trucks shielded from detection. This transformed the CB radio into a tool with significant political implications. "Speaking in this code, truckers used their radios to coordinate resistance to the double nickel speed limit. They would warn each other over the airwaves of upcoming speed traps."
**"Convoy"-Induced CB Radio Craze** : The immense success of CW McCall's song "Convoy" ignited a widespread CB radio craze, causing sales to skyrocket not only among truckers but also within the general public, who were captivated by the song and its unique terminology. By 1979, projections indicated that approximately half of all cars and trucks on national highways would be equipped with CB units. "The initial success of Convoy went way beyond record sales. It kicked off a whole CB radio craze."
**CW McCall Signature CB Model** : In the year following the release of "Convoy," between 400,000 and 500,000 new CB radios were sold each month. Among these was a specific CW McCall signature model, highlighting the extensive reach and commercial impact of the craze. "Four to five hundred thousand new radios were sold each month in the year after Convoy was released, including a CW McCall signature model."
**Decline of the Craze** : Over time, the widespread popularity of the CB radio craze eventually diminished, coinciding with the end of the musical career for the fictional character CW McCall. "In time of course, the CB radio craze came to an end. And so did the musical career of the fictional bread commercial character CW McCall."

# curiosity

**1970s Trucker Pop Culture Boom** : The 1970s experienced a significant surge in pop culture revolving around truckers, a micro-trend exemplified by movies such as "Smokey and The Bandit" (1977), which became the second highest-grossing film of its year and starred Burt Reynolds as a bootlegging truck driver. Other films included "White Line Fever" and the 1978 movie "Convoy," directed by Sam Peckinpah and starring Kris Kristofferson. This cultural phenomenon was notably amplified by CW McCall's hit song "Convoy." "What was the deal with the boom in pop culture about truckers back in the 1970s? They were thinking of movies like the action comedy Smokey and The Bandit..."
**1973 Oil Crisis Commencement** : The oil crisis commenced in October 1973 with the outbreak of war between Israel and a coalition of Arab countries. In retaliation for U.S. military support to Israel, Arab nations drastically reduced their oil exports, pledging a 5% monthly cut until Israeli forces withdrew from occupied territories. "In October 1973, war broke out between Israel and a coalition of Arab countries."
**Gas Shortages and Skyrocketing Prices** : The Arab oil embargo led to a severe reduction in gas supplies across the U.S. and an almost doubling of gas prices. Drivers frequently endured four to five-hour queues for fuel, often running out of gas while waiting. Many Americans perceived this as evidence of the country's imminent collapse. "The oil embargo made gas supplies in the US go way down. And gas prices go way up, almost doubling."
**Shock to American Self-Perception** : The oil crisis fundamentally challenged America's self-perception, particularly concerning the automobile. In the early 1970s, cars were at their largest and least fuel-efficient, viewed as "living rooms on wheels," with little concern about fuel availability. The crisis therefore represented a significant shock to this ingrained national identity. "It's a fundamental shock to our self-perception."
**Nixon Administration's Conservation Efforts** : The Nixon administration responded to the oil crisis by appealing to the public for significant sacrifices, framing gas conservation as a patriotic duty. Measures included closing gas stations on Sundays and implementing license plate-based rationing systems. "The Nixon administration was asking the public to make big sacrifices, saying that conserving gas was patriotic."
**55 MPH National Speed Limit Implementation** : To conserve fuel during the oil crisis, President Nixon proposed, and many states rapidly adopted, a national highway speed limit of 55 miles per hour. This measure provoked anger among millions of American drivers for years, who considered it an infringement upon their perceived rights to cheap fuel and unrestricted driving. "President Nixon proposed lowering the speed limit on all highways to 55 miles per hour to conserve fuel."
**Disproportionate Impact on Truckers from Speed Limit** : Independent truck drivers were particularly hard-hit by the 55 mph speed limit because their income was directly tied to the speed and efficiency of their deliveries. Compounding their difficulties were higher diesel prices and reduced fuel availability, which pushed them "really over the edge." "And it was truck drivers who felt the effect of the speed limit laws the most, especially the independent ones who didn't work for big companies or belong to the union."
**First Trucker Blockade (December 4, 1973)** : On December 4, 1973, J.W. "Riverrat" Edwards initiated a spontaneous, single-person protest by parking his rig in the middle of Interstate 80 in Pennsylvania. Other truckers, informed via CB radio, joined his protest, leading to hundreds of trucks and cars bringing the interstate to a standstill for nearly five hours, with vehicle lines extending up to 10 miles. This event sparked similar blockades across the nation. "On December 4, 1973, J.W. Riverrat Edwards parked his rig in protest in the middle of the interstate."
**Nationwide Trucker Strike (January 31, 1974)** : Beginning January 31, 1974, independent truck drivers nationwide launched an 11-day strike, refusing to transport goods and paralyzing the country's supply chain. This action resulted in widespread issues such as rotting citrus in Florida and California, livestock unable to reach markets, and bare supermarket shelves on the East Coast, pushing the nation to the verge of economic collapse. "On January 31st, 1974, independent truck drivers across the country went on strike... For 11 days, they park their trucks and refuse to transport goods, bringing the nation's supply chain to a standstill."
**Violence During Trucker Strike** : The 1974 trucker strike was marked by violence against drivers who chose not to participate. In at least eight states, truckers were injured by sniper bullets or objects thrown at their vehicles. One truck driver was fatally shot in Delaware, and others feared for their lives, refusing to speak on camera for fear of retaliation. "Drivers in at least eight states were injured either by snipers bullets or objects thrown at trucks. One truck driver was shot to death in Delaware."
**Nixon Administration's Response to Strike** : President Nixon publicly condemned the striking truck drivers as "violent desperados" and indicated the administration's readiness to use military force to ensure the movement of trucks. However, Nixon himself received the majority of public blame, with many believing the oil crisis was an "artificial conspiracy" orchestrated by oil companies to inflate prices. "The president demonized the truck drivers as violent desparados. The Nixon administration made clear today that it is now preparing to use military force if necessary to keep trucks moving."
**Perception of Truckers as Modern Cowboys** : During the oil crisis and strikes, many Americans came to view truck drivers as romantic, modern cowboy figures, bravely asserting their rights. This perception resonated with millions experiencing frustration over gas lines, creating a receptive environment for a hit song about truckers uniting against authority. "Many people viewed the truck drivers as romantic modern cowboy figures, bravely standing up for their own rights."
**CB-Inspired Copycat Songs** : The immense popularity of CW McCall's "Convoy" led to a proliferation of copycat songs about CB radios. Dozens of these tracks were released, with artists like Red Sovine and the duo Merle Haggard and Leona Williams achieving chart success with trucker-themed narratives. "Dozens of copycat songs about CB radios were released."
**Hollywood's CB-Inspired Productions** : Hollywood quickly capitalized on the CB radio craze, producing numerous movies and TV shows inspired by the phenomenon. Notable examples include "The Dukes of Hazzard," the film "Smokey and the Bandit," and "Convoy the movie." "Hollywood got in on the craze too, with a ton of CB inspired movies and TV shows."
**"Convoy" the Movie (1978)** : A 1978 film directed by Sam Peckinpah and starring Kris Kristofferson, this movie was based on the popular hit song "Convoy" by CW McCall, further solidifying the trucker cultural trend. "There was also the movie White Line Fever about an independent trucker who takes on the industry and 1978's Convoy, which was directed by Sam Peckinpah and starred Chris Christofferson."
**Resurgence of "Convoy" Amidst Canadian Protests** : The novelty hit "Convoy" experienced a significant return to the zeitgeist due to recent trucker-led protests in Canada against vaccine mandates. Modified cover versions of the song, as well as the original, were prominently featured in protesters' YouTube videos and social media posts. "Thanks to the recent trucker protests in Canada, Convoy has been back in the zeitgeist lately."
