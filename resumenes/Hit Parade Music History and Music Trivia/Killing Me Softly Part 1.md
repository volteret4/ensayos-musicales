## albums
**First Take (1969)** : Roberta Flack's debut album, recorded when she was 32. It was initially a sleeper, released in June 1969, not cracking Billboard's Top LPs chart until January 1970, and then only briefly. Its trajectory changed dramatically in 1972 when Clint Eastwood used "The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face" from the album in his film "Play Misty for Me," leading to the album's re-entry and eventual ascent. By April 1972, it soared to #22, then reached the Top 10, and finally #1 on the album chart by the week ending April 29, 1972, where it stayed for six weeks. This made Flack the first Black female soloist to have a #1 album, an achievement that had previously eluded artists like Aretha Franklin and Diana Ross. "Fleck recorded First Take when she was 32 years old."
**Chapter 2 (1970)** : Roberta Flack's second album, released in 1970. It became a word-of-mouth hit, lingering on the lower rungs of the album chart for months despite not producing any hit songs. Listeners appreciated Flack's distinctive interpretations of sultry soul. "Listeners appreciated Fleck's unique takes on Sultry's Soul."
**Quiet Fire (1971)** : Roberta Flack's third solo LP, released in late 1971. The album featured another cover of a Carole King song. "dropping a third solo LP in late 1971 called Quiet Fire that had yet another Carol King cover on it."
**Roberta Flack & Donny Hathaway (1972)** : A full duet album with Donny Hathaway, which arrived as Flack's "First Take" album was reaching #1. Within weeks of its release, this duet album rose to #3 on the charts, marking Donny Hathaway's first hit LP and giving Roberta Flack two simultaneous Top 10 LPs. "Her duet album, Roberta Fleck and Donnie Hathaway, arrived just as first take was reaching No. 1."

## songs
**The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face (1969)** : Originally penned by British folk singer Ewan MacColl in 1957, Flack's 1969 version was a deep cut on her debut album "First Take" and was largely ignored for three years. Clint Eastwood heard it on her album and chose it for a romantic montage in his 1971 directorial debut, "Play Misty for Me," paying Flack $2,000 for the rights. Fleck's interpretation slowed the song significantly, from under three minutes to over five on the album, and was delivered with a deliberately languorous pace full of awe. After the film became a modest box office hit, Atlantic re-issued the three-year-old track as a single in March 1972, trimming it to about four and a half minutes. Its smoldering slowness became its selling point, leading it to quickly rise on the Hot 100, reaching #1 and staying there for six weeks by April 29, 1972. It also won the Grammy for Record of the Year in March 1973. "The smoldering slowness was the song's selling point."
**Compared to What (1969)** : This song, a jaunty protest track of social criticism, was first recorded by jazz pianist Les McCann for a 1966 studio album and later became a crossover hit in a live version with saxophonist Eddie Harris. Roberta Flack recorded her own more sensual version for her 1969 debut album "First Take," making it one of the first songs she recorded for Atlantic. "But Fleck had her own more sensual take on compared to what."
**You've Got A Friend (1971)** : A cover of Carole King's emerging standard, soon to be made famous by James Taylor, this one-off single was a duet with Donny Hathaway. It marked Roberta Flack's breakthrough on the pop charts, debuting in the Top 40 at #38 and peaking at #29 that summer. This song was widely covered in 1971, also appearing on King's "Tapestry" album and being covered by LaBelle. "This one off single finally broke Fleck on the pop charts."
**Where Is The Love (1972)** : This smoothly soulful single was a duet with Donny Hathaway from their collaborative album "Roberta Flack & Donny Hathaway." The song was a major hit, reaching #3 on the Pop chart and #1 on the R&B chart. "Fleck and Hathaway's smoothly soulful single from the album, Where Is The Love, Reach No. 3, Pop No. 1, R&B."
**Killing Me Softly With His Song (1973)** : The origin of this song came from Laurie Lieberman's impressions of a Don McLean performance, which her songwriters Norman Gimbel and Charles Fox turned into a song. Roberta Flack discovered Lieberman's version on a TWA flight in 1972 and felt compelled to record it due to its title and her classical background, believing she could add something unique. Her full title version, unlike The Fugees' "Just Killing Me Softly," was met with acclaim. Marvin Gaye, after hearing her perform it at his late 1972 concert, urged her to record it immediately, certain it would be a smash. Released officially in January 1973, it rapidly ascended the Hot 100, hitting #1 in just five weeks. It topped the chart in March 1973, the same week Flack accepted the Record of the Year Grammy for "The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face," and would go on to win Record of the Year itself in March 1974, making Flack the first artist to win the prize two years in a row. "By the time I got to New York, I knew I had to do that song, and with my classical background, I knew I'd be able to add something to it."

## curiosities
**Early Life & Education** : Roberta Cleopatra Flack was born and raised in Black Mountain, North Carolina, and was a classically trained pianist by her early teens. She received a full scholarship to Howard University at age 15, making her one of the youngest students ever to enroll, and graduated at 19 with a bachelor's degree in music education. "Born and raised in Black Mountain, North Carolina, Roberta Fleck was a classically trained pianist by her early teens."
**Career Transition to Performing** : After graduating from Howard, Flack initially became a music teacher in North Carolina before returning to Washington D.C. to teach junior high students. During this time, she also began playing in clubs around D.C., shifting her repertoire from classical to include more pop, jazz, blues, and gospel, drawing large crowds of D.C. music fans. "She graduated at just 19, became a music teacher back in North Carolina, and eventually returned to Washington to teach junior high students and play clubs around DC."
**Discovery by Les McCann** : While performing in D.C. clubs, Roberta Flack caught the attention of renowned jazz pianist and vocalist Less McCann, who was captivated by her voice. McCann subsequently arranged an audition for Flack with Atlantic Records, the label that had recently propelled Aretha Franklin to chart success. "It was while playing these clubs that Roberta Fleck attracted the attention of renowned jazz pianist and vocalist, Less McCann."
**Impact of Clint Eastwood's "Play Misty for Me"** : Clint Eastwood, making his directorial debut with the 1971 film "Play Misty for Me," personally selected Roberta Flack's two-year-old deep cut "The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face" from her "First Take" album to soundtrack a romantic montage. Eastwood paid Fleck $2,000 for the rights, a sum that proved immensely valuable for Fleck's career as the film's modest box office success launched her previously ignored song to #1 status. "Clint Eastwood was captivated and paid Fleck $2,000 for the rights to use her version of The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face in Play Misty for me."
**First Black Female Soloist with a #1 Album** : Roberta Flack achieved a significant historical milestone for the week ending April 29, 1972, when her debut album "First Take" reached #1 on the album chart. This made her the first Black female soloist to have a #1 album, an accomplishment that had eluded even established legends like Aretha Franklin and Diana Ross. "making Roberta Fleck amazingly the first black female soloist with a No. 1 album, an achievement that had alluded even Aritha Franklin and Diana Ross."
**Grammy Record of the Year Wins** : Roberta Flack made Grammy history by winning the Record of the Year award two years in a row. She first received the prize in March 1973 for her 1972 hit "The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face." The following year, in March 1974, she again accepted the Record of the Year Grammy, this time for her 1973 chart-topper "Killing Me Softly With His Song." "She became the first artist to win that prize two years in a row."
**Interpreting Folk for Soul** : Flack's breakthrough came from her ability to reimagine white folk songs, particularly "The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face" and "Killing Me Softly With His Song," within her unique classical piano and spiritual soul idiom. This approach expanded the possibilities for Black female artists in popular music, allowing her to compete successfully on the charts with major rock and pop acts of the 1970s. "The key to Fleck's breakthrough had been reimagining a white folk song in her own classical piano meets spiritual soul idiom."
**Rock & Roll Hall of Fame Status** : Roberta Flack remains unrecognized by the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, having not even been nominated for induction. "Two of them, Roberta Flack and Paddy Label haven't even been nominated."

## albums
**Just Being Myself (1973)** : This LP marked a period where Dionne Warwick, temporarily adding an "E" to her last name (Warwicke), came as close as she ever would to "rocking out" in an attempt to reboot her career following the split of her longtime mentors, Burt Bacharach and Hal David. "And, on her 1973 LP Just Being Myself, Warwick, W-A-R-W-I-C-K-E, came as close as she ever would to rocking out."

## songs
**Don't Make Me Over (1963)** : Dion Warwick's first hit, a number 21 Pop and number 5 R&B hit in January 1963. This song was one of the first specifically written for her by Bert Bacharach and Hal David, after Bacharach was impressed by her vocals on The Drifters' B-side "Mexican Divorce." "Don't Make Me Over. A number 21 pop, number 5 R&B hit in January 1963."
**Anyone Who Had A Heart (1963)** : This song became Dion Warwick's first Top 10 pop hit later in 1963. It was another composition penned by Burt Bacharach and Hal David, specifically tailored to Warwick's distinctive, self-assured voice. "And later in 1963, anyone who had a heart, Warwick's first top 10 pop hit."
**Walk On By (1964)** : A significant hit for Dion Warwick, reaching #6 Pop and #1 R&B in 1964. The song, built on a bossa nova beat and a gentle horn hook reminiscent of the Tijuana Brass, initially presented as lounge music. However, Warwick's vocal performance amplified the drama in the chorus, leading to a cathartic piano crescendo, transforming the song into "pure soul" that blended "girl group ache" with "grown folks regret." "Built out of a bossan Nova beat, and a gentle horn hook that sounds a lot like the Tijuana brass. Walk On By reads as lounge music, a drink that's stirred, not shaken."
**Reach Out For Me (1964)** : Originally a 1963 single for soul singer Lou Johnson, where it peaked at #74 on the Hot 100. Dion Warwick's cover, released a year later, added "just enough of a glide" to the Bacharach-David composition, propelling it to a Top 20 pop hit. It also showcased Warwick's "code switching skill" by simultaneously becoming a #1 R&B hit, countering any reputation for being solely a "pop" singer. "One year later, when Warwick took it on, she added just enough of a glide to reach out for me that it became a top 20 pop hit."
**A House Is Not A Home (1964)** : One of the Bacharach-David songs made famous by Dion Warwick in 1964. This track later gained further recognition when it was reinterpreted decades later by R&B luminaries such as Luther Vandross, which emphasized the inherent soulfulness Warwick had originally brought to the song. "And other Bacarack David songs she made famous, such as 1964's A House Is Not A Home. Were reinterpreted decades later by R&B luminaries like Luther Vandross."
**Alfie (1967)** : The title tune for the 1966 British film starring Michael Caine, written by Bert Bacharach and Hal David, and first sung by British singer Syla Black. Warwick's version became the biggest hit, outcharting Syla Black's original and a Cher cover (which reached #32 Hot 100). Dion Warwick's "Alfie" reached #15 in 1967 on the Hot 100 and #5 on the R&B chart, showcasing her ability to transform an improbable track into a success. "And yet, Dion Warwick's Alfie was the biggest hit version of all."
**I Say A Little Prayer (1967)** : Dion Warwick recorded the first version of this Bacharach and David classic, achieving #4 Pop and #8 R&B in late 1967. While Aretha Franklin's legendary version reached #3 R&B and #10 Pop in 1968, Warwick's version actually outcharted Franklin's on the pop chart. "Deon recorded the first version of Backerak and David's All-Time Classic. I say a little prayer. And she got it to number four pop, number eight, R&B, in late 1967."
**Theme from Valley of the Dolls (1968)** : The B-side to Warwick's "I Say A Little Prayer," this song, not penned by Bacharach or David but by Andre and Dory Previn from the movie drama "Valley of the Dolls," charted independently and became Warwick's biggest hit of the 1960s. Despite being an improbable choice for her, it was a smash, reaching #2 in February 1968. "It's B side, a song not penned by Bert Backerak or Hal David charted on its own and became Deon's biggest hit of the 1960s."
**Do You Know The Way To San Jose? (1968)** : Although it only reached #10 on the Hot 100, this Bacharach-David standard from the late 1960s is considered Dion Warwick's most remembered hit from that period. "Though it only reached number ten on the hot 100, her hit from this period that's probably best remembered is her take on the Backerak David standard. Do you know the way to San Jose?"
**This Girl's In Love With You (1969)** : A gender-flipped interpretation of Herb Alpert's 1968 #1 hit "This Guy's In Love With You." Warwick took her version to #7 on both the pop and R&B charts in 1969, demonstrating her skill in interpreting white artists' songs for a multi-racial audience. "Warwick flipped the gender and took this girl's in love with you to number seven on both the pop and R&B charts in 1969."
**We Are The World (1985)** : Dion Warwick contributed a solo vocal to the USA for Africa charity mega-single. Her performance is highlighted as the "all-around best solo vocal" in the introductory three minutes, characterized by her subtle vocal masterclass, finding harmonics and runs not in the original score, and delivering a heartfelt, empathetic, and soulful performance without over-singing. She also generously set up Willie Nelson's subsequent quirky vocal. "Dion Warwick finds the heart, and, as the lyrics says, sends it without hogging the spotlight."
**Then Came You (1974)** : A quintessential Tom Bell Philly Soul production, this song was a collaboration between Dion Warwick and lead Spinner Philippe Wynne. The track was designed to showcase their fiery vocals and became a #3 Pop and #1 R&B smash, marking a successful career reboot for Warwick with a producer closer to the cutting edge of 70s pop. "A quintessential Tom Bell Philly Soul production was that song. Then came you."

## curiosities
**Musical Upbringing and Influences** : Dion Warwick was born into the family that produced The Drinkard Singers, a gospel vocal troupe led by her aunt Sissy Houston (Whitney Houston's mother). While she grew up singing in church, she is not strictly a gospel, jazz, or soul singer, but rather "exophisticated." Ella Fitzgerald and Sarah Vaughn were clear influences. She emerged from the Brill Building environment of early 60s post-Elvis Presley, pre-Beatles urban pop. "Warwick was born into the same family that produced the drinker singers. The gospel vocal troupe, led by Whitney's mother and Dion's aunt, Sissy Houston."
**The Bacharach/David Muse** : After Bert Bacharach was impressed by her vocals on The Drifters' "Mexican Divorce" (on which she sang backup with The Drinkard Singers affiliates), he introduced her to lyricist Hal David. Within months, Bacharach and David began writing songs uniquely suited to Warwick's self-assured voice, including "Don't Make Me Over" and "Anyone Who Had A Heart," effectively making her their muse. "Within months, Bacarack and David began writing songs uniquely suited to Dion Warwick's self-assured voice, including Don't Make Me Over."
**Pop vs. R&B Crossover Skill** : Dion Warwick often faced the "knock" that she was more pop than R&B, partly due to her sophisticated interpretations. However, her "code switching skill" allowed her to achieve #1 R&B hits (like "Walk On By" and "Reach Out For Me") simultaneously with her pop success, and her soulful interpretations of Bacharach-David songs were later celebrated and reinterpreted by R&B artists like Luther Vandross. "However, this reputation for pop smoothness, the Lied Warwick's code switching skill, her reach out for me was also a number 1 R&B hit."
**Interpretation of White Artists' Songs** : More than any other Black female singer of the 1960s, Dion Warwick is credited with "decoding" the interpretation of white artists' songs for a multi-racial audience, as exemplified by her cover of Herb Alpert's "This Guy's In Love With You" as "This Girl's In Love With You." "So you could fairly argue that more than any black female singer of the 60s, Deon Warwick decoded the interpretation of white artists' songs for a multi-racial audience."
**Post-Bacharach/David Career Reboot** : After her long-time mentors Burt Bacharach and Hal David split in the early 1970s, Warwick sued them to resolve her contract, necessitating a career makeover. For a few years, she even added an 'E' to her last name (Warwicke). Her reboot was solidified by teaming with Philadelphia producer Thom Bell, a progenitor of Philly Soul, who produced her hit duet "Then Came You" with Philippe Wynne of The Spinners. "She kind of had to, when her longtime mentors, songwriting duo Bert Macarach and Hal David, split up just after the start of the 70s, Warwick had to sue them to resolve her contract with the super smooth pair."
**Rock & Roll Hall of Fame Status** : Dion Warwick has been nominated multiple times for the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame but remains uninducted. As of the time of the recording, she was again nominated, with the speaker indicating they would vote for her. "The other two, Shaka Khan and Dion Warwick have been nominated multiple times, but remain uninducted."

## songs
**I Sold My Heart to the Junk Man (1962)** : This 1962 remake of a 1940s doo-wop song was credited to The Blue Bells, Patti LaBelle's group. However, Patti LaBelle did not actually sing on this track; it was performed by a different group called The Starlets. The label owner arbitrarily slapped The Blue Bells' name onto the record. Despite this, it reached #15 in 1962, breaking The Blue Bells onto the charts. "This 1962 remake of the 40s duop Didi I sold my heart to the junk man was credited to the Blue Bells, Patty LeBelle's group. But it was really sung by a different group, The Starless."
**Down the Aisle (1963)** : By 1963, Patti LaBelle's actual voice was featured on The Blue Bells' hits, including this wedding song. "Down the Aisle" reached #37 on the Hot 100 and #14 on the R&B chart. "Fortunately, by 1963, the four woman Blue Bells were scoring hits like Down the Isle, the wedding song, with Patty's actual voice."
**You'll Never Walk Alone (1964)** : A cover by The Blue Bells of the Broadway standard, which had also been an anthem for Jerry and the Pacemakers. This interpretation became a Top 40 hit for The Blue Bells in 1964, demonstrating LaBelle's ability to make any song her own. "This included The Blue Bells cover of the Broadway standard, turned Jerry and the pacemakers anthem, You'll Never Walk Alone, a top 40 hit for The Blue Bells in 1964."
**I'm Still Waiting (1966)** : Patti LaBelle and The Blue Bells transformed this stately ballad, originally written by Curtis Mayfield for The Impressions, by amplifying its yearning and drama. Their version cracked the R&B Top 40 in 1966, showcasing their interpretive skill during the mid-60s. "The Blue Bells version of I'm Still Waiting, cracked the R&B top 40 in 1966."
**Take Me For A Little While (1967)** : This song was a #36 R&B hit for The Blue Bells in 1967. It proved to be their last hit of the 1960s, as founding member Cindy Birdsong was poached by Motown Records shortly after its release, leading to the group's regrouping and eventual renaming to LaBelle. "Take Me For A Little While wound up being the Blue Bells last hit of the 1960s, as the trio regrouped and eventually renamed themselves simply Lebel."
**Lady Marmalade (1974)** : A sexually liberated space funk jam by LaBelle, this song helped them command the charts in 1974. "Lady Marmalade" showcased the trio, led by Patti LaBelle, in glam rock makeup and cemented their image. "with their sexually liberated space funk jam, Lady Marmalade."

## curiosities
**Early Life and Choral Background** : Patricia Louise Holt, who would become Patti LaBelle, was born in Philadelphia. She developed her singing skills in her church choir, which she eventually formed her first singing group from at the age of 16. "For Patricia Louise Holt, born in Philadelphia, the weirdness of the music business started early. The future Patty LeBelle, who grew up singing in her church choir and formed her first singing group at just 16..."
**Uncredited Vocal on First Hit** : Patti LaBelle did not sing on her group The Blue Bells' first hit, the 1962 song "I Sold My Heart to the Junk Man." The track was actually sung by a different group, The Starlets, but The Blue Bells' name was affixed to the record by the label owner, illustrating common "shenanigans" in the early 60s music business with girl groups. "The future Patty LeBelle, who grew up singing in her church choir and formed her first singing group at just 16, didn't even sing on her group's first hit."
**Insane Vocal Range** : Patti LaBelle is known for her exceptional vocal abilities, described as an "insane vocal range" that she is not shy about showcasing. "Whereas Ms. Paddy Label is, well, not shy, about showing off her insane vocal rain."
**Rock & Roll Hall of Fame Status** : Patti LaBelle remains unrecognized by the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, having not even been nominated for induction. "Two of them, Roberta Flack and Paddy Label haven't even been nominated."

**Black Women Artists' Legacy (60s-80s)** : A wave of sophisticated Black women artists, including Roberta Flack, Dion Warwick, and Patti LaBelle, came of age in the 1960s, a decade when they fought to assert themselves in the studio and on the road. "In a song, many accused of being messianic and self-serving in the guise of being charitable, a chance for performers to chest-thump."
**Critiques of Artists Being Taken For Granted** : The women discussed, including Roberta Flack, Dion Warwick, and Patti LaBelle, are often "taken for granted" by critics and pop fans due to their consistent, reliable excellence over long careers. This overlooks the significant "craft" and artistry inherent in their work, especially for subtle vocalists like Dion Warwick. "The women we'll be discussing today are, in many ways, taken for granted. # curiosity

**Mid-70s Artistic Freedom for Black Women** : The mid-1970s represented a significant period where black women artists like Roberta Flack, Dionne Warwick, Labelle, and Chaka Khan experienced considerable artistic freedom and commercial influence on the music charts.
