## albums
**First Take (1969) - Debut Album and Sleeper Hit** : Recorded for Atlantic when Flack was 32 years old. Released in June 1969, it didn't crack Billboard's Top LPs chart until January 1970, and then only briefly. After "The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face" appeared in *Play Misty for Me*, the album re-entered the chart in March 1972 for the first time in two years and soared to No. 22 by April, higher than any previous Flack album. Two weeks later it was in the Top 10, and for the week ending April 29, 1972, it reached No. 1. That made Flack the first Black female soloist with a No. 1 album. "The prodigy had become a late bloomer."
**Chapter Two (1970) - Word-of-Mouth Second Album** : Issued later in 1970. It became a word-of-mouth hit, riding the lower rungs of the album chart for months even though it spawned no hit songs. Listeners appreciated Flack's unique takes on sultry soul, including a song she introduced as being about a "Reverend Dr. Lee." Donny Hathaway played piano and sang on the LP, and Flack recorded songs he wrote. "It became a word of mouth hit, riding the lower rungs of the album chart for months, even though Chapter 2 spawned no hit songs."
**Quiet Fire (1971) - Third Solo LP** : Released in late 1971 while Flack "kept plugging away" through the year. It included yet another Carole King cover, following her duet of "You've Got a Friend." "dropping a third solo LP in late 1971 called Quiet Fire that had yet another Carol King cover on it."
**Roberta Flack & Donny Hathaway (1972) - Full Duet Album** : Recorded during 1971 and released just as First Take was reaching No. 1. Within weeks it rose to No. 3, giving Donny Hathaway his first hit LP and giving Flack two simultaneous Top 10 LPs. Its single "Where Is the Love" reached No. 3 Pop and No. 1 R&B. "giving Donnie Hathaway his first hit LP and Fleck two simultaneous top 10 LPs."
**Feel Like Making Love (1974) - Self-Produced Album** : Flack produced the album herself after her previous producer, Joel Dorn, left for another label. It took her more than eight months to make. The album was still on the charts in 1975 and early 1976, reinforcing her status as a crossover type. "Flack wound up producing the record herself when her previous producer Joel Dorn left for another label."

## songs
**Compared to What (1969) - Early Atlantic Recording** : One of the first songs Flack recorded for Atlantic, included on First Take. Les McCann first recorded the jaunty protest song of social criticism for a 1966 studio album. It was about to become a crossover hit for him in a live version with saxophonist Eddie Harris. Flack offered her own, more sensual interpretation. "Fleck had her own more sensual take on compared to what."
**Reverend Lee (1970)** : A Chapter Two track. Flack introduced it as a song about a "very big, strong, black, sexy, southern Baptist minister" who thinks he has his program together until he meets a lady who proves otherwise. "Until he runs up against a lady who shows him that he ain't got it together."
**You've Got a Friend (1971) - Duet with Donny Hathaway** : Atlantic executive Jerry Wexler suggested Flack and Hathaway record together, and they cut this cover of Carole King's emerging standard as a kind of experiment. The one-off single finally broke Flack on the pop charts. It debuted in the Top 40 at No. 38, as counted down by Casey Kasem, and peaked at No. 29 that summer. That same year the song also appeared on King's Tapestry, went to No. 1 for James Taylor, and was covered by Labelle. "This one off single finally broke Fleck on the pop charts."
**The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face (1969/1972) - First No. 1** : Written in 1957 by British folk singer and political activist Ewan MacColl. It was covered by many artists, mostly folkies, including the Kingston Trio, the Chad Mitchell Trio, the Brothers Four, and Peter, Paul and Mary. Flack first heard it through gospel-folk duo Joe and Eddie's 1963 version. Most versions ran under three minutes, but she slowed it way down: her album cut runs over five minutes and is sung at a deliberately languorous pace, suffused with awe. Clint Eastwood paid her $2,000 to use it in *Play Misty for Me*. Atlantic then issued the three-year-old track as a single, trimmed to about four and a half minutes but with her glacial singing speed intact. It entered the Hot 100 in March 1972, hit No. 1 three weeks after cracking the Top 40, and stayed there for six weeks. In March 1973 it won the Grammy for Record of the Year. "The smoldering slowness was the song's selling point."
**Where Is the Love (1972) - Duet with Donny Hathaway** : The smoothly soulful single from the Roberta Flack & Donny Hathaway album. It reached No. 3 Pop and No. 1 R&B. "smoothly soulful single from the album, Where Is The Love, Reach No. 3, Pop No. 1, R&B."
**Killing Me Softly with His Song (1973) - Second No. 1 and Back-to-Back Grammys** : Lori Lieberman told her songwriting partners, Norman Gimbel and Charles Fox, how knocked out she was by a Don McLean performance at the Troubadour in Los Angeles. They turned her impressions into this song. Lieberman's version was not a hit, but Flack heard it on the audio channel of a cross-country TWA flight in 1972. She told Fred Bronson, author of *The Billboard Book of Number One Hits*: "By the time I got to New York, I knew I had to do that song, and with my classical background, I knew I'd be able to add something to it." When she performed it as a guest at a Marvin Gaye concert in late 1972, Gaye told her backstage not to perform it again until she had recorded it, so sure was he that it was a smash. Released in January 1973, it reached No. 1 in five weeks. For the week ending March 3, 1973, it was No. 1 on the Hot 100 and rising to No. 2 on the Soul Singles chart, the same week she accepted Record of the Year for "The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face." It won Record of the Year itself in March 1974. Its full title distinguishes it from the Fugees' version, known simply as "Killing Me Softly." "I thought it had an awfully good title."
**Feel Like Making Love (1974) - Third Straight No. 1** : The title track of her self-produced album, in her purest silky-soul mode, "as much a vibe as a song." It became her third straight No. 1 hit, topping the Hot 100 just weeks before Dionne Warwick's "Then Came You." Her two previous smashes came close but did not top Billboard's soul singles chart. This one was both a pop and R&B smash and held the soul chart for more than a month. "Feel like making love was Flack in her purest silky soul mode."

## curiosities
**Classical Prodigy from Black Mountain** : Roberta Cleopatra Flack was born and raised in Black Mountain, North Carolina, and was a classically trained pianist by her early teens. She won a full scholarship to Howard University at age 15, one of the youngest students ever to enroll at the historically Black college in DC, and graduated at just 19. "She won a full scholarship to Howard University at age 15, one of the youngest students ever to enroll at the historically Black College in DC."
**Schoolteacher and DC Club Singer** : After graduating she taught music in North Carolina, then returned to Washington to teach junior high and play clubs around DC, working nights as an accompanist to other acts. Following in the footsteps of Nina Simone, she shifted from classical to pop repertoire, began to sing, and drew packed houses with her blend of jazz, blues and gospel. "she began to draw packed houses filled with DC music fans drawn to her blend of jazz, blues, and even gospel."
**Discovered by Les McCann** : Jazz pianist and vocalist Les McCann saw her in the DC clubs and arranged an audition with Atlantic Records, which had recently taken Aretha Franklin to the top of the charts. McCann later wrote about her. "her voice touched, tapped, trapped, and kicked every emotion I've ever known. She alone had the voice."
**Clint Eastwood and Play Misty for Me** : Coming off *Dirty Harry* (1971), Clint Eastwood was making his directorial debut, *Play Misty for Me*, and needed a song for a long, languorous romantic montage between his character and actress Donna Mills. He happened to hear First Take, by then more than two years old, and keyed into her deep cut of "The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face." He paid $2,000 for it. When the film became a modest box-office hit, the song took off and Flack seemed like an overnight sensation. "paid Fleck $2,000 for the rights to use her version of The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face in Play Misty for me."
**First Black Female Soloist with a No. 1 Album** : When First Take reached No. 1 for the week ending April 29, 1972, Flack became the first Black female soloist with a No. 1 album. "an achievement that had alluded even Aritha Franklin and Diana Ross."
**First Artist to Win Record of the Year Twice in a Row** : She won Record of the Year in March 1973 for "The First Time Ever I Saw Your Face" and in March 1974 for "Killing Me Softly with His Song." On the night of the first win, she was also No. 1 on the Hot 100 with "Killing Me Softly." "She became the first artist to win that prize two years in a row."
**Middle-Tier Career Before 1972** : Going into 1972, Flack was a respected singer in the middle tier of pop, selling enough LPs to justify her Atlantic career but not a chart-topper. "this could have been the extent of Roberta Fleck's cultural profile."
**Competing with Rock Giants** : Flack expanded what was possible for Black female artists in popular music, which made it a good time for other '60s veterans to try crossing over in the '70s. "competing effectively on the charts with the likes of the Rolling Stones, Jatro Tull and Elton John."
**Not Nominated for the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame** : Flack has never been nominated for the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame. "Two of them, Roberta Flack and Paddy Label haven't even been nominated."

## albums
**Just Being Myself (1973) - Reboot Attempt** : Released after Burt Bacharach and Hal David split up, while she was spelling her name "Warwicke." On this LP she came as close as she ever would to rocking out. "came as close as she ever would to rocking out."

## songs
**Mexican Divorce (c. 1962) - Backing Vocals for the Drifters** : A Drifters B-side co-written by Brill Building songwriter Burt Bacharach. The backup vocals came from Drinkard Singers affiliates: Cissy Houston, her friend Doris Troy, and Cissy's nieces Dee Dee Warwick and Dionne Warwick, then 21. Bacharach was especially impressed with Dionne. "Bert Bacarack was especially impressed with Dion and introduced her to his regular songwriting partner, lyricist Hal David."
**Don't Make Me Over (1963) - First Hit** : Written by Bacharach and David for her voice. It reached No. 21 Pop and No. 5 R&B in January 1963. "Bacarack and David began writing songs uniquely suited to Dion Warwick's self-assured voice."
**Anyone Who Had a Heart (1963) - First Top 10** : Released later in 1963, this Bacharach-David song was her first Top 10 pop hit. "anyone who had a heart, Warwick's first top 10 pop hit."
**Walk On By (1964)** : Reached No. 6 Pop and No. 1 R&B. It is built on a bossa nova beat and a gentle horn hook similar to the Tijuana Brass, so it reads as lounge music. At the chorus Warwick ramps up the drama toward a cathartic piano crescendo and the song becomes pure soul. "Girl Group Ake crossed with grown folks regret."
**Reach Out for Me (1964)** : The Bacharach-David song started as a 1963 single for soul singer Lou Johnson, which peaked at only No. 74. A year later Warwick's version became a Top 20 pop hit and a No. 1 R&B hit, belying her reputation as more pop than R&B. "she added just enough of a glide to reach out for me that it became a top 20 pop hit."
**A House Is Not a Home (1964)** : A Bacharach-David song she made famous. Decades later it was reinterpreted by R&B luminaries such as Luther Vandross. "these songs were given their soulfulness in the first place by Dion Warwick."
**Alfie (1967) - Biggest Hit Version** : Bacharach and David wrote the title tune for the 1966 British film *Alfie*, starring Michael Caine. Cilla Black sang it first, but the writers wanted Warwick to have a go at it. Her version outcharted both Black's original and Cher's cover, which was recorded to promote the film's U.S. release and peaked at No. 32. Warwick's reached No. 15 Pop and No. 5 R&B in 1967. "Dion Warwick's Alfie was the biggest hit version of all."
**I Say a Little Prayer (1967) - Original Version** : Warwick recorded the first version of this Bacharach-David classic, reaching No. 4 Pop and No. 8 R&B in late 1967. Aretha Franklin's 1968 version did better on R&B (No. 3) but reached only No. 10 Pop. "Deon Warwick's prayer actually outcharted Franklin's on the pop chart."
**Theme from Valley of the Dolls (1968) - Biggest '60s Hit** : The B-side of "I Say a Little Prayer," written by André and Dory Previn for the pulpy movie drama *Valley of the Dolls*, not by Bacharach and David. It charted on its own and became her biggest hit of the 1960s. "Deon's theme from Valley of the Dolls reached number two in February 1968."
**Do You Know the Way to San Jose (1968)** : A Bacharach-David standard that reached only No. 10 on the Hot 100 but is probably her best-remembered hit of the period. "her hit from this period that's probably best remembered."
**This Girl's in Love with You (1969)** : After Herb Alpert scored a 1968 No. 1 with "This Guy's in Love with You," Warwick flipped the gender and took it to No. 7 on both the pop and R&B charts. "Warwick flipped the gender."
**Then Came You (1974) - Duet with The Spinners** : Thom Bell paired Warwick with Spinners lead Philippé Wynn after she invited the group to open her summer 1974 tour. A quintessential Bell Philly soul production, it took more than three months to climb the Hot 100, since Warwick hadn't been in the Top 10 in more than four years. It hit No. 1 Pop in October 1974 and No. 2 R&B. "It was the first number 1 pop hit for the spinners Warwick and producer Tom Bell."
**Once You Hit the Road (c. 1975-76)** : From a second album with Thom Bell, it put her back in the R&B Top 5. "was back in the R&B top five with once you hit the road."
**We Are the World (1985) - USA for Africa Solo** : Warwick sang a solo line in the introductory section of the charity single, followed immediately by Willie Nelson. Like most participants, she had not known the song before the January 1985 session. Months later she pulled off a similar feat on her own legendary charity mega-single. "at the end of Warwick's line, that's country legend Willie Nelson coming in."

## curiosities
**Hard to Define** : AllMusic critic William Ruhlmann wrote that she grew up in church but isn't a gospel singer. She cites Ella Fitzgerald and Sarah Vaughan as influences but isn't a jazz singer, and she isn't a soul singer in Aretha Franklin's sense or a standards singer like Lena Horne or Nancy Wilson. He wrote that she could only have emerged from the Brill Building environment of post-Elvis, pre-Beatles urban pop. "it is easier to define Dion Warwick by what she isn't rather than what she is."
**Drinkard Singers Family** : Warwick was born into the family behind the Drinkard Singers, the gospel troupe led by her aunt Cissy Houston, Whitney Houston's mother. "Warwick was born into the same family that produced the drinker singers."
**Bacharach and David's Muse** : The duo had written hits for Perry Como and the Shirelles, but Warwick got the tone of their love-lorn, contemplative, jazzy cocktail pop and gave it soul, turning it into a kind of urbane R&B. "In essence, Dion Warwick became Bacarack and David's muse."
**Bridging Audiences** : More than any Black female singer of the '60s, Warwick made white artists' songs work for a multiracial audience. "Deon Warwick decoded the interpretation of white artists' songs for a multi-racial audience."
**Suing Bacharach and David** : When the duo split up just after the start of the '70s, Warwick had to sue them to resolve her contract. "Warwick had to sue them to resolve her contract with the super smooth pair."
**The Added "E"** : For a few years she spelled her name W-A-R-W-I-C-K-E. "in what might be read as a proto-prince move, she even added an E to the end of her last name."
**Rock Hall Snub** : She has been nominated for the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame multiple times but not inducted. "Shaka Khan and Dion Warwick have been nominated multiple times, but remain uninducted."

## albums
**Over the Rainbow (1966) - Blue Belles LP** : A Top 20 hit on the R&B album chart. "turned the Judy Garland standard into credible R&B for the first time in that song's history."
**Moon Shadow (1972)** : Named after their cover of a Cat Stevens song. "Their 1972 album, Moon Shadow, was named after a cat Steven's song that the trio covered to memorable effect."
**Pressure Cookin' (1973) - Glam Reboot** : An acclaimed album that completed the group's reinvention as glam rockers. Its centerpiece was a mashup of Thunderclap Newman's "Something in the Air" with Gil Scott-Heron's "The Revolution Will Not Be Televised." "tricked out in spacesuits, feathers and giant collars like Ziggy Stardust, Gone Soul."
**Nightbirds (1974) - Peak Glam Soul LP** : An acclaimed album produced by Allen Toussaint, which included "Lady Marmalade." "the peak LP of their glam soul phase."

## songs
**I Sold My Heart to the Junkman (1962)** : A remake of a '40s doo-wop song, credited to the Blue Belles but sung by the Starlets. Despite that, it reached No. 15 and broke the Blue Belles on the charts. "The Blue Bells name was slapped onto the record by the owner of the label, Patty and her girls had just signed to."
**Down the Aisle (1963)** : A wedding song that reached No. 37 on the Hot 100 and No. 14 R&B. "by 1963, the four woman Blue Bells were scoring hits like Down the Isle, the wedding song, with Patty's actual voice."
**You'll Never Walk Alone (1964)** : A cover of the Broadway standard, which had become a Gerry and the Pacemakers anthem. It was a Top 40 hit for the group. "when LeBelle wrapped her voice around a song, any song, it became hers."
**I'm Still Waiting (1966)** : A Curtis Mayfield ballad written for the Impressions. The Blue Belles version cracked the R&B Top 40. "they amped up the yearning, the drama."
**Take Me for a Little While (1967)** : Reached No. 36 R&B. It was the group's last hit of the '60s, released right before Cindy Birdsong left. "Take Me For A Little While wound up being the Blue Bells last hit of the 1960s."
**Can I Speak to You Before You Go to Hollywood? (early 1970s)** : Written by Nona Hendryx as a jab at Birdsong's departure. "Nona Hendrix would eviscerate Birdsong's departure in a song she wrote for Lebel."
**You've Got a Friend (1971)** : A Labelle cover of the Carole King song, released the same year as the James Taylor and Flack-Hathaway versions. "even LeBelle, the recristened trio led by Patty LeBelle, recorded a cover that year of You've Got A Friend."
**Won't Get Fooled Again (early 1970s)** : The Who song was among the group's covers while they toured with the band. "Pete Townsend, band leader of The Who, was a fan of Lebel's take on his groups Won't Get Fools Again."
**Lady Marmalade (1974-75) - No. 1** : Written by Bob Gaudio of the Four Seasons and L.A. songwriter Kenny Nolan, who had barely visited New Orleans. They aimed to capture the sex workers of Bourbon Street. An earlier recording by the studio disco group the Eleventh Hour was unpromising. Allen Toussaint produced Labelle's version as a hybrid of Dr. John-style funk and Pointer Sisters-style soul. Its French chorus, meaning "do you want to sleep with me?", became a radio in-joke. After a three-month climb, it topped both the R&B and pop charts in February and March 1975. "After a decade and a half of striving, label had finally arrived."
**What Can I Do for You (1975)** : The follow-up to "Lady Marmalade." "returning to the R&B top 10 and skirting the pop top 40 with what can I do for you."

## curiosities
**Losing Cindy Birdsong to The Supremes** : In 1967, Motown poached founding member Cindy Birdsong to replace Florence Ballard in the Supremes. The remaining trio regrouped as Labelle. "The wound from Birdsong flying the coop never really healed."
**Touring with The Who** : Labelle toured with The Who in the early '70s. "Lebel also toured with British rockers The Who."

## albums
**Rufus (1973) - Debut** : On some tracks, multi-instrumentalist and de facto leader Ron Stockert sang lead, but Chaka Khan took over as frontwoman. "Rufus could easily have been an FM radio style album rock band."

## songs
**Maybe Your Baby (1973)** : A cover of the Stevie Wonder song, which marked the band's shift toward funk. "Khan assumed the mantle of front woman comfortably, leading the band like she was born to do it."
**Tell Me Something Good (1974)** : Stevie Wonder wrote this for himself but gave it to Rufus, impressed by their cover of "Maybe Your Baby." It reached No. 3 Pop and R&B. "Wonder even showed up in the studio to help guide Khan on how she should sing the song, playing off its signature talk box."
**You Got the Love (1974)** : The band's first R&B No. 1, billed as Rufus featuring Chaka Khan. "Khan displayed the long power of a patty libelle."
**Once You Get Started (1975)** : A Top 10 hit on both the pop and R&B charts. "Khan had the sass and poise of a Dion warwick."
**Sweet Thing (1976)** : Reached No. 1 R&B and No. 5 Pop. "Khan had the soulful smoothness of Roberta Flack."

## curiosities
**Roots in The American Breed** : Rufus grew out of the American Breed, which had a 1968 Top 5 hit with "Bend Me, Shape Me." "The band Rufus grew out of a biracial rock group called The American Breed."
**Billing Change** : "Tell Me Something Good" was the last major hit credited to Rufus alone; afterward Chaka Khan received top billing. "It was the last major hit credited only to Rufus."

# curiosity

**We Are the World (1985)** : Michael Jackson and Lionel Richie co-wrote the song. Jackson sang the first chorus with Diana Ross, Steve Perry and Daryl Hall took the second, and Cyndi Lauper delivered an ad-lib before the full chorus. "everybody except Michael Jackson and Lionel Richie were unfamiliar with before showing up to record that night in January 1985."
**'70s Wave of Black Women Artists** : By 1974, Flack, Warwick and Labelle were all charting. These artists came of age in the 1960s and used that experience to gain more control in the 1970s. "Paying their dues in the 60s gave these women clout in the 70s to call more of the shots."
**Legacy for Later Generations** : These artists paved the way for later stars such as Lauryn Hill and Whitney Houston, and for the later cover of "Lady Marmalade." "an earlier generation of R&B queens found themselves in the eye of a quiet storm. And they made their own weather."
**You've Got a Friend Everywhere in 1971** : In 1971 the song appeared on Carole King's Tapestry, became a No. 1 hit for James Taylor, broke through for Roberta Flack and Donny Hathaway, and was covered by Labelle. "You've Got A Friend really got around in 1971."
**Disco Curveball** : In the late 1970s, dance music took over the charts and forced each of these singers to change course. "all four of these ladies took different paths to try to stay ahead of the spinning disco ball."
