## songs
**Good Times (1979)** : Sheik's most immortal hit, which topped the Hot 100 in August 1979. Nile Rogers conceived it as a variation on Kool & The Gang's "Hollywood Swinging." It features Rogers' distinctive guitar line and Bernard Edwards' iconic percolating walking bass line, which Edwards reportedly created on the spot. The lyrics, including "Happy Days are here again," were written by Rogers and Edwards to be wistful and ironic, reflecting the 1979 economic downturn by fusing 1920s nostalgia (inspired by Al Jolson) with 1970s disco, incorporating elements of George Clinton and Cole Porter, and featuring synchronized vocals from Alpha Anderson, Lucy Martin, and Fonsi Thornton. "by August 1979, good times, the lead single from Sheik's acclaimed third album, Riscay, had topped the Hot 100."
**My Forbidden Lover (Unknown Year, post-1979)** : This single was Sheik's follow-up to "Good Times," featuring the same triple-team vocals by Alpha, Lucy, and Fonsey. It stalled at number 43, marking a lowly peak and signaling the end of Sheik's two-year "imperial phase." "Their next single, the Sterling, My Forbidden Lover, with the same triple team vocals by Alpha, Lucy and Fonsey. Stalled at number 43, a lowly peak for the follow-up to a number one hit."
**Rebels Are We (Unknown Year, post-1980)** : The lead single from Sheik's fourth studio album, "Real People," it reached number 61 on the Hot 100 but found more success on the R&B chart, peaking in the Top 10. "Its lead single The Lucy Martin Showcase Rebels Are We Topped Out at a lowly number 61 on the Hot 100. Although it did make the R&B Top 10."
**Give Me the Loven (circa 1983)** : A track from Sheik's later period, representing an attempt to modernize their sound. It failed to enter the Hot 100 and only charted in the lower half of the R&B chart, illustrating the band's ongoing commercial slump. "Even as they tried to modernize the Sheik sound, tracks like Give Me the Loven would miss the Hot 100 entirely and peter out in the lower half of the R&B chart."

## curiosities
**Formation and Musical Style** : Sheik was conceived by guitarist Nile Rogers and bassist Bernard Edwards as a collective of top-notch players, aiming to offer sophisticated, jazzy, and instrumentally complex dance music, with lyrics often imploring listeners to dance. "Sheik, the Swab, Disco, Ira Band conceived by guitarist Nile Rogers, and bassist Bernard Edwards as a collective of top-notch players, offering sophisticated, jazzy, and instrumentally complex dance music."
**Musical Template for the 1980s** : By 1979, Sheik was setting a musical template for the upcoming decade, contributing to the birth of hip hop and shaping the sound of pop music in the 1980s. "They were going to set a musical template for the 80s, helping to spawn both a new genre, hip hop, and the sound of pop."
**"Good Times" Bass Line Influence** : The iconic bass line from "Good Times" traveled widely, being famously "borrowed," "bitten," or "stolen" by numerous artists including the Sugar Hill Gang ("Rapper's Delight"), Queen ("Another One Bites the Dust"), Blondie ("Rapture"), Grandmaster Flash ("The Adventures of Grandmaster Flash on the Wheels of Steel"), Father MC, and Will Smith. "The Good Times bass line traveled everywhere, most famously or infamously, depending on your point of view, on this historic single."
**Meeting Queen (1979)** : During 1979, while recording their respective albums ("Risqué" and "The Game"), Sheik members met John Deacon and other members of Queen. Bernard Edwards later commented to "New Musical Express" about Deacon spending time in their studio, alluding to the influence on "Another One Bites the Dust." "In 1979, while recording their respective albums, Riscay and The Game, Sheik met John Deacon and the members of Queen."
**Impact of Disco Sucks Movement** : The "disco sucks" movement significantly impacted Sheik's career. "Good Times" was ejected from the number one spot by The Knack's "My Sharona" in August 1979, which Nile Rogers believed was a direct cultural challenge from rock fans. After this, Sheik never returned to number one or the Billboard Top 40, effectively ending their two-year "imperial phase." "For Sheik in particular, it was awkwardly timed."
**Post-Imperial Phase Slump (Early 1980s)** : Following the end of their chart dominance around 1979, Sheik entered a prolonged period of commercial struggle. Their albums and singles released as late as 1983, such as "Give Me the Loven," performed poorly, contributing to professional strain between Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards. "Sheik's two-year imperial phase was over."
**Strained Relationship Between Founders** : As Sheik's chart fortunes declined in the 1980s, the relationship between Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards became more "chilly and business-like," even when Edwards was brought in to play bass on Rogers' external production projects. "This strained the relationship between Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards."

## curiosities
**Inspiration for "Good Times"** : Nile Rogers claimed that his guitar line for Sheik's "Good Times" was inspired by his favorite Kool & The Gang song, "Hollywood Swinging," as he tried to create a variation on its relentless groove. "Nile Rogers claims he was trying to come up with a variation on his favorite, Cool In The Gang song."
**"Good Times" Lyrics Co-Writer** : Co-wrote the "joyous, wistful, and knowingly ironic" lyrics for "Good Times" with Bernard Edwards, drawing inspiration from Al Jolson's "Happy Days Are Here Again" to comment on the 1979 economic downturn. "Rogers and Edwards paired this sturdy melodic backdrop with joyous lyrics that were wistful and knowingly ironic."
**"Rapper's Delight" Royalties and Live Renditions** : Initially, he and Bernard Edwards were not credited or compensated for the "Good Times" bass line's use in "Rapper's Delight," which Rogers considered "musical larceny." They later received writing credits and royalties, and Rogers continues to integrate rhymes from "Rapper's Delight" into his live performances of "Good Times" to this day. "Within months, Good Times co-writers Bernard Edwards and Nile Rogers were given writing credits, and eventually royalties on the song."
**Blaming the Disco Sucks Campaign** : To this day, Nile Rogers attributes the end of Sheik's "imperial phase" and their subsequent chart decline directly to the "disco sucks" movement. "They were bitter at the disco sucks campaign. To this day, Nile blames it for the change in their chart fortunes."
**Post-Sheik Production Challenges (Early 1980s)** : Following Sheik's decline, Rogers, alongside Edwards, experienced a "long, fallow period" where their production work on projects for artists like Johnny Mathis and Debbie Harry, as well as the "Soup for One" soundtrack, largely failed to achieve significant chart success. "For all its success, the Diana album only briefly postponed the Sheik production team's time in the wilderness."
**Feeling of Failure (circa 1983)** : In his autobiography, Rogers recounted feeling like a failure around 1983, as his projects since the Diana album had mostly flopped, leading to heavy indulgence in cocaine and alcohol. "In his autobiography, Rogers said he felt like a failure, circa 1983."
**Meeting David Bowie (1982)** : Met David Bowie at a New York club in 1982, initiating a collaboration that would rejuvenate Rogers' career. Bowie recognized Rogers' immense talent and restless spirit. "David Bowie and Nile Rogers met at a New York club in 1982 and quickly took a shine to each other."
**"Let's Dance" Production in Montreux (1982)** : Bowie invited Rogers to Montreux, Switzerland, to write and record, specifically requesting that Rogers help him create the biggest hits of his career, departing from Bowie's previous avant-garde work. "He invited Nile to write and record with him in Montreux, Switzerland."
**Arranging David Bowie's "Let's Dance" (1983)** : When Bowie presented "Let's Dance" as a folk-like song, Rogers requested to do an arrangement, transforming it by altering his guitar part and inverting the melody up an octave, infusing it with a funky sound. This collaborative success helped restore Rogers' confidence. "Nile helped David shape his song into a hit. And David gave Nile back his confidence."
**Emergence as Leading New Wave Rock Producer (1983-1984)** : His work on David Bowie's "Let's Dance" album, which was both commercially successful and danceable, positioned him as a leading producer of danceable new wave rock music. By late 1983 and 1984, he became pop's most in-demand producer. "It also represented a new permutation of Nile Rogers, moving him out from behind his chic success and making him the leading producer of a novel brand of danceable new wave rock music."
**Producing INXS's "Original Sin" (1983)** : Produced "Original Sin" for INXS's "The Swing" album, contributing his signature riffy guitars. He encouraged lyrics about an interracial couple ("Dream on White Boy, Dream on Black Girl"), inspired by his own multi-racial upbringing, and brought in Daryl Hall for harmonies. The song was a significant club hit, paving the way for INXS's American breakthrough. "Original Sin was a cutting-edge dance rock single featuring Nile's signature riffy guitars."
**Remixing Daryl Hall & John Oates' "Adult Education" (1984)** : Produced a remix of the early 1984 single "Adult Education," enhancing the track with hand-clapping rhythms and tribal chants to make it more infectious. "Nile was producing a remix of Darryl Hall and John Oats' early 1984 single Adult Education."
**Remixing Duran Duran's "The Reflex" (1984)** : Hired to "rethink" Duran Duran's "The Reflex" from an album cut into a radio-friendly hit. Rogers' remix rebuilt the song to sound dynamic enough for clubs and arenas, leading to Duran Duran's first US number one hit in June 1984, despite initial label concerns that it sounded "too black." "Nile's remix kept the best elements of the track, but he rebuilt The Reflex to sound like it could be playing in a Pat Night Club, or, as in the song's music video, A Pat Arena."
**Producing Duran Duran's "The Wild Boys" (1984)** : Immediately after the success of "The Reflex" remix, Rogers was commissioned to produce "The Wild Boys," a new studio track for Duran Duran's live album "Arena," incorporating tribal drums and "shining sounds" reminiscent of Sheik's style. "Then, almost immediately, the band gave Rogers another assignment, producing a brand new track...They called it The Wild Boys."
**Meeting Madonna (Prior to 1984)** : Met Madonna prior to 1984 after a live club appearance. He was impressed by her drive, though he initially underestimated her potential beyond dance clubs. "whom, like David Bowie, he'd first met at a New York nightclub, but unlike Bowie, at that time, she was largely unknown."
**Producing Madonna's "Like a Virgin" Album (1984)** : Produced Madonna's second album, "Like a Virgin," negotiating a financially advantageous deal he later described as his most lucrative LP. He brought in Bernard Edwards and Tony Thompson to play live instruments, believing it would "bring the songs to life," and coached Madonna's vocals for songs outside her natural range, such as "Like a Virgin" and "Material Girl." "Nile brought in both of his instrumental peers from Sheik, bassist Bernard Edwards, and drummer Tony Thompson to play on the Like a Virgin album."
**Strategic Advice to Sire Records (1984)** : Advised Sire Records to delay the release of Madonna's "Like a Virgin" album to continue promoting singles from her debut, which was unexpectedly performing well. This strategy proved successful, setting up the second album perfectly. "Rogers advised them to hold the new album that he just produced and keep promoting singles from the first one."
**First Number 1 Album as Producer (1985)** : "Like a Virgin" became Rogers' first number one LP as a producer, a milestone that neither his Sheik, Diana Ross, nor David Bowie albums had achieved. "Remarkably, the Like a Virgin album was the first number 1 LP of his career."
**Multiple Number 1 Hits as Producer** : By 1985, he had accumulated five Hot 100 number one hits as a producer (Sheik's two chart-toppers, Diana Ross's "Upside Down," David Bowie's "Let's Dance," and Madonna's "Like a Virgin"), or six if his remix of Duran Duran's "The Reflex" is included, with most of these successes occurring after the disco sucks movement. "And the single was his fifth Hot 100 number 1 as a producer following his two chic chart toppers, Diana Ross's Upside Down, and David Bowie's Let's Dance."
**Strained Partnership with Bernard Edwards (1980s)** : As Sheik's commercial prospects waned, Rogers fully shifted his focus to production, leading to a "chilly and business-like" professional relationship with Bernard Edwards, despite Edwards' contributions to Rogers' produced albums for Bowie and Madonna. "This strained the relationship between Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards. Nile brought in his friend to play bass on both the David Bowie and Madonna albums, but their interactions were more chilly and business like."
**Producing Mick Jagger's "She's the Boss" (1985)** : Produced Mick Jagger's first solo album, "She's the Boss," released in 1985. "As varied as Mick Jagger on his first solo album 1985's She's the Boss."
**Producing Peter Gabriel for "The Gremlins" Soundtrack (circa 1984)** : Produced a song for Peter Gabriel that was featured on "The Gremlins" soundtrack. "Peter Gabriel, with a song that was on No Kidding, The Gremlins soundtrack."
**Saving The Thompson Twins Project (1985)** : Hired by Arista executive Clive Davis to salvage The Thompson Twins' floundering "Here's to Future Days" album after its lead single failed to chart in the US in late 1984. Rogers remixed a hit, turning the album into a success. "Arista Executive, Clive Davis hired Nile Rogers to save The Thompson Twins project."
**Producing Sister Sledge's "Frankie" (1985)** : Produced a new album for Sister Sledge in 1985, capitalizing on the UK reissue success of their 1979 single "Thinking of You." This album produced Sister Sledge's first and only UK number one hit, "Frankie." "Sister Sledge went back into the studio with Nile Rogers in 1985 and produced a new album, which spawned Sister Sledge's first and only UK number one hit, Frankie."

"Bernard Edwards co-produced the theme with longtime James Bond score composer John Barry."
**Chart Competition with The Power Station (1985)** : In the summer of 1985, hits produced by Bernard Edwards for both Duran Duran ("A View to a Kill") and The Power Station ("Get It On (Bang a Gong)" and Robert Palmer's "Addicted to Love") were simultaneously competing on the charts, showcasing his significant independent production success. "At one point in the summer of 1985, hits by both Durandaran and the power station were going head-to-head on the charts, and both of them were produced by Bernard Edwards."

## songs
**Rapper's Delight (Unknown Year, post-1979)** : Considered one of the first recorded rap singles and the first to crack the Billboard Hot 100, where it peaked at number 36. It is notorious for "borrowing," "biting," or "stealing" the bass line from Sheik's "Good Times." The bass line was not sampled but replayed by a studio bassist (Bernard Roland or Chip Sheeran) at the direction of Sugar Hill Records mogul Sylvia Robinson. The song established rap as a recorded media and coined the term "hip hop." "the seminal Rapper's Delight by the Sugar Hill gang...one of the first recorded rap singles, and the first period to crack the Billboard Hot 100 where it peaked at number 36."

## curiosities
**"Good Times" Bass Line Recreation** : At the insistence of Sugar Hill Records mogul Sylvia Robinson, the "Good Times" bass hook was meticulously replayed by a studio bassist (either Bernard Roland or Chip Sheeran) to provide a sturdy backdrop for the MCs' rhymes, indicating a deliberate effort to recreate the original. This act later led to Bernard Edwards and Nile Rogers receiving writing credits and royalties. "At the direction of Sugar Hill Records mogul Sylvia Robinson, who needed a sturdy backdrop for the rhymes of MC's Wonder Mike Wright, Big Bang Hank Jackson, and Master G. O'Brien, the Good Times bass hook was replayed by a studio bassist."
**Coining the Term "Hip Hop"** : "Rapper's Delight" is credited with both establishing rap as a recorded media and coining the very term "hip hop," effectively making the "Good Times" hook even more immortal. "Rapper's Delight established rap as a recorded media, coined the very term hip hop, and, you might say, made the Good Times hook even more immortal than it already was."

**Diana (1980)** : An album produced by Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards. Ross initially rejected their "cavernous, busy" mixes, opting to re-record her vocals and restructure the entire mix with Motown engineer Russ Terrana, over the producers' objections, to achieve a more direct and prominent vocal presence. The revised version was deemed superior and radio-friendly, and the album reached number two on the Billboard album chart. "The recording sessions for the album that would be dubbed simply Diana."

## curiosities
**Inconsistent Chart Success (1970s)** : Despite being a prominent star in the 1970s, Diana Ross had a "hit or miss chart career," with her songs either topping the charts or completely missing the Top 40. "For such a luminary, she had a surprisingly hit or miss chart career."
**Motown's Comeback Initiative (1979-1980)** : By 1979, Ross had not had a major hit in three years. Motown sought to solidify her comeback by engaging Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards, then considered the "hottest duo in dance music," to produce her album. "By 1979, Diana Ross hadn't had a major hit in about three years."
**Asserting Creative Control on "Diana" (1980)** : Dissatisfied with Nile Rogers and Bernard Edwards' initial mixes for the "Diana" album, Ross, in "true diva fashion," insisted on re-recording her vocals and completely restructuring the mix with Motown engineer Russ Terrana, overriding the producers' objections. Her conviction proved correct, resulting in a more direct, uncluttered, and radio-ready sound. "So, over Edwards and Rogers objections, Ross went back into the studio with Motown Engineer Russ Tarana, and she both re-recorded her vocals and restructured the entire mix."

"A category the hall organizers can use by Fiat without a full membership vote."
